Chapter 18Nobody Is Going to Deliver Your Purpose

The note under Tess’s door says, in a beautiful copperplate hand, Is the animal all right?

He knocked first. She stopped in the middle of a scale and stood there holding her breath, a grown woman hiding in her own apartment with the end of a clarinet stuck in a basket of clean laundry. The towels were supposed to spare the neighbors the first stages of her musical education. They’d made it sound like something dying under a porch. When the knocking stopped, the note came. It’s signed by the man downstairs. He’s included his phone number in case she needs a ride to the vet.

Tess played in high school. At fourteen she could get from the bottom of the instrument to the top without anything sounding as though it had sustained a head injury. At fifteen she started seeing a boy who smoked and could get them both into R-rated movies, and the clarinet went under the bed. It survived two moves and the death of her mother. The boy is now a bald man whose main activity online is taking a hard line on parking. Of the two, the clarinet had the better prospects for revival.

She’s had the pads checked and bought new reeds. She’s found the folding stand, which goes up like a beach chair and has about the same ambitions regarding your fingers. What the music store can’t sell her is twenty minutes of being audibly awful with nobody else in the building. People talk about the courage it takes to begin. Sometimes it takes a room with a goddamn door.

Fran, who used to fill her mother’s prescriptions and now runs the film night at the center, knows a room. The community center has a little committee room upstairs with an old upright piano, thick walls and one window over the trash cans. The center also has a problem, the city’s notice that it closes at five from January 1. So a clipboard sits by the front door, and every name signed after five goes downtown to help the recreation department decide whether evenings are worth the electricity and the custodian. The custodian is Bev. Her evening hours come to twelve a week, and they go when the lights do.

So Fran’s offer has a hook in it. Six Tuesdays, which is what’s left of the year. Four half-hour slots a night in the little room. Tess gets her door, and every person she brings through it after five is a head on the department’s sheet. The man downstairs is warmly in favor.

She says yes, and she notices what just happened. She wanted somewhere to be bad at the clarinet. One phone call later it’s a campaign with a deadline and another woman’s paycheck riding on it. Tess does payroll. She knows to the dollar what twelve hours a week looks like when it comes off a check. Twelve times fifty-two is six hundred and twenty-four hours a year, and she has watched smaller numbers than that decide who keeps the car.

Then Fran asks for a hundred words for the center’s website. Name, photograph, a bit about your passion. The department reads the website, Fran says. Under the empty box, in pale gray, is a helpful suggestion. Tell us what you were put here to do.

On the morning she opens this email, Tess has just spent forty minutes on her knees persuading the plastic thing under the sink to stop dripping. If she was put here to do that, somebody higher up could have supplied a washer.

She could write that she wants to play the clarinet. It’s a small answer for a large box. Other people will have a grandmother who played, or a belief that music brings communities together. Tess’s belief so far is that she wants half an hour on Tuesday and the right to stop in the middle of a scale because she doesn’t like how her tongue feels. The website will presumably want a more inspiring tongue.

What she wishes, reading that question, is that somebody would tell her. She’d take a boring answer. Repair zippers. Teach boys to read. Get the price of hearing-aid batteries down. She had an answer once, and it was mostly disgusting. From her mid-thirties until she was forty, she and her sister Ellen looked after their mother, and fought about whose turn it was. Tess’s share was a lot of the wiping and most of the waiting. Waiting rooms, hold music, the pharmacy line, the hour between the pill and the pill working. When the fridge had milk in it and the prescription was picked up and a woman with an ulcer on her heel was asleep on clean sheets, you knew where you stood in relation to the day. Sometimes you stood outside the pharmacy at six minutes past closing, mouthing imaginative words at a man who had every right to go home. It was a hard way of knowing what to do. It did tell you.

She wanted out of it the whole time. She’ll say so if you ask. Some evenings she watched the clock on the hospital wall and wanted it over. Then it was over, and the wanting turned out to have been holding up a surprising amount of the roof.

After her mother died, everybody told Tess she could finally do things for herself. They said it with feeling, as if a fascinating woman named Herself had been patiently developing interests in the guest room. Three years later they still hand it to her, like a coupon for a store nobody can give her directions to. It turned out Herself could watch three consecutive shows about couples buying vacation homes and was willing to do it again the next night. Tess could account for the missing hours of her mother’s week to the minute. She had much less idea what to do with the hours that came back. Nobody has needed to know where she is at six o’clock since the funeral. That’s the freedom.

What would you like the answer to answer?

One universal meaning has a serious appeal. If a correct answer exists, you might waste some years on the wrong one, but you could find out, change course and stop renegotiating the whole arrangement in the shower. A dying woman might get to know whether she’d done enough. I can think of very few people who’d turn down a reliable answer to that one, and most of them run weekend workshops.

A shared tradition supplies a lot of this. You know whose door to go to after a death, what to carry and what to say when it opens. Arranging a society so that a wrecked household gets fed on Thursday is no small thing. Tess got four casseroles. She also wonders about the woman who brings the casserole every time, including the weeks she could use one herself.

Now take a man whose purpose was delivered. In December 1944 a twenty-two-year-old Japanese intelligence officer, Second Lieutenant Hiroo Onoda, was sent to Lubang, a small island in the Philippines. His orders, as he told it afterward, were to hold out and fight on, never to surrender and never to take his own life. The army would come back for him. Eight months later the war ended. Leaflets fell on the jungle saying so. Onoda and the three men with him studied the leaflets, found mistakes in them and decided they were an enemy trick. One of the four walked out in 1950. In 1952 planes dropped letters and photographs from their families. Another trick. One was shot dead in 1954. The third, Kinshichi Kozuka, was killed by police in October 1972 while the two of them were burning rice the island’s farmers had harvested, twenty-seven years into the peace. Onoda went on alone.[1]

In February 1974 a young Japanese traveler named Norio Suzuki went looking for him. The story usually told is that Suzuki had said he was off to find Lieutenant Onoda, a panda and the Abominable Snowman, in that order. He found the lieutenant in about four days. Onoda talked to him and still wouldn’t come out. He was waiting for orders from his superior officer. So Japan went and found his superior officer. Major Yoshimi Taniguchi was sixty-three and selling books on Kyushu. In March he came to Lubang and read out an order to stop fighting. Onoda, who was fifty-one, stood at attention in the jungle for a bookseller, and bowed, and that was the end of the war.[2]

Look at what he’d had. The psychologists Frank Martela and Michael Steger split the meaning people want from life into three parts. A life that makes sense to you, aims that give you a direction, and a feeling that your being alive counts for something.[3] Onoda had all three for twenty-nine years, at a strength most of us will never know. Every morning made sense. He knew what to do next. He was sure it mattered. His purpose even came with a procedure for handling news that it had expired. And over those years about thirty of the island’s inhabitants were killed in clashes with soldiers of a war that had ended.[1]

Onoda was a trained soldier given real orders in a real war, and most of what we know about his reasoning comes from the memoir he published afterward, which is a man’s account of himself.[4] Nothing here shows that a purpose somebody hands you has to end in graves. The Thursday casserole is handed down too. Lubang shows something narrower. A delivered purpose can give a man the sense and the direction and the mattering, all of it, and be wrong about the world. From the inside it feels the same either way. The people placed to notice the difference were the islanders, and the orders hadn’t mentioned them.

The philosopher Susan Wolf builds that into her account of meaning. For Wolf it takes two things together. A person has to care about what she’s doing, and the thing has to have some worth beyond the bare fact that she cares.[5] Wolf doesn’t claim to have settled what gives a thing that worth. Her second condition keeps the question open to people other than the devotee. Onoda can’t close it by reporting how much the mission meant to him. The neighbors get a say.

He went home a hero to a great many people. Nobody issued the next order. The following year he moved to Brazil and raised cattle.[1]

Tess had two of the three. Her mother’s need gave her a direction and a sense of counting for something, even when almost nothing that was happening made sense. If you’d tried to explain the larger plan for an infected heel, she’d have put you to work changing the dressing. Then the need stopped. Nobody flew in to read her an order. People dropped leaflets. You can do things for yourself now. She believed them, which puts her one up on the lieutenant, and it hasn’t helped, because the leaflets never say what the things are. Nobody needs her to play the clarinet. She’d like to. Where does that go on the form?

A head is a head

The first person to call about a slot plays the trombone. His name is Leon and he sounds wonderful on the phone. He gives Tess his preferred half hour and his heartfelt guarantee that he’ll stop on the dot of seven, even in the middle of something. Men have promised her undying love and sounded less sure. She puts him down for six-thirty and herself for six.

A woman wants a place for her son, Kit, who’s eleven and takes piano at school. The piano at his dad’s has headphones. His mom’s apartment has nothing to play. Then a man calls who wants the room to practice the speech he’s giving at his daughter’s wedding. He keeps losing his voice at the part about his wife. No instrument is involved, and he wonders if that disqualifies him.

Having to rule on this bothers Tess more than it should. Had somebody already written down what the room was for, she could be fair by looking it up. Music program, instruments only. Community space, speeches welcome. Deciding it herself means the man can think she decided badly and tell her so to her face. A law from on high would give her somewhere to forward complaints, which is a good half of the attraction of being a small part of a large design. The sentence “I’m afraid the system won’t let me” has brought deep spiritual comfort to a great many people who profess no religion at all.

The clipboard, as it happens, has no opinion. Bev has checked. The department counts names after five and doesn’t ask what the names were doing. A father crying over an index card is worth exactly one trombonist. Tess gives him seven o’clock.

That evening she tries to write the hundred words. After caring for her late mother, Tess found healing through music. She takes out healing. Then she takes out late mother. A woman who once drove a wasp out of the kitchen with a frozen fish stick deserves a richer afterlife than becoming the first clause in a promotional email. The sentence is also premature. So far Tess has produced four doubtful notes and frightened the man downstairs.

The people you can hear through a wall

On the first Tuesday, Bev shows her which light switch belongs to the little room. She’s set out a chair and a piano bench. Coffee is downstairs by the urn, with the enamel mugs and the spoons. The front door sticks unless you lift it a little by the handle, and so does this one. Bev demonstrates the amount of shoulder involved. Then she walks Tess back down to the clipboard. Name, time in, time out, purpose of visit. Print, she says. They don’t count what they can’t read.

“Does it matter what I put for purpose?”

“Nobody’s ever asked me.” Bev squares the sheet under the clip. “Twelve hours a week, though. That’s my car payment.”

Tess prints her name. The purpose column is two inches wide. She leaves it blank and goes upstairs.

She has the first thirty minutes. It takes her six to put the clarinet together, wet the reed and get the stand open without losing a finger. Then she sits there. The room smells of paste and dust. Long ago somebody stuck a gold star under the edge of the piano lid, where it could only ever be awarded to a person lying on the floor. From the big room next door come a shuffle of small feet and a teacher insisting on something called soft knees.

She plays. The first sound is good enough to give her a shock. For a second she’s fourteen and can still do it. On the second note a finger goes down that should have stayed up, and the noise that comes out would have confirmed the man downstairs in his gravest suspicions. The physics isn’t on her side. A clarinet is close to a tube closed at one end by the reed, so it favors the odd harmonics. Overblow a flute or a saxophone and it jumps an octave. Overblow a clarinet and it jumps a twelfth, to three times the frequency, nineteen semitones up.[6] One careless thumb and a lullaby becomes a smoke alarm.

Nothing happens. The teacher goes on asking for soft knees. Nobody reassures her. Nobody tells her not to give up, or offers the number of an excellent private teacher. She tries again. It dawns on her how much of playing at home consisted of imagining somebody listening to her play at home. Take that person out of the room and the room gets a lot bigger.

At six-thirty she goes out to tell Leon it’s his turn. He’s on a chair in the hallway with the trombone between his knees. Did he hear much through the door? “Some,” he says. She realizes she’d been hoping for either nothing at all or enough to be very impressed. He asks where to put his coat.

Tess goes down to the kitchen and pours herself a coffee. The first note from upstairs pushes slowly through the ceiling, huge and gently wavering. It sounds like a very big ship asking a much smaller ship out for a drink.

Tess hasn’t found the thing she’ll be best at. Nobody ran down a hallway shouting for an agent. She did something for half an hour and came to care a little how it went.

That’s my trouble with the advice to find your passion. It asks a person at the trailhead to report on the view from halfway up. Tess knew she liked the feel of the instrument. She couldn’t have known what it’s like to adjust her breath over a run of three notes until the middle one finally belongs with the other two, because that piece of experience isn’t available until you’ve done it badly for a while. It may become the reason she comes back. Asking for that reason up front is like demanding references from your unborn children.

None of this means anybody can learn to love anything by sticking at it. Long exposure to an understaffed night shift mainly deepens your understanding of what an understaffed night shift does to a person. You’re allowed to learn enough to leave.

And a reason, once you have one, is worth money to other people. In one of Adam Grant’s field experiments, paid callers raising money for a university read accounts of what scholarship funds had done for students. Over the following month that group’s weekly pledges and money raised went up against its own earlier figures. The comparison groups’ didn’t.[7] The study counted calls and dollars at one call center for a month, and it can’t tell you what any caller’s life meant. Two people can still read it with interest. One is the caller who has spent a shift being hung up on by graduates in golf sweaters and would like to know what the money does. The other is her manager, who has just watched more dollars come out of the same wages.

Tess knows that second reader. For years she was the cheapest item in her mother’s care, the one resource every discharge plan assumed and none of them priced. The more plainly she cared who’d go without if she stopped, the more got balanced on top of her. A person’s honest answer to why does this matter can be the tool that keeps her from ever going home. She thinks about that on the stairs with her coffee. Then she thinks about a city department that has just learned a handful of people want a room badly enough to run it for nothing.

At seven the man with the wedding speech arrives and shuts the door. Tess has gone up for her coat. She can hear his voice stop, start, then settle into a rhythm. A pause. A clear burst of laughter. He’s made himself laugh, and nobody else is in there. She realizes she’d assumed his wife was dead. For all she knows the two of them are alive, happily married and in possession of a joke about the bride that takes nerve to get through with a straight face. She goes downstairs before she can find out. He asked for a room. His reasons can stay his.

Six Tuesdays

At the end of the first evening Tess writes her profile. I live in an apartment and I’m learning to play the clarinet again. I wanted a room where I could make a horrible noise. Other people wanted one too. Knock if you need the key. She runs the last sentence past Bev, who’d like it to say when to knock. A statement of human purpose benefits from being read by the woman whose door you’ve just volunteered.

Back at her building a second note is waiting, asking what turned out to be wrong with the animal. She knocks and tells him the truth. He takes it well.

“Can you play me something, then?”

She brings the clarinet down. At his kitchen table she plays the three notes with the hard one in the middle. On the table sits a little wind-up metronome he’s owned since he was twelve. He sets it going. She tries at his speed, misses and asks him to slow the thing down. Beethoven put numbers from one of these on his symphonies, and players have spent two centuries finding many of them punishingly fast and suspecting his metronome was broken. A 2020 study compared his marks with the tempos in 36 recordings by different conductors and proposed something duller. He was probably reading the scale at the wrong point on the weight.[8] Very few grand accounts of the purpose of life can absorb a change of tempo from a man in bedroom slippers. Hers waits while he moves the weight.

Some nights it all looks contemptibly small to her. Whole towns are being made uninhabitable and families are being killed in them, and here’s Tess scheduling a trombonist. A full calendar is no proof of what a decent bastard she is. Then she runs it the other way. On the day a town gets to be habitable again, what’s anybody hoping to save? Among other things, a room where somebody unremarkable can shut the door and make a noise nobody has to account for.

So here’s her claim at its actual size. Four half hours on a Tuesday make two hours. Six Tuesdays make twelve. They aren’t twelve hours of progress. Somebody can spend eighteen minutes looking for his glasses. But twelve hours that belonged to a locked room now belong to people who can use them. The arithmetic is modest and anybody can check it, which is more than you can say for what you were put here to do.

On the third Tuesday Leon cancels. Tess comes out of her own half hour, looks at the sign-in sheet and catches herself working out who she could call to come and be a head. Her sister. The man downstairs, slippers and all. She has the feeling in her stomach she used to get outside the pharmacy at six minutes past. That’s when she understands that a purpose did get delivered after all. It came on a clipboard. Keep the count up. It fits her like her mother’s cardigan, because it’s the only kind she’s had practice wearing. Somebody goes without unless Tess keeps going.

It isn’t a bad purpose, and that makes it hard to see around. Bev’s car payment is real. But Tess came here to play the clarinet, and she has an empty room, a spare half hour and nobody who needs to know where she is.

She goes back in and plays. At a quarter to seven she gets a note right that she’s been getting wrong for two weeks. She hears the difference at once, a roundness she can hardly believe came out of her own mouth. She does it again and the roundness is gone. Five minutes later the man with the speech goes by in the hallway and puts his thumb up through the glass panel in the door. He got through it at the wedding. Well, almost. His daughter read the last line for him. He says that was his favorite part.

The sixth Tuesday is the last of the year. Bev unclips the little room’s sheets to go downtown with the rest. Twenty-six names. Tess six times, Leon five, Kit six, Kit’s mother six from a chair in the hallway, the man with the speech three. Neither of them knows whether twenty-six is a lot. Somebody at the recreation department will know, and may have known before counting.

Tess has told Fran she’ll take a turn with the key if the room outlives the year. She’s also told her she won’t be the unpaid woman who does Tuesdays until they carry her out. Fran said of course. People always say of course.

Before the sheets go in the envelope, Tess asks for them back. For six weeks she’s left the purpose column empty beside her name. She goes through all six and prints clarinet in a space two inches wide. It’s a small answer. It’s also the first thing in years she’s put on a form on nobody’s behalf but her own.

Bev seals the envelope. At eight she turns off the upstairs lights. Nobody downtown has told her whether she’ll be paid to turn them on again.
