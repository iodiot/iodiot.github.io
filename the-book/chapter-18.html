Chapter 18Nobody Is Going to Deliver Your Purpose

The note through Tess’s door says, in a beautiful copperplate hand, Is the animal all right?

There is no animal. There is a clarinet, a basket of clean washing and a woman who thought that by sticking the end of one into the other she could spare the neighbours the first stages of her musical education. The note is signed by the man downstairs. He has included his telephone number in case she needs a lift to the vet.

Tess played at school. At fourteen she could get from the bottom of the instrument to the top without anything sounding as though it had sustained a head injury. At fifteen she started seeing a boy who smoked and could get them both into films rated eighteen. The clarinet went under the bed. It resurfaced during two house moves and the death of her mother, by which time the boy had become a bald man whose principal online activity was taking an unusually hard line on parking. Of the two interests, the clarinet had rather better prospects for revival.

She’s had the pads checked and bought new reeds. She’s retrieved the folding stand, which goes up like a deckchair and has roughly the same ambitions regarding your fingers. What she cannot buy at the music shop is twenty minutes in which to be audibly rubbish without anybody having to stay in the same building. People talk about the terrible courage it takes to begin. Sometimes it takes a room with a bloody door.

Fran, from the film night, knows a room. There’s an old upright piano in the little committee room at the hall. Thick walls, a single window over the bins. Tuesday is the caretaker’s late shift for the big room next door. Could they try letting people practise in the little room one evening a week? Six weeks, to begin with. The committee agrees. The caretaker agrees. The man downstairs is warmly in favour.

Then somebody asks Tess for a hundred words about what the project means to her. It’s for one of those little profiles on the hall website. Name, photograph, a bit about your passion. Underneath, in pale grey, the box offers the helpful suggestion Tell us what you were put here to do.

On the morning she opens this email, Tess has just spent forty minutes on her knees persuading the plastic thing under the sink to stop dripping. If she was put here to do that, somebody higher up could have supplied a fucking washer.

She could write that she wants to play the clarinet. It seems an awfully small answer for such a large box. Other people will have answers that go back to childhood, a grandmother who played, an abiding conviction that music brings communities together. Tess’s abiding conviction so far is that she wants half an hour on Tuesday and the right to stop halfway through a scale because she dislikes how her tongue feels. The website will presumably want a slightly more inspiring tongue.

What she really wishes, reading that question, is that somebody would tell her what she was put here to do. She’d take a fairly boring answer. Repair zips. Teach boys to read. Get the price of hearing-aid batteries down. A specified area of responsibility would be welcome. For several years the business of looking after her mother supplied one. When there was milk in the fridge, the prescription had been collected, and a woman with an ulcer on her heel had got to sleep on clean sheets, you knew more or less where you stood in relation to the day. Sometimes you stood in the pharmacy at six minutes past closing, mouthing increasingly imaginative words at a man who had every right to go home. It was a hard way of knowing what to do. It did, nevertheless, tell you.

After her mother died, everybody told Tess she could finally do things for herself. People said this with feeling, as if a fascinating woman called Herself had been patiently developing a range of interests in the spare room. It turned out Herself could watch three consecutive programmes about couples buying holiday homes and was willing to do so again the following evening. Tess could account for the missing hours of her mother’s week down to the minute. She had considerably less idea what to do with the hours that had come back.

What would you like the answer to answer?

There’s a serious appeal to the idea of one universal meaning. If there’s a correct answer, you might waste a few years on the wrong one, but in principle you could find out, change course and stop having to renegotiate the whole arrangement in the shower. A parent could pass something firmer to a child than a taste in sandwich fillings and a heritable tendency to worry about the oven. A dying person might get to know whether they’d done enough. I can think of very few people who would turn down a reliable answer to that last one.

A shared tradition can supply much of this without ever furnishing a convincing argument for somebody outside it. You know whose door to go to after a death, what to carry, which words to say when the person opens up and looks at you blankly. It is no small achievement to have arranged a society in which a distressed household gets fed on Thursday. If your tradition does this for you, you needn’t set fire to it in order to have an interesting inner life. It’s also worth asking how it works for the person who brings the casserole every time, even when she's the one who could use a meal.

Suppose a conclusive message did arrive from the deepest available authority. Imagine the skies over every city on Earth opening simultaneously to reveal that the purpose of human life is music. We have our answer. It’s a Tuesday. Who gets the committee room from six till half past? Should the child who already plays beautifully get the hour before bedtime? Should the drummer, whose neighbours are threatening a sort of informal extradition proceeding, have it instead? Do we sell the piano to repair the roof, or let rain drip onto the piano in recognition of its superior metaphysical status? The answer in the sky doesn’t settle any of this. Someone still has to turn up with a bucket.

And what if you don’t like music? Is the deaf astronomer who has spent thirty good years mapping galaxies now supposed to come home to a recorder on the pillow? The idea that a universal answer would settle our personal obligations quietly assumes agreement about what counts as obeying it. Actual people are impressively capable of starting a schism over the correct load for a dishwasher. Give them a cosmic mandate and a trombone and I’d bring a packed lunch.

When we ask what a life means, we may be trying to get several very different things out of one sentence. Frank Martela and Michael Steger distinguish three aspects in the psychology of meaning. One concerns whether your life makes sense to you. One concerns having aims that give you direction. The third concerns whether being alive seems to count for something. It’s a conceptual distinction, the sort you can test against your Tuesday. It doesn’t come with a laboratory assay for how much purpose you’ve got left in the bottle. [1]

Think of the woman whose partner walked out last month. She knows exactly why. She has seen the messages, knows the other person’s name, understands the train times rather better than she would ever have liked. The whole thing makes horrible sense. She still doesn’t know what to do with her Sunday. Giving her a better account of the train timetable is unlikely to help. Or think of the delivery driver in the middle of a punishing shift. His aim is clear at every turn. In three hundred yards turn left. The question eating at him isn’t where to go. It’s whether he can go on spending the best hours of every day getting bulk dishwasher tablets to a person who thinks a toilet brush with a musical handle is an urgent requirement.

Tess’s mother needed her. That need gave her a direction and a sense of counting to somebody, even when very little about what was happening made sense to her. If you’d tried to explain the greater plan for an infected heel, she might have put you to work changing the dressing. Now those needs have come apart. Nobody needs her to play the clarinet. She’d quite like to do it. Where is that allowed to go on the form?

Borrow my reason for a bit

The first person to ask for a slot is a man who plays the trombone. He sounds wonderful on the telephone. He gives Tess his name, his preferred half-hour and his most heartfelt guarantee that he can stop by six thirty, even in the middle of something. There have been people in her life who promised her undying love with less conviction. She puts him down for the second half-hour. She gives herself the first.

A woman asks for a place for her son, who does piano at school. The piano at his dad’s has headphones, but there’s nothing for him to play at his mum’s. A man wants to use the room to rehearse the speech he’ll give at his daughter’s wedding. He tells Tess he’s losing his voice whenever he gets to the bit about his wife. There’s no instrument involved. He wonders if that disqualifies him. There is at present no written constitution for Tuesday, so the only remaining question is whether he’s free at seven. He is.

That little bit of rule-making bothers Tess. If somebody else had already drawn up a purpose, she could be fair by consulting it. A music project, instruments only. A community space, speech welcome. What authority has she got to decide between them? A clarinet, a notebook, and a set of neighbours who have indicated a strong preference for life. That’s thin.

There’s a less flattering consideration too. If she makes the decision, the man or the mother can think she’s made it badly and tell her so to her face. An eternal law would give her somewhere to forward complaints. Much of the appeal of being a small part of a large design is that there is allegedly an upstairs department. The ability to say “I’m afraid the system won’t let me” has offered a deep spiritual consolation to quite a few people who profess no religious belief whatsoever.

What could it mean to put something worth doing into the world yourself? Is any preference enough? There are people whose authentic, magnificently absorbing passion is getting strangers fired for a joke they made eleven years ago. They could show you a calendar packed with goals, a community of fellow enthusiasts and a collection of measurable results. By any purely administrative account their lives are enviably purposeful. You might prefer to share a car with the man who has trouble getting through a wedding speech.

The philosopher Susan Wolf takes this difficulty seriously. In her account a meaningful life involves someone actually caring about and engaging in something that has value beyond the bare fact they care. She places meaning alongside happiness and moral duty as a reason people do things. Consider the years somebody devotes to learning a difficult piece of music. It can cost them pleasure. Nobody would enter their house with a warrant if they stopped. And yet there can be excellent reason to keep playing, lodged in the piece, in the chance to bring it to life, in this person’s particular love for it. [2]

What makes an activity worth caring about is an honest philosophical fight. Wolf’s proposal gives it a place to happen. The man pursuing a stranger’s dismissal can’t end the discussion by pointing out how meaningful the campaign feels to him. The stranger gets a voice. So does the trombonist who fills an empty hall with a piece of music six people adore and two hundred people would pay good money to escape. Pleasure isn’t a public vote either. If you want a machine that will take all these interests in at one end and produce the correct Tuesday at the other, you’re welcome to invent it. Please make the drawer for complaints removable.

Tess gives the man with the speech a slot. He can use half an hour she expects will otherwise sit empty. If thirty musicians want those four places next week, they will revisit the question. It’s a surprisingly grown-up way to proceed for a woman who has been waiting to be told the meaning of her existence by an online text box.

That evening she tries to write the profile. After caring for her late mother, Tess found healing through music. She takes out healing. Then she takes out late mother. A woman who once drove a wasp out of the kitchen with a frozen fish finger deserves a richer afterlife than becoming the first clause in a promotional email. More importantly, the proposed sentence is premature. So far Tess has produced four doubtful notes and frightened the man downstairs.

The people you can hear through a wall

On the first Tuesday the caretaker shows her which light switch belongs to the little room. She’s set out a chair and a piano stool. For anyone who wants tea there are enamel mugs and spoons downstairs beside the urn. She’s called Bev. She knows how to lift the door a little by the handle to stop it sticking. The committee had suggested branding the trial Open Doors. Bev can show them the precise amount of shoulder required to honour the brand promise.

Tess has the first thirty minutes. It takes her six to put the clarinet together, adjust the reed and get the stand open without losing a finger. Then she sits there. The room has a smell of paste and dust. Someone long ago has stuck a gold star underneath the edge of the piano lid, where it could only ever be awarded to a person lying on the floor. From next door she can hear a shuffle of small feet and a teacher insisting on something called soft knees.

She plays. The first sound is good enough to give her a shock. For a second she’s fourteen and can still do it. On the second note she remembers a finger going down that should have stayed up. The noise that comes out would have confirmed the downstairs neighbour in his gravest suspicions.

Nothing happens. The teacher goes on asking for soft knees. Nobody reassures her. Nobody tells her not to give up or offers the telephone number of an excellent private teacher. She tries again. She gradually realises how much of her experience of playing at home consists of imagining the experience of listening to her play at home. Once you take that person out of the room, it’s remarkable how much more room there is.

At half past she goes out to tell the trombonist it’s his turn. He’s sitting with the instrument between his knees. Has he heard much through the door? “Some,” he says. It occurs to her that she was hoping for either nothing at all or enough to be very impressed. He asks where to put his coat.

There is no applause from the stairs as Tess goes down to the kitchen. She makes tea for herself. The first note from upstairs pushes its way slowly through the ceiling, huge and gently wavering. It sounds like a very big ship asking a much smaller ship out for a drink.

What happened upstairs is interesting partly because of what Tess still hasn’t got. She hasn’t found the thing she’ll be best at. Nobody ran down a corridor shouting for an agent. If talent were the condition of admission, the two of them could probably vacate the building now, and the evening might yet be made available to whatever goes on next door with knees. She chose to do something time-consuming and came to care a little about what happened in doing it. Next week she may care more, or get bored or decide the reed is wrong. The only way of finding out involves attending a life that has already begun.

Here’s a difficulty with advice to discover your passion. It asks a person at the entrance to report on the view from halfway up the hill. You can know what attracts you before you’ve done much of it. You can enjoy records, like the feel of an instrument in your hands, fancy the person playing one at the wedding. What it’s like to listen carefully to a run of three notes, adjusting your breath until the middle one finally belongs with the other two, is a little piece of experience that will initially be unavailable to you. It might become the reason you come back. Asking for that reason at the outset is like demanding references from your unborn children.

Be wary of turning this into a theory that anybody could love any job if they just gave it long enough. Long exposure to a badly staffed night shift can chiefly deepen your appreciation of what a badly staffed night shift does to a person. You’re allowed to learn enough to leave. There is an enormous difference between fishing around for something you might want to sustain and being told to manufacture a sense of vocation as a substitute for two extra people on the rota.

In one of Adam Grant’s field experiments, paid university fundraisers read accounts of how scholarship money affected students. Over the following month the group given that material increased its weekly pledges and money raised relative to its own earlier performance. The comparison groups, who received different information or did their usual work, didn’t show the same improvement. A concrete account of who was affected could change how people did a particular job. The researchers had figures for calls and donations. They did not take delivery of the meaning of any individual caller’s life. [3]

There are at least two people who might read that result with interest. One is the caller who has spent an entire shift being dismissed at speed by people who still have her university’s crest on their golf jumpers. It can matter to know what the money does. The other is her manager, who sees the possibility of extracting a larger sum using precisely the same wages. They have both understood something real about the study. That’s why it matters who is writing the next paragraph.

If a hospital discovers that a porter takes pride in bringing frightened people safely down a corridor, it has discovered something good about the porter. It hasn’t discovered a previously overlooked reason he can manage on less money. Meaning is particularly easy to extract from people who have a good reason for doing what they do. The more they care who will go without if they stop, the more work an institution can quietly balance on top of them. Their personal answer to why it matters is sometimes the instrument used to prevent them ever going home.

At seven the man with the wedding speech arrives. He shuts the door. Tess has gone back upstairs for her coat. She can hear his voice stop, start, then settle into a steady rhythm. There’s a pause and a clear burst of laughter. He’s made himself laugh, there’s nobody else in there. She realises she’d assumed that his wife was dead. For all she knows the pair of them are alive, happily married and in possession of a shared joke about the bride that takes considerable nerve to get through with a straight face. She goes downstairs before she can learn the answer. He asked for a room. She can offer one small piece of the world in which his reasons can remain his own.

Six Tuesdays

Suppose you’re still inclined to find the whole business disappointingly local. A man gets through a speech. A woman plays tolerably well for twenty-four minutes. Stars explode. Entire families are being killed, whole towns are being made uninhabitable, and here’s Tess making arrangements for a trombonist. There has to be something seriously off about our priorities if this is the scale of our response.

Yes, some problems require large commitments. If you’re in a position to move a substantial amount of money to a place where it would keep people alive, those people have every right to be seriously unimpressed by your Tuesday. You could do real, terrible harm while filling your calendar with charming local activities, then hold out the calendar as proof of what a decent bastard you are. The existence of many sources of meaning still leaves room for bad choices among them. We should argue about which causes get the useful rooms, who has them already and who has been promised them so often they’ve given up asking for a key.

But try carrying the argument in the other direction. What are we hoping to save, on the day a town does get to be habitable again? Among other things, a place where somebody entirely unremarkable can shut a door for half an hour and make a noise nobody has to account for. A world preserved purely for the convenience of the next world-saving committee would be a strange sort of victory. Somebody ought eventually to get to live in the thing.

Tess hasn’t established the meaning of life for humanity. She can already tell the difference between two possible arrangements of a particular Tuesday. In one, the boy waits a week to get back to the piano at his dad’s. In the other, he has half an hour with one here. Add up all four half-hours on one evening and they amount to two hours. Run the trial for six weeks and you’ve made twelve hours of practice available. You haven’t made twelve people happy, or produced twelve hours of progress. Somebody can be late. Somebody can spend eighteen minutes looking for their glasses. But twelve hours that used to belong to a locked room will have become twelve hours somebody could use. There’s room for a serious claim in that modest arithmetic. There’s also a limit to the claim, which is one of the things that makes it worth making.

At the end of the first evening Tess writes her profile. I live in a flat and I’m learning to play the clarinet again. I wanted a room where I could make a horrible noise. Other people wanted one too. Knock if you need the key. She goes over the last sentence with Bev, who suggests specifying when and where people should knock, otherwise the person they will wake up at six on a Sunday is fairly easy to identify. There’s a satisfying editorial principle in there. A statement of human purpose benefits from being read by the person whose front door you have just volunteered.

On the third Tuesday the trombonist cancels. Tess sits for a bit after her own slot with no pressing responsibilities, an extra half-hour and a pretty good idea of what the next thirty minutes might be for. She could play some more. She could go home to the washing. She could ring Ellen. She could just sit there while somebody else teaches a group of five-year-olds an interesting medical fact about knees. None of these possibilities, on its own, is capable of explaining the universe. It would be unfair to expect that of an instrument that still requires its own supply of cork grease.

She plays. At quarter to seven she gets a note right that she’s been getting wrong for two weeks. She hears the difference immediately. There’s a lovely roundness to it that she can hardly believe has come from her own mouth. She does it again and the roundness goes. Five minutes later the man with the speech goes past in the corridor and puts his thumb up through the window. He got through it at the wedding. Well, almost. His daughter read the last line to him. He says that was his favourite part.

The trial will finish. Somebody has to work out whether the use of the space is worth paying for, and who will lock up when the children’s class no longer runs late. Tess can agree to take a turn. She can decline a permanent appointment as the unpaid woman who does Tuesdays until they carry her out. Both decisions can be made by a person who takes the room seriously. Some purposes deserve to come to an end, some belong to several people, and some would benefit greatly from having a second key cut.

At home there’s another note from downstairs, this time asking what was wrong with the animal. She knocks. She tells him the truth. He considers it for a moment, sufficiently polite to go through a small performance of surprise.

“Can you play us something, then?”

She goes up for the clarinet. At his kitchen table she plays the three notes with the difficult one in the middle. It comes out clear. On the table he has a little mechanical metronome he’s owned since he was twelve. He sets it going. She tries again at the speed he’s set, misses, and asks him to slow the thing down. There are few grand accounts of the purpose of life that can absorb a change of tempo from a man in bedroom slippers. This one is quite happy to wait.
