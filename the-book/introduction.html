IntroductionBefore You Start

The cover calls this a darkly funny guide to being human. You’re entitled to ask what the guide has seen of the route.

Nobody has finished it and come back. Everyone who got to the end of being human stopped sending reports, which leaves a serious gap in the literature. What’s left is field notes from people partway along. Most of them are lost, several are selling something, and one of them wrote this book. So before you give it an evening, here’s what you’re getting.

You’re not getting a morning routine. This book won’t tell you to get up at five, keep a journal, forgive your father or quit your job. It doesn’t know your father. It has never been in your kitchen, and a stranger who has never been in your kitchen has no business telling you what to do with the one life you get to use it in. Where I do suggest something, it’ll be about the size of a phone call, and I’ll show the arithmetic so you can check it.

The book has a lot of science in it. Cosmology, neuroscience, probability, some physics. I’ve tried to use each for what it can actually tell you, which is always less than you’d like. Cosmology can tell you the age of the universe and show its work. It has nothing to say about whether to lend your brother the money. A certain kind of book fixes this by having the galaxy clear its throat and say something about gratitude. When the physics stops here, I’ll say where it stopped, and the rest of the argument will have to go on foot.

The studies come with their limits attached. A lot of what psychology knows about the human heart was learned from college students doing something mildly embarrassing for course credit. One study you’ll meet sent them into a room wearing Barry Manilow’s face on a T-shirt. When one of those experiments turns up, I’ll tell you what the people in it were asked to do, what got measured, and how far the result can be carried before it drops something. A finding with its limits attached is less fun at dinner. It has the advantage of still being true when you get home.

You’ll get stories you can repeat at dinner anyway. A horse that appeared to do arithmetic until a psychologist started watching the man who asked the questions. A planet that astronomers reported seeing for years and that was never there. Three penguin eggs hauled through an Antarctic winter to a museum that was in no hurry to have them. They’re true, they’re checked, and each chapter lists where to check them yourself.

The jokes are about the things people are actually afraid of. Dying. A body that wears out in an order nobody would have chosen. Money running out before the month does. Being replaced by something that doesn’t need lunch. Being forgotten, or worse, remembered accurately. I won’t insult you, since I don’t know you, and most of what I could guess at I’ve done myself. The target is the flattering story, the one each of us keeps so as to stay the decent party in every account.

Around grief the rule changes. The person who has lost somebody keeps their dignity, and the joke goes to what the world asks of them next: the form, the insurer, the acquaintance with a theory. You’ll also find some swearing, saved for the moments when nothing politer will do.

I should say why anybody writes a book like this. The respectable reason is that the questions are interesting. The other reason is that explaining how to live is a lot more comfortable than doing it, and you get to sit down. I’m not exempt. Wherever I sound as though I have this handled, remember that a person who had it handled would have written a fridge magnet, not twenty-three chapters.

The book opens on “you.” That’s one particular person, and you’ll notice soon enough that it isn’t exactly you. This you is in their early thirties, rents an apartment with a roommate, and has a mother, a brother, a sister, a father who has moods and an uncle named Ben who keeps screws in a mixing bowl. This you once got a laugh at a coworker’s expense and has been thinking about it for three years. About eleven hundred nights, most of the thinking done around two. I borrowed the pronoun because it’s hard to hide behind. Where the shoe fits, I can’t stop you from wearing it. Where it doesn’t, you get to watch somebody else walk around in it, which is one of the oldest pleasures there is.

After a while the you steps back and a town takes over, a mid-sized city a bus ride from a bay. A man who writes assembly instructions for furniture and calls himself a technical author. His older brother, who earns more. A pharmacist. A woman whose father, at eighty-one, wants to make a film about an alien that steals a sock. A community center with a custodian named Bev. All of them are invented. I’m telling you now because the studies and the history aren’t, and you should always know which kind of thing you’re being handed.

Things go wrong for them. Somebody loses a job, and a coworker helped it happen. Twelve seconds of an old man in his kitchen get loose in the world and can’t be called back. A building’s evenings come down to a line in a budget. Somebody dies. I didn’t rescue anybody for your comfort, or for mine, and I’d have liked to. A book that argues you can live without guarantees and then hands one to every character by the last chapter has told you what its author really believes.

What’s left to offer is smaller than a method. A few distinctions that hold weight. Arithmetic you can check. And one habit the book keeps coming back to, which I’ll state plainly at the end, once the people in it have earned the sentence.

It starts in a checkout line, with a lemon and something you said three years ago. It ends in a kitchen, where a man in his late seventies is about to throw a pancake at you. I can’t tell you whether you’ll catch it. That’s about the position the book takes on everything else.
