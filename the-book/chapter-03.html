Chapter 03The Big Bang Wasn't the Beginning of Your Problems

At the planetarium the lights go out, the ceiling disappears, and a girl in the third row puts one hand into her father’s popcorn without taking her eyes off the birth of the stars. She’s eight. When the house lights come up she has a question.

“What was there before the universe?”

Her father has paid for two tickets and would like the people with the seventy-foot ceiling to handle this one. “Well,” he says, “there was the Big Bang.”

“Yes,” she says. “Before that.”

Watch his hands. He folds the top of the popcorn bag over once, then again, then a third time, like a man sealing a bid. It buys him four seconds. Every movie this kid has ever seen started while something else was going on. People were finding their seats. Somebody was arguing about the popcorn. So what was the universe doing while the universe got ready? Physicists get paid to ask that. They haven’t answered it. Her father doesn’t know they haven’t, and I’m not sure he’d use the information if he did.

He gets her every other weekend. Twenty-six weekends a year comes to fifty-two days in which to be the father who knows things. She spends the other three hundred and thirteen in a house across town with her mother and a man who owns a telescope. Her father has been told about the telescope twice.

So he doesn’t say I don’t know. He says, “It was all squeezed into one tiny dot. And then it exploded.” He shows her the dot between his finger and thumb. Then he does the explosion with both hands, and the bag he sealed so carefully goes under the seat in front.

I know the voice he does it in. I own one. It sits about a half step below my real voice, and it has explained interest rates, the Electoral College and how planes stay up, on the strength of a headline and a general feeling. Nobody has ever asked it for a source. An eight-year-old is about to.

One tiny dot

“Where was the dot?” she says.

“In the middle.”

“The middle of what?”

She isn’t being difficult. She’s using a rule that has never let her down. Water on the kitchen floor leads back to a pipe. A broken lamp leads back to a ball, and the ball leads back to her. Everything she has met so far had something before it and somewhere to be. She’d like the universe to hold still for the same treatment.

Twelve words from her father, and the picture has already gone wrong in two places. Take the explosion first. A bomb goes off somewhere, and the pieces fly out into a room that was there before the bomb. The standard account in cosmology has no room and no somewhere. It says that the part of the universe we can observe was once far hotter and denser than it is now, and that space itself has been stretching ever since, so that galaxies not bound to each other by gravity keep getting farther apart. No spot in the sky is where it happened. It happened where she’s sitting, and where the popcorn landed, and everywhere else. [1], [2]

Then the dot. What the measurements support is that everything we can see was once packed very close together. They don’t say the whole universe was a marble in an empty room. The whole may be far bigger than the part we see, and nobody knows how big.

The grown-up name for the dot is a singularity, and it has done the father’s kind of work for a lot of people. Run Einstein’s equations for an expanding universe backward and the density climbs. Keep going, and at a finite time in the past the numbers run off to infinity. That’s the singularity. It sounds like an object, a thing you could keep in a jar. All the word marks is the place where the equations quit giving answers. At densities like that, physicists expect quantum effects to matter, and no tested theory covers them. A sign at the edge of the map has been mistaken for a town.

His daughter is waiting. Children can wait in a way that uses up the air.

“The middle of everything,” he says, and stands up, because the next show is being seated.

Ask me

In the lobby one wall says THE UNIVERSE IS 13.8 BILLION YEARS OLD in letters a foot high. Under the letters hangs a speckled blue and orange oval the size of a ping-pong table. Beside the oval stands a man of about seventy in a red vest, wearing a button as big as a saucer. The button says ASK ME.

Her father steers her to the far end of the wall.

He had the number already. Everybody does. He’d heard “13.8 billion years” and assumed that anything with a birthday that specific must have a birth certificate on file. And here, at last, is a question he can do.

“So how big is it?” she says.

“Okay. Nothing goes faster than light. Light’s had 13.8 billion years to get here. So the farthest thing we can see is 13.8 billion light-years away. That’s the edge.”

She’s reading the wall. She reads everything now. It started sometime last fall, and it started at the other house.

“It says forty-six.”

It does. In smaller letters under the oval: The observable universe extends about 46 billion light-years in every direction.

The wall is right, and the reason is one of the better surprises in the subject. The oldest light we receive has been traveling for nearly the whole 13.8 billion years. While it traveled, the space it was crossing kept stretching, and the matter that sent it kept being carried away. That matter is now about 46 billion light-years from us. So the part of the universe we can see has a radius more than three times the one her father just announced. Even that is only a horizon, the distance from which light has had time to arrive. A horizon isn’t a wall. Nobody knows how much universe lies past it. [10]

He looks at the wall for a while. The honest sentence has three words in it. He tries a longer one first.

“That’s probably counting both directions.”

Both directions would be 27.6. He knows it while he’s saying it. She can’t multiply decimals yet, and he gives that about a year.

From across the lobby you might take the next few seconds for a father deciding how much science a kid can handle. She can handle plenty. What takes the time is deciding how much ignorance a man can bear to admit in front of his daughter on one of his fifty-two days.

“I don’t know why it says that,” he says.

It’s the first true thing he’s said since the lights came up. He waits for the floor to open. She looks at him. Then she looks down the wall at the red vest.

“He says to ask him.”

What’s outside the bread

The man in the vest has been standing next to that button since ten this morning, and they’re his first customers. He doesn’t start with the wall.

“You ever watch raisin bread rise?”

She hasn’t. He does it with his hands anyway. Picture two raisins deep inside the loaf, an inch apart. The dough between them swells until the inch is two inches. A third raisin started two inches away, and in the same time its gap has grown to four. The near raisin moved off by one inch and the far one by two. Double the distance and you double the speed. Now sit on a different raisin and look around. Same view. Every raisin sees all the others backing away, the far ones fastest, and every raisin has excellent grounds for believing it’s the middle. For galaxies, that proportion between distance and speed is called Hubble’s law. [5]

“The raisins don’t get bigger,” the man says. “Just the gaps.” The same goes for atoms, the solar system and the galaxy we live in. Things held together by their own forces stay the size they were while the great distances between galaxies grow.

Her father would like to point out that this is more or less what he meant by the middle. He lets it go.

“What’s outside the bread?” she says.

The man in the vest looks as if he’s been handed a present. “That’s where the bread quits on you.”

A loaf has a crust, an oven and somebody standing over it who’d like breakfast. None of those comes with the cosmology. The geometry of the gaps is the only part you’re allowed to take out of the bakery. Carry the crust home too and you’ll spend a long evening looking for the outside of space.

“So is there an outside?”

“Don’t know,” the man says. “Nobody’s found an edge.” He says it the way you’d give somebody the time.

How does anyone know the raisins are moving at all? By the light. Light from a distant galaxy arrives with its wavelengths stretched toward the red end of the spectrum, and the farther away the galaxy, the bigger the stretch. For galaxies that aren’t too far off, the stretch grows roughly in proportion to the distance, which is the raisin arithmetic seen from our own raisin. Run the stretching backward and everything was closer, denser and hotter. That’s a measurement of how the universe has changed over time. It isn’t a photograph of the first instant. [5]

The oldest hiss in New Jersey

“How do you know it was hot?” her father says. It surprises him. It’s the first question he’s asked a stranger in about a year.

The man in the vest has a favorite story, and it’s about pigeon shit. For the girl’s sake he says droppings.

In the mid-1960s two radio astronomers at Bell Labs, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, were working with a twenty-foot horn antenna on a hill in Holmdel, New Jersey. It picked up a faint, steady hiss of microwave noise that they couldn’t account for. The hiss was the same by day and by night, and the same wherever they pointed the horn. They went through everything that might be producing it. A pair of pigeons had been roosting inside and had coated the horn with what Wilson, in his Nobel lecture, called “a white material familiar to all city dwellers.” [7] The two men trapped the birds and released them about thirty miles away. The birds came back. Nearly fifty years later Penzias told a radio interviewer about buying a box of shotgun shells. [11] Then they cleaned out the horn. Almost all of the hiss was still there.

Consider what they could know that afternoon. They hadn’t heard that physicists at Princeton had predicted a leftover glow from a hot young universe and were building a receiver to look for one. No theory was riding on the cleaning. They had a noise, a broom and a question, which was whether the noise would survive the broom. It did. A phone call later put the two groups in touch, and in 1965 they published side by side. The Princeton paper supplied the cosmology. The Bell Labs paper is called “A Measurement of Excess Antenna Temperature at 4080 Mc/s.” It reports that at one frequency the antenna read about 3.5 kelvin hotter than they could explain, and about the same in every direction they’d checked. It makes no announcement about the universe. [7], [8]

Her father did the creation of everything an hour ago with two hands and a bag of popcorn. These two had caught the oldest light anybody has ever caught, and they wouldn’t claim more than a hiss.

One frequency gives you one number. A glow left over from a hot beginning makes a much riskier promise. Its brightness, measured across many frequencies, has to trace a particular hump called a blackbody curve. For today’s temperature of about 2.7 kelvin the hump peaks near 160 gigahertz, a wavelength of about 2 millimeters. The Holmdel horn listened at 4,080 megahertz, or 7.35 centimeters, far down one slope. The rest of the curve was still out there, free to embarrass everybody. Decades later the FIRAS instrument on NASA’s COBE satellite compared the sky with a calibrated source at frequencies across the whole peak. On NASA’s published graph the uncertainties in the measurements fit inside the thickness of the predicted line. [9]

The graph is on the wall next to the oval. She studies it.

“Where are the dots?”

“Under the line,” says the man in the vest, as pleased as if he’d put them there himself.

“Can we see the light?”

He lays his hand flat on the speckled oval. This is it. For its first 380,000 years or so the universe was a glowing fog of loose electrons and nuclei, and light couldn’t get anywhere without being knocked off course. Then things cooled enough for atoms to form. The fog cleared and the light got away. It has been traveling ever since, stretched by the expansion from a glare into microwaves, and the oval is a map of it over the whole sky. People call it the universe’s baby picture. It’s a picture from 380,000 years in. The first instant isn’t in it. [1], [3]

“Not with your eyes,” the man says. “But that’s what it looks like.”

She puts her own hand next to his.

The number over their heads comes out of the same speckles. Nobody read 13.8 billion years off a clock. Take a model of the universe with six adjustable quantities, fit it to the pattern of warmer and cooler spots in that map along with other measurements, and the age falls out. The Planck satellite team’s fit gives about 13.8 billion. [4] A different model could give a different number. First it would have to account for the stretched light, the shape of the hump and the amounts of helium and heavy hydrogen cooked in the first few minutes, all in the same universe. [1] Parts of the picture are still argued over, including how fast the expansion is going right now. Whoever wants to replace it inherits all of the homework.

Before that

She hasn’t forgotten. She’s eight, and she’s been carrying it since the third row.

“What was before the hot part?”

“Nobody knows,” says the man in the vest.

Her father waits for the rest. That is the rest. Then the man tells her what the not-knowing looks like up close, and he seems to enjoy it more than the pigeons.

Maybe time itself starts there. In some models it does, and then asking what came before is like asking what’s north of the North Pole. The comparison shows how a question that works everywhere else can run out of meaning at one spot. It doesn’t show that time has such a spot. Maybe the hot beginning followed something earlier. Some physicists study models in which a previous universe shrank and bounced. These ideas aren’t interchangeable, they don’t have equal support, and none of them has been confirmed. [6] “Of course there was a before” assumes clocks run the same however far back you go. “Of course there wasn’t” gives one possibility the standing of a measurement. An eight-year-old is entitled to find both answers unsatisfying. So are the people working on it.

Her father has spent the afternoon treating I don’t know as a hole in the floor. He has just watched a man in a red vest stand in the hole for five minutes and get thanked for it. From over here it looks more like the place where the work is. The men with the horn stood in it too. They said what the antenna could support and stopped, and their paper has a title you could mistake for a parts list. His own answer had a dot, a middle and an explosion. All it had going for it was that it ended the conversation, and it didn’t even do that.

It would be nice to report that he feels lighter. He feels like a man who has been corrected by a wall. But he asked a question in public and nothing fell on him, and his daughter is looking at the oval, not at him.

She doesn’t seem to think less of the man in the vest for not knowing. She seems to think the question just got bigger, which she likes.

On purpose

She’s supposed to be asleep on the pull-out by nine. At nine-thirty they’re sitting on the strip of grass behind his building, because she wanted to find out whether any of it shows from his place. The sky over the parking lot is orange. They count eleven stars.

“Does the universe have a plan?”

The first question was hers. This one he’s been asking for a year. If somebody planned it, then somebody meant the lawyers, the second toothbrush and the calendar with her initials on alternate Fridays, and he has notes. He puts his head in his hand.

“God, I hope not,” he says. “Imagine this being on purpose.”

Nothing he learned this afternoon settles it. Cosmology can give you the age of the universe, the temperature of its oldest light and the recipe for the first atoms. It has found no sign that any of that was aimed at a strip of grass behind an apartment building. It also hasn’t found that the strip of grass doesn’t matter. That would be a second claim, about what’s worth caring about, and the microwave sky is silent on it either way. The physics will take you from the fog to the galaxies. It won’t tell you whether last year was deserved, and it has no advice about a kid who’s still awake.

So he’s back where he was in the third row, with a question he can’t answer and a daughter waiting. The difference is that he’s seen it done. Say how far your reading goes. Stop there. Leave the rest open, and stay in the room.

“This what?” she says.

He has the low voice ready. He could say mosquitoes.

“Last year,” he says, in the other one. “I don’t know why it went the way it did. I’m still working on it.”

She considers the eleven stars. “We could ask the man in the vest.”

“He just does the universe.”

“Can we go back, though?”

They can. It’s two weeks from today, and it’s his weekend. That part is on purpose. He signed it.
