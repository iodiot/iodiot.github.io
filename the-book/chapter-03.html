Chapter 03The Big Bang Wasn't the Beginning of Your Problems

At the planetarium the lights go out, the ceiling disappears, and a child in the third row puts one hand into a parent's popcorn without taking their eyes off the birth of the stars. When the house lights come up the child has a question. “What was there before the universe?”

The parent has paid for two tickets and would like the people with the seventy-foot ceiling to handle this one. “Well, there was the Big Bang,” the parent begins. “Yes,” says the child. “Before that.”

You can feel the small trap snap shut. Every movie this kid has ever seen started while something else was happening. People were finding their seats. Somebody was arguing about popcorn. So what was the universe doing while the universe was getting ready? There's a respectable question here, and another respectable question about whether those words still make sense at the far end of time.

The Big Bang is one of the strongest scientific accounts we have of the early history of the observable universe. It is also surrounded by questions it does not settle. The model tells us a great deal about a universe that was once hotter and denser and has expanded for billions of years. It does not hand us a final answer to why there is a universe, whether time had an absolute beginning, or whether “before” is even a meaningful word at the earliest boundary we can describe.

The parent had heard “13.8 billion years” and reasonably assumed that anything with a birthday that specific must have a birth certificate on file. Let's follow the number back to the evidence. Then we can see which parts of the child's question it answers.

Why we need a first domino

The child’s question has a useful brutality. You can say hot dense early state in your most authoritative voice. A person with popcorn stuck to their jumper can still ask where that came from. Children have done a great deal for philosophy by being entirely unimpressed with a change of vocabulary.

The demand for an origin feels logical because causes matter. We would not be wise to ignore them. If water appears under a sink, it is useful to know whether a pipe cracked or a child poured a glass on the floor. If a bridge fails, engineers look for the physical sequence that led to failure. In those cases, “what caused this?” narrows the field of action. The answer changes what we should do next.

Cosmic origins ask a different kind of question. We can describe earlier and earlier stages of the universe, but asking for a cause of the whole sequence may require a kind of explanation that does not fit inside the sequence. That does not make the question meaningless. It does mean we should notice when the word “cause” quietly assumes time already exists, and when “before” assumes a clock that can continue past the thing we are calling the beginning.

Think of a row of dominoes spread across the kitchen floor. You can ask which tile knocked over this one. You can also ask who lined them up. Catch the person who placed the first tile and you can ask what possessed them to spend Sunday on their knees doing that. You've changed the question twice, and the language of what came first has politely concealed both changes.

We are not wrong to want answers. The trouble starts when we mistake the emotional relief of an answer for evidence that it is true. A sentence can soothe us and still be unsupported. A sentence can leave us unsettled and still be the most honest account available.

There are several questions tangled together here. What physical process produced the universe we observe? Does the universe have an absolute beginning? Why is there something rather than nothing? Does any of this tell us what our lives are for? Cosmology can make progress on the first question. It can constrain possible answers to the second. The last two belong to a different conversation. We get confused when we expect one kind of answer to do every job.

What the Big Bang actually describes

The phrase “Big Bang” encourages a picture of an explosion. We imagine a dark, empty room, a central flash, and pieces flying outward into space. It is a vivid image. It is also a poor description of the standard cosmological model.

The model describes the observable universe expanding from an earlier state that was much hotter, denser, and more uniform than the universe we see today. As the universe expanded, it cooled. Particles formed, atoms eventually formed, gravity gathered matter into stars and galaxies, and the large-scale universe continued to evolve. The Big Bang is not a bomb exploding at one location inside a pre-existing container. On cosmic scales, it is space itself expanding, so distant, unbound regions grow farther apart. The model does not identify a special central spot in space from which everything flew outward. [1], [2]

Imagine two raisins well inside a rising loaf. The dough between them expands. Pick a different raisin and the same thing happens from there. The loaf earns its keep for one sentence, then promptly goes to hell as a model of the universe. Bread has a crust, an oven and an outside observer who would like breakfast. None of those comes free with the cosmology.

The same care applies to the phrase “the universe was a point.” In some popular explanations, the whole universe is compressed into a single point and then expands. That sentence can suggest that all of space was a tiny marble surrounded by an empty region. The evidence we have does not require that picture. What we can say with confidence is that the portion of the universe we can observe was once far more compressed and hot. The total universe may extend beyond what we can observe. We do not know its full size, and an observable horizon is not a wall at the edge of existence.

Look at the gap between two raisins in the model. If a one-inch gap becomes two inches, a two-inch gap becomes four under the same uniform stretch. Double the original separation and you double the increase. That’s a useful piece of geometry to take out of the bakery. Take the crust or the oven home with you and you’re going to spend a long evening looking for the outside of space. Atoms and gravitationally bound systems such as our solar system hold together as the large-scale distances between unbound galaxies grow.

That's already quite a discovery. The model lets us work backward from what reaches our telescopes now. The first surprise is that a good deal of the evidence arrives as perfectly ordinary light, stretched to wavelengths our eyes can't see.

The oldest light is not the beginning

One of the most remarkable things we can observe is the cosmic microwave background, usually shortened to CMB. It is faint radiation arriving from every direction. Today it has cooled to a temperature of about 2.7 kelvin. Its pattern is almost uniform, with tiny variations that carry information about the early distribution of matter and energy.

The CMB is often described as the universe’s baby picture. The comparison is useful if we remember that it is not a picture of the first instant. The light we detect was released when the universe was about 380,000 years old. Before that, the universe was a hot plasma. Electrons scattered light so often that photons could not travel freely across long distances. As the universe expanded and cooled, electrons combined with nuclei to form neutral atoms. The fog lifted. Light could travel. The radiation from that transition has been stretched by the expansion into the microwave signal we measure now. [1], [3]

In New Jersey in the 1960s, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson had an antenna picking up more microwave noise than they could account for. A pair of pigeons had been living in the horn. The birds went, their droppings went, and almost all the excess stayed. There is something rather lovely about a route to the early universe that takes you through the question of whose turn it is to clean up the pigeon shit. [7]

An observer in a distant galaxy could also look in every direction and find old light arriving. Each of you stands at the center of the region from which light has had time to reach you. This gives both of you impeccable grounds for saying “the view from here.” A joint application for the position of Center of Everything may encounter difficulties at interview.

In their 1965 paper Penzias and Wilson reported what their instrument could support. At the frequency they measured, there was excess noise with much the same strength in the different directions they checked. Physicists at Princeton offered a cosmological explanation in a companion paper. Those were two separate jobs. Had cleaning the horn made the signal disappear, a beautiful theory would have come out of that afternoon owing two pigeons an apology. [8]

Three lines of evidence

The hot Big Bang model did not win because “everything must have started somewhere.” It gained strength because several independent kinds of evidence point toward a universe that was once hotter and denser.

First, distant galaxies show cosmological redshift. As light travels through expanding space, its wavelength stretches. The more distant a galaxy is, on average, the more its light is shifted toward longer wavelengths. This pattern is evidence that the universe is expanding on large scales. If we run that expansion backward in our models, the universe was more compact in the past. The observation is not a view of the first instant; it is a measurement of a change in the universe over time. [5]

Second, a leftover glow should have a particular shape when you plot its intensity across frequencies. This offered the universe a sporting chance to embarrass the theorists. Penzias and Wilson had a puzzling excess at one frequency. Decades later the FIRAS instrument on NASA’s COBE satellite compared the sky across a range of frequencies with a calibrated thermal source. On NASA’s published graph the measurement uncertainties fit inside the thickness of the predicted curve. A child looking at that graph might reasonably ask where the observations are. Under the line is a rather glorious answer. [9]

The third line comes from chemistry. In the expanding universe's first few minutes, nuclear reactions produced, among other things, helium nuclei and a small residue of deuterium, a heavy form of hydrogen. The predicted abundances depend on conditions at the time. Astronomers can look in relatively unprocessed gas today and check the accounting. Deuterium is a particularly good witness. Nobody has had to persuade it to remember what it was doing thirteen billion years ago.

Expansion gives you a changing distance scale. The shape of the microwave spectrum gives you a thermal history. The light elements let you check what the first few minutes could have cooked. A rival account has to get all three jobs done in the same universe. There’s the satisfaction in the thing. Any fool can explain a loaf of bread after you’ve shown it to him. Ask him what else should be in the kitchen before you open the door.

There are also details that remain unsettled. The measured expansion rate has produced tensions between some methods of estimating it. Models of inflation, a proposed period of very rapid early expansion, are consistent with important features in the CMB, but the physical process behind inflation remains unknown. These are live scientific questions. They do not erase the evidence for expansion or the hot early universe. Uncertainty in the details is not a permission slip to treat every idea as equally supported.

The age we can calculate

The familiar estimate that the universe is about 13.8 billion years old is not a reading from a cosmic stopwatch. It is inferred by combining observations with a model of how the universe has expanded and what it contains. The Planck Collaboration’s analysis of the cosmic microwave background, using the standard six-parameter cosmological model and additional measurements, gave an age close to 13.8 billion years. The shorthand is useful. The conditions behind the estimate matter too. [4]

A measured age is not the same as a measurement of the total size of everything. We can estimate how long the observable universe has been expanding from its hot early state. We cannot use that number to conclude that the whole universe is only 13.8 billion light-years across, or that it has an edge we could reach by traveling long enough. Light takes time to travel, and space has expanded while it traveled. Our observable horizon reflects what signals have had time to reach us, not necessarily the full extent of existence.

The age printed on the planetarium wall is the result of fitting a model to observations. Estimates have uncertainties. If a different model can fit those observations and outperform this one on other measurements, the estimate can change with it. Anyone announcing a replacement owes us more than an interesting story. They've inherited all the homework.

Thirteen point eight billion sounds like the sort of number that ought to put a row about who called whom firmly in its place. Then the lights come up. A kid has lost a coat. The age of the universe survives the discovery without revision. Whether the family makes the bus depends rather heavily on finding that bloody coat.

Where the model reaches its edge

A model can describe a great deal and still leave a boundary. When scientists apply the equations of classical general relativity to the universe and extrapolate backward, some solutions reach a state called a singularity. In the mathematics, quantities such as density or curvature can become infinite. That result is often retold as though physics had found a tiny point where everything was packed together and then watched it explode.

Follow some classical cosmological solutions backward and quantities such as density diverge. We give that trouble a dignified name, a singularity. Giving it a name does not tell us what physically happened there. At those extremes we expect quantum effects to matter, and we lack a tested theory that takes us across the disputed ground.

This leaves room for serious possibilities. The hot early phase may have followed an earlier phase. Some mathematical models explore a bounce from a previous contracting universe. Others describe a beginning in which ordinary time does not extend farther backward. There are proposals involving inflation, quantum states, or a larger cosmic setting. These ideas are not interchangeable, and they do not all have the same evidential status. At present, none has become a confirmed answer to the question of an absolute origin. [6]

The word “nothing” is especially slippery. In ordinary speech, nothing means no objects, no room, no time, no laws, no possible events. In physics, a “vacuum” can still have fields, structure, and rules. A quantum vacuum is not the same thing as absolute nonexistence. If someone says the universe came from nothing, it is worth asking what they mean by nothing. If their nothing has laws and physical properties, it may be a very interesting something wearing a dark coat.

The boundary of a model is not proof that reality ends there. It is also not proof that an explanation waits just beyond it, ready to deliver a purpose. We can mark what our best theories describe, identify where they lose reliability, and keep working. This is not the end of thought. It is a more accurate map of where thought currently stands.

Before may be a different kind of question

We ask what happened before the Big Bang because every event we know has a before. But “before” is a temporal relation. If time itself has a boundary in a particular cosmological model, asking what happened before it may be like asking what lies north of the North Pole. The analogy helps us notice a possible problem in the question. It does not prove that time had a boundary or that no earlier phase existed.

That caveat matters. The phrase “time began with the Big Bang” is often stated with more certainty than the evidence warrants. Some models treat the Big Bang as the beginning of time as we understand it. Other models explore a prior phase or a different description in which our familiar time is not fundamental. We do not have a settled answer. The evidence supports a hot, expanding early universe. It does not let us confidently describe every possible condition before that history.

There are at least two ways for the parent to get this wrong. “Of course there was a before” assumes the familiar arrangement of time holds however far back you go. “Of course there wasn't” gives a particular possibility the authority of a measurement. A child is entitled to find both answers unsatisfactory. So is a physicist.

The personal question inside the cosmic one

The kid might simply be curious about the start of the film. Adults sometimes smuggle a second question into the ticket price. If the universe had an intention, perhaps somebody meant for me to be here. If there was a plan, perhaps what happened to my family last year belongs to it. You can know how hydrogen formed and still have to sit through a long night with those questions.

Cosmology can tell us about the universe’s age, expansion, early radiation, and the conditions in which matter formed. It can explain how stars made many of the elements that later became planets and bodies. That history is remarkable. It does not tell us that the universe was aiming at us. It also does not prove that our lives are meaningless. The physical account and the moral interpretation are separate kinds of work.

We can be tempted to treat a cosmic explanation like an official family history. If the universe has a purpose, perhaps our pain belongs inside a design. If it has no purpose, perhaps our pain is pointless. Both conclusions smuggle a human need into a scientific description. A telescope can measure light from ancient galaxies. It cannot tell us whether a particular loss was deserved or whether a person should keep going through a difficult season.

The universe is indifferent in the limited sense that physical processes do not distribute outcomes according to human fairness. Gravity does not spare the kind person. A virus does not check character references. A storm does not review the household’s moral record before choosing a roof. That is a statement about nature’s mechanisms, not a complete philosophy of how people should treat one another.

What evidence would count as finding a purpose? Suppose every telescope tomorrow reports a new kind of radiation. You can imagine the work of checking it against other instruments and other directions in the sky. Now suppose the parent hears the child say, very quietly, that the dark part of the film was frightening. There’s something to do with that information before a single grant application needs filling in.

Curiosity without a final answer

“I don't know” earns its place when you can name the next thing worth knowing. In the case of the microwave sky, people can measure the pattern more closely and compare the result with competing predictions. In the case of whether existence has a purpose, it helps to say what you would even count as an answer. Different questions place different demands on the person holding the popcorn.

We can sort cosmic claims into useful categories. The expansion of the observable universe is supported by measurements. A hot, dense early phase explains the CMB and the observed abundances of light elements. The age of about 13.8 billion years follows within a well-tested model and its assumptions. Inflation is a leading framework for a very early expansion phase, but its underlying mechanism is not known. What existed before the earliest phase we can describe remains unresolved. These are not identical levels of certainty.

The parent looks down. There are crumbs in the child's palm. From the back of the room you might mistake those few seconds of silence for an adult deciding how much science a kid can handle. Quite a lot, as it happens. What takes the time is deciding how much ignorance a parent can bear to admit in front of their kid.

On the way out, the parent takes another run at it. “We can tell that it used to be much hotter. There's light left over from when it cooled down. Whether there was a before, I don't know.” The child walks a few more steps. “Can we see the light?” Now they have a question they can do something with. They turn around and go back in.

The parent holds the door. A child came in with a question. Somewhere between the ticket desk and the way out, somebody acquired an obligation to answer honestly. For a universe whose laws make no mention of what anybody owes a child, it seems to have produced at least two people who can tell when an answer falls short.
