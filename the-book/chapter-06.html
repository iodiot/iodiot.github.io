Chapter 06Free Will, Responsibility, and Other Uncomfortable Questions

You did not choose the ingredients

You did not choose your genes. You did not choose the neighborhood, language, family, or century into which you were born. You did not select the first people who taught you what anger looked like, what money meant, or whether being loved was something you could count on. You did not choose the brain you received, the early conditions that shaped it, or the list of things that make your nervous system reach for the emergency brake.

And then one day, usually before you have finished learning how to operate a printer, someone asks you to take responsibility for yourself.

The request can sound unfair. How can you be responsible for a personality you did not design? How can a person be blamed for doing what their history and biology made them do? If every action comes from prior causes, then perhaps “choice” is only the story the brain tells after the machinery has already moved. Perhaps nobody is really responsible. Perhaps we should abolish blame, punishment, praise, regret, and every awkward conversation that begins, “I think we need to talk about what happened at dinner.”

The other response is to defend free will by treating people as if they created themselves. You chose your choices. You could have done otherwise. Therefore every outcome is yours, and the conditions that shaped you are irrelevant. This is emotionally satisfying to people who have already succeeded and less satisfying to people who have spent their lives being told that every obstacle is a character test.

Both answers are too neat. The first turns human beings into passive events. The second acts as if we each arrived from nowhere, fully assembled, carrying an instruction booklet we chose to ignore. We are not self-created. We are also not rocks.

The question is not whether you chose the initial conditions of your life. You did not. The question is what responsibility can mean for creatures who are formed by causes, can reflect on some of those causes, and can sometimes alter what happens next.

The ghost in the decision

When people talk about free will, they often picture a tiny executive sitting inside the skull, untouched by biology and history, making decisions from a position of perfect independence. This inner chooser is supposed to be the real you: not your body, not your childhood, not the brain producing the thought, but a separate source of action that could have chosen anything at all.

The image creates an impossible job. If the chooser is shaped by nothing, how does it choose? If it has reasons, preferences, memories, or a personality, then those are part of what shapes the choice. If it is not influenced by anything, its decision begins to look less like freedom and more like a coin flip happening in a room with no windows.

Adding randomness does not solve the problem. If a decision is partly random, that may make it less predictable. It does not automatically make it more yours. A thunderstorm is unpredictable. We do not call it morally responsible for the broken umbrella.

Perhaps freedom does not require being an uncaused cause. Perhaps it means something more practical: being able to consider reasons, respond to information, pause, imagine consequences, and act without someone else directly forcing your body. This kind of freedom comes in degrees. It can be impaired by coercion, addiction, brain injury, panic, ignorance, or a lack of real alternatives. It can be strengthened by education, safety, time, support, and practice.

That answer will not satisfy everyone. Some philosophers, often called incompatibilists, argue that genuine responsibility requires a deeper kind of freedom than this. Others, known as compatibilists, argue that responsibility can exist in a determined world if our actions flow through our own values and capacities rather than external force. The disagreement is real. No brain scan has settled it. We should be suspicious of anyone who claims to have ended centuries of philosophy with a demonstration involving a wrist flick and a rotating clock.

But the practical version of freedom is not nothing. A person who is threatened into handing over their wallet is different from a person who plans a robbery. Someone whose actions are severely constrained by illness or coercion is different from someone who understands what they are doing and can respond to reasons. These differences matter even if both people’s actions have causes.

What the button experiments can and cannot tell us

The neuroscientist Benjamin Libet and colleagues ran a now-famous experiment in the 1980s. Participants watched a moving clock and flexed a finger or wrist whenever they felt like it. They reported when they first became aware of the urge to move. The researchers also recorded a gradual change in electrical activity over the scalp that preceded the movement, known as the readiness potential. In that task, the measured neural activity began before participants reported conscious awareness of their intention to move.

The result is interesting. It suggests that some brain activity related to action preparation can precede the conscious feeling of deciding. It does not demonstrate that every meaningful choice is unconsciously completed before awareness. The participants were making arbitrary, low-stakes movements with no substantial reason to choose one moment over another. Deciding whether to flex a wrist now or a few seconds later is not the same kind of problem as deciding whether to confess a betrayal, leave an unsafe relationship, or spend ten years caring for a parent.

Later studies extended the question. In a 2008 experiment, Chun Siong Soon and colleagues used brain imaging to decode which of two buttons participants would press several seconds before they reported having made the decision. The researchers reported accuracy of around 60 percent: above chance, not perfect. The task again involved an arbitrary choice between buttons. A signal that modestly predicts a simple choice is not a recording of a fully settled decision, much less proof that a person’s entire future is fixed and consciously irrelevant.

There are also questions about how to interpret the readiness potential itself. Aaron Schurger, Jacobo Sitt, and Stanislas Dehaene proposed in 2012 that the gradual buildup seen before spontaneous actions could partly reflect the brain’s accumulation of fluctuating background activity until it crosses a threshold for movement. On this account, the readiness potential need not be a hidden decision already made and waiting to be revealed. It can be a pattern that emerges when researchers line up many trials by the moment the movement occurred.

These experiments do not make the brain irrelevant to choice. They make it unavoidable. Decisions are physical events in a living organism. There is no scientific evidence that a separate, nonphysical manager steps in from outside the causal story. But “the brain is active before I notice an intention” does not mean “my conscious reasoning never matters.” Conscious awareness may be late to some processes, involved in others, or able to interrupt and revise an emerging action. The details depend on what kind of decision we are studying.

A decision is not a single light switch. It is a sequence: information arrives, memories and expectations become relevant, options are considered, bodily states shift, attention narrows or widens, intentions form, actions begin, and consequences feed back into future choices. Some of that happens outside awareness. Some of it is conscious. The borders between the stages are not neatly labeled in the experience of deciding, which is why “I chose it” can be both true and incomplete.

The causes are part of you

Suppose you are deciding whether to apologize. The decision has causes. Perhaps you remember how it felt when someone apologized to you. Perhaps you were taught to avoid conflict. Perhaps the person you hurt matters to you. Perhaps you are embarrassed, defensive, tired, or afraid that an apology will be used against you. Your brain and body bring all of this into the moment, whether or not you can list it on a whiteboard.

Does that mean you did not choose? It depends on what you mean by choose. If you mean “did I create every condition that influenced this decision?” then no. No one does. If you mean “did the action come through my own capacities to think, care, hesitate, and respond to reasons?” then often yes, at least partly.

Causes do not automatically make an action alien to the person who performs it. Your memories cause you to recognize a friend. Your fear causes you to step away from a ledge. Your values influence which promise you keep. We do not usually treat these influences as intruders taking over a person from the outside. They are part of how a person acts.

Of course, some causes do bypass or overwhelm our usual capacities. A threat can compel someone. A seizure can produce movements the person does not intend. A severe mental state can alter judgment. A drug can change what feels possible. A child may not understand the consequences of an action in the same way an adult does. These facts matter. Responsibility is not an on-off switch that every human being receives at birth and carries at the same level forever.

The simple question “Could you have done otherwise?” hides several questions. Could you have done otherwise if the circumstances were different? If you had more time, better information, or less fear? If someone had intervened earlier? If you had noticed the pattern before it became a habit? Or do we mean that with the entire past and every physical condition held exactly fixed, the universe could still have produced a different choice? That final question is where philosophical arguments about determinism become especially difficult. It may not be the question that helps us decide what to do tomorrow.

For daily responsibility, it often matters whether a person can understand reasons, learn from consequences, and change their behavior with support or pressure. Those capacities are real, uneven, and responsive to conditions. We can ask what happened, what the person understood, what choices were available, what influence they had, and what would make future harm less likely. We do not need to solve the ultimate metaphysics of causation before setting a boundary or asking for an apology.

Explanation is not an excuse

When someone says, “I did it because I was drunk,” “I learned that behavior at home,” or “I was scared,” they may be offering an explanation. An explanation can help us understand the action. It does not automatically make the action acceptable.

If a person who grew up around violence repeats violence, the history matters. It may help explain how the behavior became familiar, what skills were never learned, or what support could help interrupt the pattern. It does not erase the harm done to the person on the receiving end. Both can be true: this person was shaped by conditions they did not choose, and their actions have consequences for someone else.

The opposite mistake is to treat explanation as irrelevant because we are angry. “They knew what they were doing” may be accurate. It may also hide important differences in capacity, threat, knowledge, or control. Understanding causes does not require taking the side of the person who caused harm. It can help us decide what safety, accountability, and change actually require.

Consider a driver who hits a pedestrian after reading a message. We need to know what happened: Was the driver distracted? Was the phone mounted? Were they speeding? Did a mechanical failure contribute? Was the road designed in a way that made the crossing dangerous? The answers do not all assign the same responsibility. Investigating causes is how we distinguish carelessness from unavoidable accident, identify system failures, and reduce the chance of another injury.

A moral response can include several things at once: stopping the harm, recognizing the person who was hurt, making amends where possible, setting limits, and changing the conditions that made repetition likely. Punishment is only one possible response, and not always the most useful one. Where a person remains dangerous, protection may require firm restrictions. Where the harm was a mistake and the person can learn, repair may matter more than humiliation. These judgments depend on context, not a single slogan about free will.

An explanation becomes an excuse when it is used to demand that the harmed person absorb the cost. “I was under pressure” may be true; it does not mean the person you insulted must comfort you. “I had a hard childhood” can matter deeply; it does not obligate your partner to stay while you repeat what you suffered. We can recognize the causes without pretending the consequences belong only to the past.

Accountability without cosmic blame

Responsibility is often treated as a synonym for blame. But there are several different questions hiding inside the word.

There is causal responsibility: did your action contribute to what happened? There is answerability: can you explain what you did and hear what it did to someone else? There is forward-looking responsibility: what can you do now to repair the harm or reduce the risk of repeating it? There is also blame, which may include anger, condemnation, or the judgment that a person deserves to suffer for what they did.

These can come apart. A person can cause harm without intending it. They may owe an explanation and repair without deserving the same condemnation as someone who planned the harm. A person can be responsible for a mistake but need help, not public humiliation. A person can deserve criticism and still not deserve cruelty. Accountability is not a demand to hate someone enough.

This distinction matters when we turn responsibility inward. “I did something harmful” is a statement about an action. “I am irredeemably bad” is a verdict on an entire person. The first can lead to apology, restitution, and change. The second often leads to hiding, defensiveness, or self-punishment that makes the injured person responsible for reassuring you.

You can take responsibility without pretending you had perfect freedom. You can say, “I was tired and angry, and I still chose to say that. It hurt you. I am sorry. Here is what I will change.” The conditions explain part of the event; your response addresses what remains yours to do now. Responsibility is not the claim that you made yourself from scratch. It is participation in what happens next.

We also need to resist the fantasy that accountability should be painless. An apology may not restore trust. Repair may take time. The person you harmed may not forgive you. That does not make the attempt pointless. It means an apology is not a vending machine where you insert remorse and receive the relationship back.

The point of consequences

If people do not create themselves, why hold them accountable at all? Because consequences are also causes. They change what happens next.

A boundary can prevent an action from being repeated. A fair rule can make harm less likely. A conversation can help someone notice a pattern. A restorative process can give the person harmed a voice and require the person responsible to face the results. A proportionate penalty can protect others or discourage behavior. A sentence of imprisonment can sometimes be necessary to prevent immediate danger, while also carrying serious costs for the person imprisoned, their family, and the community. These responses should be judged by what they do, whom they protect, and whether they treat people justly—not by the emotional pleasure of seeing someone suffer.

If a person’s behavior is caused, then changing the causes and consequences can matter more, not less. We teach children because learning affects behavior. We build laws because predictable consequences shape choices. We provide treatment because some conditions change what a person can control. We separate someone from a situation when they continue to cause harm. None of these practices requires us to believe people are uncaused miracles.

This does not mean consequences are merely tools to manipulate behavior. People are not appliances with a reward button. The person who was harmed matters in their own right, not only as evidence in a social engineering project. Accountability can recognize that a wrong was done and that the injured person deserves protection and repair. The response also influences what becomes possible in the future.

A useful system asks more than “How much pain does this person deserve?” It asks: What happened? Who was harmed? What did the person know and control? What needs to be repaired? What will protect people now? What conditions contributed, and which can be changed? Are we treating similar cases consistently? These are not soft questions. They are harder than reflexively reaching for punishment, because they require us to care about both justice and what follows from it.

There will still be cases where no response can fully repair the harm. Some actions permanently alter a life. Some people refuse to change. Some institutions protect the powerful and punish the vulnerable. A more careful account of responsibility will not make justice easy. It can prevent us from confusing vengeance with repair and certainty with fairness.

You are not the author of your first draft

You are not responsible for everything that shaped you. You did not choose your first draft: the temperament, family rules, inherited fears, early lessons, and historical circumstances that arrived before you had the language to question them. People start with unequal resources and unequal constraints. Calling every difference a choice is a way to make the winners feel that the race was fair.

But a first draft is not the only draft. We can learn new skills, seek help, change routines, tell the truth about our patterns, and build environments that make better actions more likely. None of this happens outside causation. Therapy, practice, friendship, medication, education, and consequences all work by changing what a person experiences and what they can do. A choice shaped by reasons is still a choice. A capacity developed through help is still yours to use.

It is also not equally easy for everyone to revise. “You can change” is true in the broadest sense and cruel when used to ignore disability, poverty, violence, discrimination, or the lack of safe options. Responsibility must include attention to what choices were realistically available and what resources a person had. We can ask people to do what they can while also asking whether their conditions give them a fair chance to do it.

This is not a loophole for doing whatever you want. “I was shaped this way” can be the beginning of honest work or the end of it. The difference is whether the explanation helps you see the next possible action. If you know anger makes you dangerous, you can leave the room, ask for support, or make a plan before the next argument. If you know you avoid difficult conversations, you can practice saying one true sentence. You may not be able to choose what first appears in your mind. You can sometimes choose what you do with it, and you can build conditions that make the next choice easier.

Our capacity to choose can grow through use. It can also shrink under threat, exhaustion, addiction, or isolation. This is one reason responsibility is not only an individual issue. Families, schools, workplaces, and governments shape the options people can see and the consequences they face. Better conditions do not guarantee good behavior. They can make better behavior more possible.

Praise has the same problem

If our history complicates blame, it also complicates credit. A person who succeeds may have worked hard, made wise choices, and persisted through difficulty. They may also have had a safe home, a good teacher, a healthy body, a useful introduction, or a lucky break at exactly the right time. The outcome can reflect both effort and conditions. Recognizing one does not erase the other.

This does not mean we should stop congratulating people. Praise can express gratitude, admiration, and recognition of effort. The mistake is to turn achievement into proof that the winner deserves every advantage and the person who struggled deserves every setback. Success is evidence that someone did something well; it is not a complete moral biography. Failure is evidence that something did not work; it is not a complete moral verdict.

A person may be proud of what they built without claiming they built themselves. Gratitude for help does not cancel accomplishment. It gives the accomplishment a more accurate shape. “I worked hard” and “I had people and circumstances that made the work possible” can fit in the same sentence. This kind of pride is sturdier because it does not depend on pretending that nobody helped.

Make the next choice easier

If behavior is influenced by conditions, then we should pay attention to the conditions around choices. A person trying to stop drinking may need more than a promise to use stronger willpower. They may need to avoid certain settings, find support, change routines, or receive treatment. Someone who keeps lashing out may need to learn how to recognize the moment before anger takes over and to make a plan for stepping away. The specific response depends on the person and the risk. The general idea is simple: do not demand that a difficult choice be made under the hardest possible conditions and then call failure proof of a rotten soul.

This is not the same as arranging life so that no effort is required. Some choices remain hard even when we prepare well. But habits and environments can make some actions easier to begin and others harder to repeat. Put the phone in another room when you need to focus. Make the apology before you construct a courtroom defense. Ask a friend to help you keep an appointment. Build rules that limit opportunities for abuse rather than relying on a powerful person’s good intentions. These are ways of influencing behavior by changing the situation in which it happens.

We often admire the person who can resist temptation in a dramatic moment and ignore the quieter work of not building the entire day around temptation. Yet arranging conditions is itself a form of agency. It is a way of using what we know about our limits to protect what we value. If you know you become cruel when exhausted, sleep is not a moral luxury. If you know isolation makes a problem worse, asking for company can be part of taking responsibility.

A smaller, more useful freedom

Maybe the free will we want is impossible: a power to have made ourselves, from nothing, into exactly the people we are. If that is the standard, nobody qualifies. The standard is less a description of freedom than a demand that a person be their own creator, which would require them to exist before they existed. The metaphysical paperwork is a mess.

There is another kind of freedom worth protecting: the ability to act from reasons, to reconsider, to learn, to resist pressure, and to participate in shaping the conditions of future action. It is not absolute. It comes in degrees, and it depends on bodies, relationships, resources, and institutions. But degrees matter. A locked door is not the same as an open one because neither offers infinite possibility.

We can hold people accountable without pretending they are self-made. We can recognize that a person’s past shaped their behavior while still asking what they will do now. We can protect people from harm without believing that suffering is the only language someone understands. We can condemn an action without declaring the person beyond change. We can take responsibility for our part without turning regret into a life sentence.

You did not choose the conditions that formed you. You did not choose every impulse, fear, or thought that appears. But you are not merely watching a stranger operate your body. You are the living process through which memory, attention, reasons, habits, and relationships meet the present. Sometimes you can pause. Sometimes you cannot. Sometimes you need another person or a safer environment before pausing becomes possible.

Responsibility begins there—not with the fantasy that you authored yourself, but with the recognition that your actions affect other people and that what happens next can still be influenced. That is less grand than absolute free will. It is also enough to make an apology meaningful, a boundary necessary, a habit change possible, and an unjust system worth challenging.

The next chapter turns to the discomfort we try so hard to avoid. If we are shaped by our histories and still able to revise some part of what comes next, then the question is not simply how to feel good. It is what we are willing to feel, tolerate, and work through in order to live as the people we want to become.
