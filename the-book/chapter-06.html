Chapter 06Free Will, Responsibility, and Other Uncomfortable Questions

At ten at night you put the alarm clock on the far side of the bedroom. You know a bastard who’d turn it off without waking up properly, and in nine hours you expect to be him. At seven you have to get out of bed. Halfway across the cold floor it occurs to you that somebody did this to you on purpose. In both versions of events, you’re right.

The clock cost nine dollars at the drugstore. It has two bells on top with a little hammer between them, and it’s in your life because the phone kept letting you off. A phone sleeps within reach. It’ll sell you nine more minutes as many times as you ask, like a bartender who’s decided your liver is your own business. So the phone stays on the nightstand and the clock goes on the shelf by the door, eight feet from the pillow, where the only way to shut it up is to be standing.

Look at what the plan assumes. It only works if you’re right about what a weasel you’ll be in the morning. Nobody sets a trap for a person they trust. At ten you want to run four miles, because there’s a 10K in April and last year you walked the hill. At seven you’ll want the comforter, and you’ll want it with a sincerity the evening you never brings to anything. The two of you are on opposite sides of an argument. You have the same Social Security number.

People like to say the one at ten is the real you, since that’s the one with the plans. The one at seven has the legs. Ask either of them who’s in charge and you’ll get a confident answer and a different name.

The clock is the smaller of tonight’s two problems. For three weeks, at about this hour, you’ve decided to call your sister in the morning. For three weeks the morning person has turned that off without waking up properly.

What you said to the green beans

Three Sundays ago your father was in one of his moods. Nobody had done anything. With him the mood arrives first and picks out its reasons once it’s had a look around the table. By the time the roast came out he’d covered the gravy, where your sister had parked, and the fact that nobody in this family comes by anymore unless there’s a meal in it. Your brother wasn’t there. He has a gift for that. Your father got up for the salt, and while his back was turned you said, to your green beans, “And he wonders why nobody visits.”

You’d picked the volume with care. It was loud enough to have said it and quiet enough not to have. He heard it anyway. He turned around with the salt in his hand and looked straight at your sister.

She’s two years older than you and she’s had the job since grade school. In your family she’s the one who says things. You’re the easy one, a reputation you’ve kept up for thirty years by the simple method of sitting next to her. Your father didn’t need to ask whose remark it was. He’s been getting remarks from that chair since before you could cut your own meat.

“You don’t like it here,” he told her, “there’s the door.”

She looked at you. It lasted about four seconds. You know because you spent them cutting a green bean in half the long way, which takes concentration. Then she picked up her keys and used the door. Your mother wrapped a plate nobody took home.

The text came from the driveway. One word. “Really?” You once spent an entire evening working out what a friend meant by one word and a period. This one you understood before the screen dimmed, and you’ve spent three weeks handling it like a message in Portuguese.

Since then your father has let it be known that she’s welcome back when she apologizes. She’s told your mother she won’t apologize for something she didn’t say. She hasn’t told your mother who did say it, and you’ve been careful not to wonder why. You’ve been to dinner twice. Both Sundays he gave you the end piece of the roast, which has been hers since she could reach the platter. You ate it. It was very good. The second Sunday he remarked that at least one of his kids knew how to act at a table, and you asked him to pass the potatoes. Thanksgiving is in nineteen days, and your mother has started asking you, of all people, whether your sister is coming.

It just came out

You have an explanation. It’s had three weeks of work and it’s a good one.

It starts with the sentence itself. You didn’t decide to say it. One second it was a thought you’d have denied having, and the next it was in the air over the potatoes in your voice. If somebody had stopped dinner to ask whether you wished to make a remark, you’d have said no and meant it.

A famous experiment seems to be on your side. In the early 1980s the physiologist Benjamin Libet put electrodes on volunteers’ scalps and sat them in front of a clock face with a spot of light racing around it. Their job was to flick a wrist whenever they felt like it, and afterward to say where the spot had been when they first felt the urge. Libet’s team averaged the recordings from dozens of flicks. The brain’s slow electrical buildup toward the movement began, on average, a little over half a second before the wrist moved. The urge people reported came about a fifth of a second before. For roughly a third of a second the brain seemed to be getting on with it before anybody upstairs had been told. [1]

Twenty-five years later Chun Siong Soon and his colleagues put people in a brain scanner with a button under each index finger and let them press whichever they liked, whenever they liked. From patterns in the scans the team could guess left or right up to seven seconds before the person reported deciding. The guesses were right about six times in ten, in a task where a coin gets five. [2] Worth investigating, certainly. As an oracle, this one still needs a day job.

So there’s your first line. It just came out, and science says things do.

Science kept going, though. In 2012 Aaron Schurger and his colleagues offered another reading of Libet’s buildup. Suppose the background activity in a brain drifts up and down all the time, and when nothing tells you when to move, you move when the drift happens to cross a line. Collect the crossings, line them up and average backward, and you get a smooth rise leading into each one, though no single flick had a rise like it. On that account the famous signal is what noise looks like after you’ve averaged it, and nobody decided anything behind your back. It’s one possible account, and the argument isn’t over. [3]

Then Uri Maoz and his colleagues asked what happens when a choice matters. Their volunteers picked between pairs of nonprofits. In some rounds the choice counted toward a real thousand-dollar donation. In others it was arbitrary, and each nonprofit got the same five hundred dollars whatever the finger did. Both kinds of round ended with the same button press. The buildup appeared before the arbitrary presses. Before the presses that moved money, in that experiment, the team didn’t find it. [5] Every one of these studies timed a finger, a wrist or a button against a report that’s hard to give accurately, and none of them tested a sentence said to a father.

Give yourself the sentence anyway. Say it left before you were consulted. It took about a second and a half.

Then came four seconds in which your sister looked at you and you performed surgery on a bean. Libet himself thought a person kept a last tenth or two of a second in which to call a movement off. [6] You had four full seconds and a knife. After that came the drive home, the text, and twenty-one evenings on which a rested adult with a phone considered the matter and left it for the morning. “It just came out” covers a second and a half. The rest took planning.

Nobody asked you

The second line of your explanation is bigger, and most of it is true. You didn’t pick that father. You didn’t pick a house where the safest thing to be at dinner was quiet, or a family that had handed out the parts before you could spell them. Somebody was going to be the mouth and somebody was going to be the easy one, and nobody asked which you’d like. You were about eight when you worked out that if you kept your eyes on your plate, the weather went over your head and came down on her. Any kid would’ve taken that deal. Most of them wouldn’t still be collecting on it in their thirties.

People who’d hold you responsible anyway tend to picture a small manager behind your eyes, somebody untouched by genes, sleep or your father, who reviews each impulse and could always have picked another. Ask who hired the manager. If the answer is a smaller manager, ask again. A choice made for reasons has a history. Take away every influence on the chooser, the upbringing and the fear and the taste for end pieces, and you haven’t set anybody free. You’ve left them nothing to choose with.

So take the hard version of your argument at full strength. Three Sundays ago, with that childhood behind you and that father in front of you and exactly that brain in your head, maybe nothing else was ever going to happen at 6:40. Philosophers have fought for centuries over whether responsibility survives that, and no experiment with a wrist has ended the fight. I can’t end it either. What I can do is notice what the argument proves if it’s right. It proves it for the whole table. Your father didn’t pick his father. Your sister never asked to be the mouth. If everybody that night could only do what they did, that covers the four seconds, the door and the end piece, and she’s still the one eating somewhere else on Sundays. The argument can take away the idea that you deserve to suffer for it. It can’t lift the cost off her, and it has no opinion about who’s going to.

You have suffered for it, a little. You’ve felt sick about that Sunday for three weeks and you’ve been counting the feeling as a kind of payment. None of it reached her.

It helps to pull apart two things that sound alike, being caused and being stuck. In the winter of 1961 the meteorologist Edward Lorenz was running a small weather model on an early computer. He wanted a second look at one stretch of it, so he typed in the numbers from the middle of an old printout, started the machine and went for coffee. The printout showed three decimal places. The computer had been carrying six. He’d typed .506 where the machine had .506127. When he came back, the new weather had peeled away from the old until the two had nothing to do with each other. [7] Nothing in that machine was free. Twelve numbers followed fixed rules, and a difference in the fourth decimal place ended up deciding where the storms went. Two years later he published a version stripped down to three equations that did the same thing. [4]

In a world where everything has causes, the small things are causes too, and “it would’ve happened anyway” is the one claim you don’t get to make. Chance wouldn’t rescue you either. Decide whether to speak up by flipping a coin and you’d be harder to predict and no more in charge than the coin. Lorenz only had a toy atmosphere, and nothing in it says what anybody owes a sister. All it takes from you is the picture of causes as a river carrying you past your own evening. You were one of the numbers at that table. Tonight, so is a clock eight feet from a pillow.

Shares

Finding more causes doesn’t thin out the first one. A delivery driver looks down at a message and hits a man in a crosswalk. His employer docks shifts for slow replies. The city painted the crosswalk where parked vans block the view. All three are true, and the man is in the hospital regardless. The employer can change the rule and the city can move the paint, and the driver still has to walk into that room and say who he is. Shares of a thing like that don’t have to add up to a hundred.

Your father has a share of that Sunday. He brought the mood, and he looked at her without checking, because checking might have meant finding out it was the easy one. That part is his, and you can’t make him pick it up. Yours didn’t shrink when his arrived. The word responsible covers three different jobs here. One is having helped cause it. One is owing her the truth. One is being in a position to do something about Thanksgiving. Your father qualifies for the first. You qualify for all three.

You could tell her which parts of your childhood made it hard to speak. They belong in the story, especially if you’d like next Thanksgiving to go differently. The trouble starts when you hand them to her as the reason she should go on carrying your end. She’d be paying for the remark and then paying again to hear how hard it was to make.

Credit works the same way, and you’ll notice nobody objects. Last April the woman who won your age group stood on a plywood box with her arms in the air. Next to the box stood the neighbor who’d had her kid every Saturday morning since January, and a volunteer who’d unlocked the restrooms at five and was now asleep on his feet. She ran the miles and she should keep the medal. Still, nobody on a podium has ever stopped the ceremony to explain that her lungs were mostly inherited. People tend to find determinism about when the blame shows up.

None of this makes you as free as the manager story wanted. It leaves you free the way people are, by degrees. Suppose somebody locks your bedroom door from the outside tonight. At seven you’ll have every reason to run that you had yesterday, and you won’t be going anywhere. Any account of freedom that sees no difference between that morning and this one has stopped describing anything you’d recognize. Fear narrows a door. So does exhaustion. Somebody getting home from a night shift at seven to lift a parent out of bed has less door than you do, and no clock will widen it. A father holding the salt narrowed yours plenty when you were eight. You aren’t eight. Three weeks after the fact, with your own apartment and your own car keys, your door is about as open as doors get. A locked door and an open one aren’t the same just because neither offers infinite possibility.

Ten o’clock

Which brings you back to the shelf. The clock is the one honest thing you’ve done about seven a.m. It doesn’t ask the morning person to be better. It assumes the worst and moves the furniture. You didn’t pick the conditions that made the person at that table. Tonight you’re one of the conditions for the person who wakes up at seven. Moving the clock took about four seconds. Nobody applauded. If there’s a victory in the morning it began the night before, while the future hero was in a bathrobe picking something out of their teeth.

So at ten you do to the call what you did to the clock. You pick up the phone and type, “Can I call you tomorrow at eight?” and send it before the evening can turn into another one.

At 10:06 she answers. “About what.”

No question mark. You type “About dinner,” delete it, and type “About what I said at dinner.” It took you three weeks to get to that pronoun. You send it. Nothing comes back, and the phone goes up on the shelf beside the clock. Tomorrow’s bastard can sleep through a lot. An appointment with a person who’s already been told what it’s about is harder to switch off.

At seven the clock goes off like a fire in a silverware drawer. You cross the cold floor hating whoever did this to you, and through the wall your roommate hates them too. By the time you’ve reached the shelf you’re upright, which was the whole of the evening person’s plan, and it’s enough. You run the four miles, badly. On the hill you think of several good reasons to call her at lunch instead, and you notice whose voice they’re in.

At eight you’re standing in your hallway in wet shoes. She picks up on the fifth ring and doesn’t say hello. You can hear her looking for her keys.

You tell her it was you. You tell her you let him think it was her, and that you’ve had two end pieces since. Then, because it’s right there and it took three weeks to build, you start in on Dad’s mood, and how it was in that house.

“I was in that house,” she says. “Same table, same father. I don’t need how you got like this. I looked right at you. Tell me about that.”

Everything you prepared is true, and none of it answers her. So you say the part that does. You saw her look. You knew what she was waiting for. You liked being the easy one more than you liked her having a father that week. You say you’ll call him today, and that she doesn’t have to do anything.

“Call me after,” she says. “Not before.”

Then she stops looking for her keys. She starts with the driveway, and how long she sat there with the engine off in case somebody came out, and she goes on from there. You stand in the hallway in your wet shoes without reaching for a single explanation, and it feels exactly as bad as you went to such trouble to avoid.
