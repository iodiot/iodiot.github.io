Chapter 20Einstein Didn’t Owe You a Life Lesson

Albert Einstein has been present at the meeting for eleven minutes when Bev asks if he’d like to take a turn locking up.

He’s on a folded proof of the poster, propped at the end of the table opposite the Oreos. A great quantity of hair and a mustache, recognizable even in a drawing by a woman who has met neither the man nor, apparently, a human ear. Under the portrait Fran has put six words. Imagination is more important than knowledge. The quote looked good in the white space. Then she added his name, and somehow he’s ended up chairing the meeting. Howard, the treasurer, slides the sign-in pencil across to him. Anybody in this building after five signs the sheet.

It’s a Thursday in the middle of January. On the first of the month the city’s recreation department began closing the community center at five, as its notice had promised. The attendance figures that went downtown “for review” are still being reviewed. The six Tuesdays of the practice room ended with the year. On the seventh, Leon took two buses with a trombone and found the place dark. Bev is down twelve hours a week.

The department has left one door open. The building can stay open after five for any group that covers the cost of a two-hour evening, staffing and running costs together. Tonight’s two hours are on Fran’s debit card. The meeting about whether to pay the city for evenings is being held on an evening somebody paid the city for.

Six evenings at $36 each come to $216, and the music fund has $96. The other $120 has to come from the people who use the room or from the film group’s coffee can. Say yes, and the lights stay on every other Tuesday until the end of March. Say no, and the review downtown has nothing left to review. Nobody at the table has ever heard of a budget line coming back from that.

Seven people get a vote. Fran, Howard and Denise, who captions the films, keep the coffee can. Four used the little room in the fall. Tess, with her clarinet case across her knees. Leon, with his trombone. Kit’s mother, voting for an eleven-year-old who can’t. And the father who practiced his wedding speech up there, who has no speech left to practice and came anyway. Bev has no vote. She has the keys.

Fran made the poster to get people to this meeting and to the Tuesdays after it. Call it Evening Access Cost-Recovery Proposal and you’ll get the attendance that name deserves. She wanted somebody to vouch for the idea that trying things has a value. Einstein had a line on the subject and made no demands on the budget.

“Did he even say it?” asks Howard.

“Yes, actually,” says Fran. She has brought the pages. This is among the happiest moments available to a person at a meeting. Somebody has asked the exact question you did the homework for. She takes out the printout and lets herself enjoy the rustle.

The line appears in an interview by George Sylvester Viereck in The Saturday Evening Post of October 26, 1929. Einstein has been talking about the confidence he felt in a prediction tested at a solar eclipse ten years earlier. Viereck asks how he weighs imagination against knowledge, and the famous sentence is in the reply.[1] The man who wrote it down later worked as a propagandist for Hitler’s Germany and served nearly four years in a federal prison over it.[2] The quotation is genuine anyway. Provenance is like that.

There. Genuine Einstein. A little silence. Somebody chooses a cookie.

Bev says, “Which part says who pays for the room?”

An attribution can be completely right and you can still be using the man as a hand puppet. With a fake quote, Fran could take the poster down, put the coffee on and enjoy denouncing the state of public education. As things stand she has to work out what the genuine article entitles her to.

What six words will cover

Be fair to her first. Last fall the little room was where spare chairs went for an extended convalescence. Somebody had to picture a working adult from a small apartment using it, and the people downtown asking for numbers would have no numbers if Fran hadn’t first imagined the door open. That should count. It’s her point, though. One of the less dignified features of being alive is how much better your own reasonable point sounds when a famous dead stranger makes it.

And the six words stop short of the cash register. Imagination found a use for the room. Therefore what? Maybe everybody who plays should pay at the door. Maybe nobody should. Maybe Bev should work six evenings for nothing and use her imagination to put something hot on her own table. The quotation is happy with all three. In the interview Einstein even makes room for the difference between feeling sure he’s right and knowing it. The poster has relieved him of the question, the example and a fair amount of modesty. Six genuine words will fit on a mug. It’s murder getting the surrounding conversation through the handle.

Kit’s mother would pay at the door without blinking. Kit is beside her near the cookies, wanting another crack at the bar his left hand still treats as a suggestion. What she needs is a booking she can count on, and if she and Kit’s dad split the driving, she might sometimes be home for the little one’s bedtime.

Leon knows a boy who’ll stop coming the day a charge goes on the door. Asking him to explain why he can’t find five bucks, in front of people he’ll be standing behind at the supermarket for the rest of the year, costs a good deal more than five bucks. Leon gave up his car when the insurance passed what the car was worth. He knows how those conversations go. He’ll put in what he can himself. He won’t have it imposed on the boy.

For a minute they’re making different claims about what the same room is for. A booking you can count on. A place you can use with whatever you have left on Tuesday. And from the chair by the door, a paid shift. Einstein wears the composure of a man who doesn’t have to choose.

“We won’t know if it works until we try it,” Fran says, and glances at the poster the way you’d glance at a friend who’s supposed to back you up.

Howard has been holding the hard question since the cookies came out. “We pay for it once and that’s what an evening costs from now on. Why would they ever fund it again?” If the Tuesdays fill, the department learns that this neighborhood will light its own building. If they don’t, it gets a second set of thin numbers, and the people at this table have spent their own money buying proof for the other side.

What it took to earn the haircut

Fran is proposing a test. Howard is worried about who reads the result. The eclipse Einstein mentioned to Viereck is a story about both.

General relativity predicted that the Sun would bend starlight passing close to it, so that a star seen near the Sun’s edge would appear out of place by about 1.75 arcseconds, a little less than the width of a centimeter seen from a kilometer away. For the eclipse of May 29, 1919, two expeditions went out, one to Sobral in Brazil and one to the island of Príncipe off the west coast of Africa, to catch that shift on glass plates in the few minutes the Moon cut the glare. One series of Sobral plates was trouble. Its star images had lost their sharpness, and taken at face value it gave about 0.93 arcseconds, close to the half-size bending that Newtonian reasoning gives. Frank Dyson, Arthur Eddington and Charles Davidson put the trouble down to focus, said so in print and gave the other results much greater weight.[3] They published the plates that would have made an awful poster.

Try settling that with a famous name. Plate says one thing, Einstein says another. Does the plate have tenure? A photograph is valuable because it doesn’t owe the theorist a result. It doesn’t interpret itself either. People who knew telescopes had to do work that other people could check, awkward plates included. Einstein’s reputation was an excellent reason to mount a hard test. It gave nobody permission to write his number on the box before the plates came home.

Just as well, because five years earlier the number on the box was wrong. Einstein’s 1911 calculation gave the half-size bending. In the summer of 1914 a Berlin astronomer, Erwin Freundlich, took instruments to the Crimea to test it at the eclipse of August 21, with money Einstein had helped find. The war got there first. Russia interned Freundlich and impounded the equipment, and the weather was too poor for useful plates anyway.[4] By late 1915 Einstein had doubled his figure and gotten it right. Good plates from 1914 would have caught him with the wrong number, years before his hair was famous. Probably. Plates aren’t always good, as Sobral showed. Either way, his reputation was rescued by a world war and a cloudy sky. Nobody recommends the method.

So Howard is half right. Six Tuesdays are a set of plates, and the department will read them, maybe with its mind already made up. What Fran can do is what the astronomers did. Count everything, the thin nights included, and say in writing how she handled them. That can’t make the department fair. It can make an unfair reading show.

On starlight in 1919, the people placed to know were the ones with the plates. In this room it’s Bev. Two hours of public use mean somebody on duty who knows what to do when the front door sticks or the upstairs lights trip. If it’s her shift, she wants to be paid for it. She’s willing to say so in front of Einstein, who has had the advantage of remaining completely affordable since April 1955. Even his brain came free. The pathologist on call at the autopsy took it out without the family’s permission and kept the pieces in jars for decades, in case the genius showed up under a microscope.[5] Bev would like to be paid while she’s still using hers.

Denise would like that in the minutes. She has captioned this group’s films for years and been paid in compliments that came close to insults. “Free is how it starts,” she says. “Then it’s just how it is.”

Einstein did write about who gets what, in a 1949 essay called “Why Socialism?” One of the most famous men alive opened it by asking whether somebody who wasn’t an expert on economic and social questions had any business discussing them.[6]

So why do we want the cleverest dead man available to rule on how to live? One reason is kind. The world is full of terrible advice, and somebody who demonstrably thought for himself, stayed curious and survived being wrong in public is decent company while you think. The other reason is the one in this room. Fran is about to ask her neighbors to bet a custodian’s hours and a coffee can on her idea, and she’d prefer not to be the only name behind it. Put a Nobel laureate’s name under it and anybody who follows it into a disaster will need a time machine and good German to complain. She wasn’t after his company. She was hiring him as a bouncer, to stop anybody quarreling with her.

Tess hasn’t said a word all evening. “I looked after my mother for years,” she says. “Nobody ever came back and said, good news, we found the money, you can stop.” She does payroll. She has seen what happens to a line on a sheet once somebody shows the work gets done without it.

Fran asks Bev what she wants.

“The hours,” Bev says. “Don’t ask me if it’s smart.” Six evenings of two hours make twelve hours between now and the end of March. She used to get twelve in a week. From her chair by the door she watches seven people decide what her Tuesdays are worth.

Fran votes yes. So does Leon, who says he’d have liked a better thing to say yes to. So does Kit’s mother. So does the father with the speech. Howard votes no. Denise votes no.

Tess votes no, against her own half hour.

Four to three. Howard takes back the pencil and looks at the portrait. Einstein has nothing to add.

So the lights stay on for one more quarter, every other Tuesday from six to eight until the end of March. The ones who can pay put money in the coffee can before they leave, and the slots are free to whoever asks. Six dates Bev can write on her calendar, in pencil. Nothing beyond that has been secured.

Who signs the notice

A small decision honestly recorded is an uncomfortable thing to own. No discovery is coming that will tell Fran whether she weighed the boy at the door correctly against Bev’s twelve hours, or against Howard’s question, which nobody answered. The problem has numbers in it. That doesn’t make it addition.

After the sixth Tuesday she’ll have the bookings, the money taken, the slots that sat empty and Bev’s record of what it took to run the room. The people who never heard about it won’t be in her figures. All of it goes downtown, where six paid evenings can be read as demand or as a bill somebody else has agreed to pick up. The file down there already holds a numbered repair slip from the fall, on which the words probable structural defect have a line through them and are easy to read.

Kit is waiting by the door with his coat on. One of the center’s music stands has a loose foot, and when he sets it where he wants it the top tilts until the music starts to slide. Fran has just taped the Einstein poster to the wall by the door. She tears a strip from the blank margin at the bottom. Kit folds it twice and wedges it under the foot. The music stays up. It’s the distinguished visitor’s first demonstrable contribution to the plan.

Coming back from stacking the chairs, Fran finds Leon reading the list of dates. He runs his finger down it twice and puts them in his phone. This is the thing she has been trying all evening to file as a small administrative misunderstanding. Her notice in the fall said music practice every Tuesday. It gave no last date. Leon came on the seventh Tuesday because, as far as her own words went, it was as much a Tuesday as the first six.

She goes over to tell him. She starts with “We weren’t clear” and hears how many people have just joined her in that sentence.

“I left the last date off the notice,” she says. “I’m sorry. You came here because of what I wrote.”

“Yes,” he says. “I thought it was me. I stood there pushing the handle up the way she showed us.”

She hadn’t pictured that. She’d pictured bad weather, wasted fares and a man annoyed with the people who run the place. She hadn’t pictured him at a dark door with a trombone case, lifting the handle again in case he’d done it wrong, wondering what simple thing everybody else here knew how to do. Nobody at tonight’s table could have told her that, Einstein included.

She offers to cover his fares. He’ll take it, he says, and he’d like the number of somebody to call if it happens again. She writes her own under the six dates. They both read the corrected notice, this time to the bottom. He takes two copies for the drugstore.

Fran stays another minute. The new booking sheet has a line for the person responsible, and if the city reads six paid Tuesdays as proof that it never has to pay for another, that’s the line it will read. She spent a good part of the evening trying to borrow a name impressive enough to go there. She signs her own, in her sloping hand.
