Chapter 20Einstein Didn’t Owe You a Life Lesson

Albert Einstein has been present at the meeting for eleven minutes when Bev asks if he’d like to take a turn locking up.

He’s on a folded proof of the poster, propped at the end of the table opposite the Oreos. A great quantity of hair and a mustache, recognizable even in a drawing by a woman who has met neither the man nor, apparently, a human ear. Under the portrait Fran has put six words. Imagination is more important than knowledge. The quote looked good in the white space. Then she added his name, and somehow he’s ended up chairing the meeting. Howard, the treasurer, slides the sign-in pencil across to him. Anybody in this building after five signs the sheet.

It’s after five, and that takes some explaining. On January 1 the city’s recreation department began closing the community center at five, as its notice had promised. The attendance figures that went downtown “for review” are still being reviewed. The six Tuesdays of the practice room ended with the year. On the seventh, Leon took two buses with a trombone and found the place dark. Bev is down twelve hours a week.

The department has left one door open, by way of the center’s manager. The building can stay open after five for any group that covers the cost, $36 for a two-hour evening, staffing and running costs together. Tonight’s two hours are on Fran’s debit card. The meeting about whether to pay the city for evenings is being held on an evening somebody paid the city for.

So seven people with a vote have to decide whether the people who use this building should pay for what the city has stopped paying for. Say yes, and evenings last until the end of March, every other Tuesday. Say no, and the review downtown has nothing left to review. Nobody at the table has ever heard of a budget line coming back from that.

Fran made the poster to get people to this meeting and to the Tuesdays after it. Call it Evening Access Cost-Recovery Proposal and you’ll get the attendance that name deserves. The fall brought in a woman with a clarinet, a boy at a piano that came with its own cutlery section, a trombonist too loud for any apartment and a father who needed a few tries at a wedding speech. Fran wanted somebody to vouch for the idea that trying things has a value. Einstein had a line on the subject and made no demands on the budget.

“Did he even say it?” asks Howard.

“Yes, actually,” says Fran. She has brought the pages. This is among the happiest moments available to a person at a meeting. Somebody has asked the exact question you did the homework for. She takes out the printout and lets herself enjoy the rustle.

The line appears in an interview by George Sylvester Viereck in The Saturday Evening Post of October 26, 1929. Viereck asks about intuition and inspiration in Einstein’s discoveries. Einstein talks about the confidence he’d felt in the prediction tested at the solar eclipse ten years earlier. Viereck then asks how he weighs imagination against knowledge, and the famous sentence is in the reply. You can go and see the question on the page. He said it, insofar as a magazine interview can tell us what somebody said in a conversation nearly a century ago.[1] The man who wrote it down later worked as a propagandist for Hitler’s Germany and served nearly four years in a federal prison over it.[2] The quotation is genuine anyway. Provenance is like that.

There. Genuine Einstein. A little silence. Somebody chooses a cookie.

Bev says, “Which part says who pays for the room?”

An attribution can be completely right and you can still be using the man as a hand puppet. That’s a more interesting problem than an internet meme with a spelling mistake. With a fake quote, Fran could take the poster down, put the coffee on and enjoy denouncing the state of public education. As things stand she has to work out what the genuine article entitles her to.

What six words will cover

Be fair to her first. Imagination has been useful. Last fall the little room was where the center put people when the big room was taken, and where spare chairs went for an extended convalescence. Somebody had to picture a working adult from a small apartment using it. The people now asking for firm numbers would have no numbers if Fran hadn’t first imagined the door open. She wants that to count in the discussion, and it should. It’s her point, though. One of the less dignified features of being alive is how much better your own reasonable point sounds when a famous dead stranger makes it.

Now follow the implied argument all the way. Einstein thought imagination important to discovery. Imagination found a use for the room. Therefore what? Maybe four people a night should pay five dollars. Maybe nobody should. Maybe Bev should work six evenings for nothing and use her imagination to put something hot on her own table. The quotation is happy with all three. You can steer it right up to the cash register, and then a human being with a wallet has to take over.

Try folding back his name and leaving the sentence. Is imagination always more important than knowledge? Picture the surgeon taking out your appendix. While she’s puzzling over an unexpected pool of blood, you want an imagination that can generate alternatives. While she’s deciding which bit is your bowel, you’d like her to know. You’d like the same woman to do both jobs and to notice when she has moved from one to the other.

In the interview Einstein himself makes room for the distinction between his confidence that he’s right and his knowing that he is. The sentence on the poster has been relieved of a question, an example and a fair amount of modesty. Six genuine words will fit on a mug. It’s murder getting the surrounding conversation through the handle.

Choosing Einstein has a commercial genius to it. Draw the hair and most people know who you mean. Try it with the face of the man who keeps the film group’s coffee can. Einstein’s face packs decades of work into an area the size of a Ritz cracker. Set it above your own opinion and you’ve borrowed a whole scientific career in under a second. No physics required.

I suspect the service he’s really asked to provide is cover. If I put my own name under a sentence about taking risks and you follow it into an expensive disaster, we’re going to have an uncomfortable conversation. Put a Nobel laureate’s name there and you’ll need a time machine and good German to escalate your complaint.

What it took to earn the haircut

The eclipse in that interview shows the difference between a name worth listening to and a name entitled to end an argument. General relativity predicted that the Sun would bend starlight passing close to it, so that a star seen near the Sun’s edge would appear out of place by about 1.75 arcseconds. An arcsecond is an angle, a 3,600th of a degree, and a centimeter seen from a kilometer away spans about two of them. For the eclipse of May 29, 1919, two expeditions went out, one to Sobral in Brazil and one to the island of Príncipe off the west coast of Africa, to catch that shift on glass plates in the few minutes the Moon cut the glare, with clouds considering the matter.[3]

Frank Dyson, Arthur Eddington and Charles Davidson reported results from the smaller Sobral telescope and from Príncipe that landed on either side of Einstein’s figure. A second series of Sobral plates was trouble. Its star images had lost their sharpness, and taken at face value it gave about 0.93 arcseconds, close to the half-size bending that Newtonian reasoning gives. The authors put the trouble down to focus, said so in print and gave the other two results much greater weight.[3] They published the plates that would have made an awful poster.

Try doing that with a famous name alone. Plate says one thing, Einstein says another. Does the plate have tenure? A photograph is valuable because it doesn’t owe the theorist a result. It doesn’t interpret itself either. People who knew telescopes had to do work that other people could check, awkward plates included. Einstein’s reputation was an excellent reason to mount a hard test. It gave nobody permission to write his number on the box before the plates came home.

Just as well, because five years earlier the number on the box was wrong. Einstein’s 1911 calculation gave the half-size bending. In the summer of 1914 a Berlin astronomer, Erwin Freundlich, took instruments to the Crimea to test it at the eclipse of August 21, with money Einstein had helped find. The war got there first. Russia interned Freundlich and impounded the equipment, and the weather was too poor for useful plates anyway.[4] By late 1915 Einstein had doubled his figure and gotten it right. Good plates from 1914 would have caught the most famous mind in physics with the wrong number. Probably. Plates aren’t always good, as Sobral showed. Either way, his reputation was rescued by a world war and a cloudy sky. Nobody recommends the method.

Distrust can get as silly as worship. A man can read about the bad plates at Sobral, close the tab before the authors deal with them and decide he’s brought down relativity while waiting for a sandwich. The astronomers lived with the awkward measurement and showed how they’d handled it.

Most of what any of us knows comes on somebody else’s say-so. The useful question is which part of the problem this particular person is placed to know. On starlight in 1919 it was the people with the plates. In this room it’s Bev. Two hours of public use mean somebody on duty for the whole two hours. Somebody has to know what to do when the front door sticks or the upstairs lights trip. And if Bev is working the shift, she wants to be paid for it. She’s willing to say so in front of Einstein, who has had the advantage of remaining completely affordable since April 1955. Even his brain came free. The pathologist on call at the autopsy took it out without the family’s permission and kept the pieces in jars for decades, in case the genius showed up under a microscope.[5] Bev would like to be paid while she’s still using hers.

An opinion from somebody who’ll have to pay

Kit’s mother is sitting near the cookies with Kit, who can now play his piece almost to the end and wants another crack at the bar his left hand still treats as a suggestion. She’d pay five dollars for a half hour without blinking. What she needs is a booking she can count on. She and Kit’s dad could split the cost, and if they split the driving too she might sometimes be home for the little one’s bedtime.

Leon, across the table, knows a boy who’ll stop coming the day a five-dollar charge goes on the door. He also thinks that asking anybody to explain why he can’t find five bucks, to people he’ll be standing behind at the supermarket for the rest of the year, prices the cheap seats a good deal higher than five dollars. Leon gave up his car when the insurance passed what the car was worth. He knows how those conversations go.

For a minute they’re making different claims about what the same room is for. A reliable place to practice. A place you can use whatever you have left on Tuesday. A paid shift. Einstein wears the composure of a man who doesn’t have to choose.

Would it help to know what he thought about who gets what? He left a forceful account. In the first issue of Monthly Review, in May 1949, he published an essay called “Why Socialism?” He opens by asking whether somebody who isn’t an expert on economic and social questions has any business discussing them. One of the most famous men alive starts by checking his own credentials. He doesn’t assume that physics qualifies him. He tries to explain why such questions concern people beyond the professionals. He separates the means science can help us understand from the ends a society chooses to serve, and he names a danger in the system he favors, the power a bureaucracy can gather.[6]

You can disagree with every line. The argument survives the haircut coming off. It’s made of connected sentences with assumptions, history and ethical commitments in them, and a reader can dispute each one. Reading the essay grants him something the poster takes away. He gets to be an adult with opinions he can be asked to defend.

The opposite mistake is waiting right behind that one. Suppose people may speak only where they hold the superior credentials. It sounds fine when a quarterback wades into planetary physics. Carry it across the street and the mother of a child in a wheelchair gets shown out of the planning meeting for lacking an architecture degree. She’s the only person present who knows what the proposed entrance is like in the rain, with one hand on the chair and the other reaching for a button mounted behind the hinge. She can’t calculate the load on the lintel. She can tell you where they put the damn switch.

So why do we want the cleverest dead man available to rule on how to live? One reason is kind. The world is full of terrible advice, and somebody who demonstrably thought for himself, stayed curious and survived being wrong in public is decent company while you think. Read a whole letter of his. Quarrel with him for three pages until you see why he chose as he did. The other reason is the one in this room. Fran is about to ask her neighbors to bet a custodian’s hours and a coffee can on her idea, and she’d prefer not to be the only name behind it. She wasn’t after his company. She was hiring him as a bouncer, to stop anybody quarreling with her.

Then Howard reads out the numbers, and they’re short enough to hold in your head. Six evenings at $36 come to $216. The center’s music fund has $96 in it. That leaves $120, which is twenty-four half-hour slots at five dollars apiece, provided the fee keeps nobody away.

Leon asks whether he can buy a supporter slot at ten dollars. He can afford ten. He just won’t have it imposed on the boy. He takes one. Kit’s mom takes two, the father with the speech takes two and Fran puts herself down for three. Bev proposes counting money once it’s in the cash box. Eight slots, eighty dollars, paid. That leaves $40, which Howard could take out of the film group’s coffee can. The other sixteen slots would go at whatever a player can manage, nothing included, no reason asked. Leon would still prefer a flat charge with a fixed number of free spots. Pay-what-you-can slots get taken by people who could pay, he says, and the boy he knows will be the last to call.

The money was never the hard part. Howard says what is. “We pay for it once and that’s what an evening costs from now on. Why would they ever fund it again?” If the Tuesdays fill, the department learns that this neighborhood will light its own building. If they don’t fill, the department gets a second set of thin numbers, the music fund is empty, the can is down forty dollars and the supporters have spent eighty buying proof for the other side.

A no costs sooner and lands on fewer people. Leon and the boy he knows have nowhere to make a noise. Kit goes back to headphones at his dad’s. Bev stays at five o’clock for good.

Tess has said nothing so far. She has the clarinet case across her knees. “I looked after my mother for years,” she says. “Nobody ever came back and said, good news, we found the money, you can stop.” She does payroll. She has seen what happens to a line on a sheet once somebody shows the work gets done without it.

Fran asks Bev what she wants.

“The hours,” Bev says. “Don’t ask me if it’s smart.” Six evenings of two hours make twelve hours between now and the end of March. She used to get twelve in a week. She doesn’t have a vote. She has the keys, and a chair from which to watch seven people decide what her Tuesdays are worth.

The seven are the three who keep the film group’s can and the four who used the room in the fall. Fran votes yes. So does Leon, who says he’d have liked a better thing to say yes to. So do Kit’s mother and the father with the speech, who has no speech left to practice and came anyway. Howard votes no. Denise, who captions the films and has been paid in thanks by this group before, votes no.

Tess votes no, against her own half hour.

Four to three. Howard takes back the pencil and looks at the portrait. Einstein has nothing to add.

So the lights stay on for one more quarter, every other Tuesday from six to eight until the end of March. Six dates Bev can write on her calendar, in pencil. Sixteen slots anybody can book in person or by phone, free to whoever says so. Nothing beyond that has been secured.

Who signs the notice

A small decision honestly recorded is an uncomfortable thing to own. Fran can set out her reasons. No discovery is coming that will tell her whether she weighed the boy at the door correctly against Bev’s twelve hours, or against Howard’s question, which nobody answered. She’d like it formally established that she’s on the right side. The problem has numbers in it. That doesn’t make it addition.

She does know what she has committed herself to finding out. After the sixth Tuesday she’ll have the bookings, the money taken, the slots that sat empty and Bev’s record of what it took to run the room. The people who never heard about it won’t be in her figures, and she won’t know how many they were. All of it goes downtown, where six paid evenings can be read as demand or as a bill somebody else has agreed to pick up. The file down there already holds a numbered repair slip from the fall, on which the words probable structural defect have a line through them and are easy to read.

Kit is waiting by the door with his coat on. One of the center’s music stands has a loose foot, and when he sets it where he wants it the top tilts until the music starts to slide. Fran has just taped the Einstein poster to the wall by the door. She tears a strip from the blank margin at the bottom. Kit folds it twice and wedges it under the foot. The music stays up. It’s the distinguished visitor’s first demonstrable contribution to the plan.

Coming back from stacking the chairs, Fran finds Leon reading the list of dates. He runs his finger down it twice and puts them in his phone. This is the thing she has been trying all evening to file as a small administrative misunderstanding. Her notice in the fall said music practice every Tuesday. It gave no last date. Leon came on the seventh Tuesday because, as far as her own words went, it was as much a Tuesday as the first six.

She goes over to tell him. She starts with “We weren’t clear” and hears how many people have just joined her in that sentence.

“I left the last date off the notice,” she says. “I’m sorry. You came here because of what I wrote.”

“Yes,” he says. “I thought it was me. I stood there pushing the handle up the way she showed us.”

She hadn’t pictured that. She’d pictured bad weather, wasted fares and a man annoyed with the people who run the place. She hadn’t pictured him at a dark door with a trombone case, lifting the handle again in case he’d done it wrong, wondering what simple thing everybody else here knew how to do. Nobody at tonight’s table could have told her that, Einstein included. Leon was the one standing there.

She offers to cover his fares. He’ll take it, he says, and he’d like the number of somebody to call if it happens again. She writes her own under the six dates. They both read the corrected notice, this time to the bottom. He takes two copies for the drugstore.

Fran stays another minute. The new booking sheet has a line for the person responsible. She spent a good part of the evening trying to borrow a name impressive enough to go at the top. She signs her own at the bottom, in her sloping hand. If the city reads six paid Tuesdays as proof that it never has to pay for another, that will have her name under it too.
