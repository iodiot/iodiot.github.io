Chapter 20Einstein Didn't Owe You a Life Lesson

Albert Einstein has been present at the meeting for eleven minutes when Bev asks if he’d like to take a turn locking up.

He’s on a folded proof of the poster, propped at the end of the table opposite the custard creams. A great quantity of hair and a moustache, recognisable even in a drawing done by a woman who has met neither the man nor, apparently, a human ear. Under the portrait Fran has put six words. Imagination is more important than knowledge. The quote looked good in the generous white space at the top. Then she added his name, and somehow he’s ended up chairing the meeting.

The practice-room trial ran for six weeks. On the seventh Tuesday a man who plays the trombone took two buses to get here and found the place dark. The children’s evening class has finished. Bev’s hours now finish at five. There’s no one on duty to open the building after that. What their guest at the end of the table has been invited to discuss, despite having died in 1955, is whether to start up again, what to charge and who’s going to get the bins in at eight.

Fran wanted the poster to make the scheme sound worth coming to. The previous six weeks have brought together a woman with a clarinet, a child trying to play a piano that came with its own cutlery section, various people with instruments too loud for a flat, and a father who needed a couple of goes at a wedding speech. Call it Committee Room Two Extension Proposal and you’ll get roughly the attendance that name deserves. She wanted somebody to vouch for the idea that trying things has a value. Einstein had a nice line on the subject and made no demands on the design budget.

“Did he even say it?” asks the treasurer.

“Yes, actually,” says Fran. She has brought the evidence. This is among the happiest moments available to a person on a committee. Somebody’s asked the exact question for which you have done the homework. She takes out the pages she printed and lets herself enjoy the rustle.

The line appears in a 1929 interview by George Sylvester Viereck in The Saturday Evening Post. The interviewer asks Einstein about intuition and inspiration in his discoveries. Einstein talks about the confidence he had felt in the prediction tested at the solar eclipse ten years earlier. Viereck then asks about the relative importance he places on imagination and knowledge. The famous sentence appears in his reply. You can go and see the question on the page. He really said it, insofar as an interview printed in a magazine can tell us what somebody said in a conversation nearly a century ago. [1]

There. Genuine Einstein. A little silence. Somebody at the back chooses a biscuit.

Bev says, “Which bit says who pays for the room?”

It is possible for the attribution to be completely right and for you still to be using the man as a glove puppet. This is a much more interesting problem than an internet meme with a spelling mistake. If the quote were fake, Fran could remove it, put the kettle on and get on with a satisfying denunciation of the state of public education. As things stand she is going to have to decide what the genuine article actually entitles her to do.

A receipt with the middle missing

Let’s be fair to her. Imagination has been useful. Six weeks ago the little room was a place where the hall committee put people when the big room was occupied, and occasionally a place where a stack of spare chairs went for an extended convalescence. It took an act of imagination to see what different use a working adult in a small flat might make of it. The people insisting on firm numbers would have had no numbers to add up if somebody hadn’t first pictured the place open. Fran wants that achievement to carry some weight in the discussion. I think she has a case. It belongs to Fran. One of the less dignified features of being alive is how much better your own reasonable case can sound when delivered by a famous dead stranger.

Now trace the implied argument right through. Einstein thought imagination important to discovery. Imagination helped us see a use for the room. Therefore… what, precisely? Therefore four people a night should pay five pounds each? Therefore nobody should pay? Therefore Bev should give up six evenings for free, during which she may use her imagination to put something hot on her kitchen table? You can steer that quotation right up to the till. At some point a human being with a wallet has to take over.

Try an experiment with the paper. Fold back Einstein’s name. Leave the sentence visible. What do you think of it now? Is imagination always more important than knowledge? In which part of an operation to remove your appendix would you like the surgeon to have an unsupervised go at this proposition? When she’s trying to see what might explain an unexpected pool of blood, an imagination capable of generating alternatives would be desirable. When she’s deciding which bit is your bowel, it would be lovely if she knew. You might even want the same highly trained woman to do both jobs, and to know when she has moved from one to the other.

In the interview Einstein himself makes room for the distinction between his confidence that he is right and his knowing that he is. The sentence on the poster has been relieved of a question, an example and a fairly considerable act of intellectual modesty. Six genuine words will fit on a mug. It is murder getting the surrounding conversation through the handle.

There’s a commercial genius to choosing Einstein for these things. Draw the hair and most people know who you mean. Try putting the face of the person who does the hall accounts on a tote bag. See if strangers in airports will buy it. Einstein’s face packs decades of intellectual achievement into an area the size of a digestive biscuit. You can place it above your own opinion and borrow an entire scientific career in under a second. The sad part is how little use you need to have made of any actual physics for the operation to succeed.

I suspect the real service the man is asked to provide is sometimes closer to an alibi. If I put my own name under a sentence about taking risks and you follow it into an expensive disaster, we may have to have a rather uncomfortable conversation. If I can get a Nobel laureate’s name under the same advice, that should at least hold you up at reception. You’ll need a time machine and a good command of German to escalate your complaint.

What it took to earn the haircut

The eclipse in Einstein’s interview is a good place to see the difference between a name worth listening to and a name entitled to end an argument. General relativity predicted that light passing close to the Sun would be deflected. From Earth, a star seen near the Sun’s limb would appear displaced a little from its usual position. The predicted shift for a ray grazing the limb was about 1.75 seconds of arc. An arcsecond is a measure of angle. There are 3,600 of them in a degree. Nobody on the 1919 expeditions expected a star to go missing for nearly two seconds of anybody’s afternoon. [2]

If the Sun were a handy transparent object you could hang there while measuring precisely where the stars behind it appeared, this would be an agreeable piece of work. The actual Sun is quite badly designed for seeing dim stars beside it. An eclipse could briefly cut its glare. Two observing expeditions went to different places for the eclipse of 29 May 1919, one to Sobral in Brazil, one to the island of Príncipe off the west coast of Africa. Photographic plates would record the star field during totality. Comparing those star positions with reference photographs of the field could reveal an apparent shift. The Moon, in what must count as a particularly demanding piece of unpaid committee work, would keep the appointments department running on time.

Here’s a scale you can picture. At a distance of a kilometre, one centimetre subtends an angle of roughly two arcseconds. That centimetre divided by a hundred thousand centimetres of distance gives one hundred-thousandth of a radian. Multiply by about 206,265 arcseconds per radian and you get a little over two arcseconds. The predicted deflection at the solar limb was a bit smaller still. You’re trying to establish a tiny angular difference from photographs taken under different conditions while a cloud considers whether to bring the whole exercise to a fairly economical conclusion.

If the optics change between one exposure and the comparison plate, positions can shift for reasons having bugger all to do with Einstein. The observers had to allow for the scale of the images, assess their quality and distinguish statistical variation from an instrument doing something unhelpful. In their paper Frank Dyson, Arthur Eddington and Charles Davidson reported estimates of about 1.98 arcseconds from the smaller Sobral instrument and 1.61 from Príncipe, expressed as deflections at the solar limb. A further Sobral series gave troublesome results. Its images had lost sharpness and the inferred deflection depended on how the change of scale was treated. The authors set out the difficulty and placed much greater weight on the other series. The result they favoured supported the larger deflection predicted by general relativity. It came with a description of how they got there, including the plate series that made for an absolutely awful poster.

Imagine trying to do this with a famous name alone. Plate says one thing, Einstein says another. Does the plate have tenure? Has it published? Would anybody buy a T-shirt with a picture of it? The value of a photograph is precisely that it doesn’t owe the theorist a steady job or an agreeable result. The catch is that it doesn’t interpret itself either. People with experience of telescopes have to do work other people can examine. The reputation of a brilliant theorist gave excellent reason to mount a difficult test. It gave the astronomers no licence to write his preferred measurement on the box before the plates came home.

There’s a point at which distrust of famous people turns every bit as silly as worship. Consider the thoroughly modern hero who reads about instrument problems at Sobral, closes the tab before the researchers start addressing them and decides he’s brought down relativity in the queue for a sandwich. Researchers had to live with the awkward measurements, explain the treatment and expose enough of the process for other people to argue about it. The man with the sandwich has scheduled no follow-up. Both may have questions. One group has done rather more to earn your confidence on the matter of a star.

You can rationally trust the work of experts you could never reproduce unaided. If your roof is bowing, call a structural engineer. If she suggests a visit before she pronounces on what’s happening, that’s a point in her favour. If the engineer also tells you how to handle your separation, you are entitled to ask what particular qualification in roofs establishes who should get the greyhound. She may well have had an exceptionally civilised separation of her own. It’s the story and the reasons you’d want to hear now, and possibly what arrangement the greyhound prefers.

Most of our knowledge comes to us through other people. I have no plans to set up an observatory to check where the stars are before I decide what to put on a sandwich. The useful alternatives are better and worse ways of relying on others. Which part of the problem is this person in a position to know about? Is there an account you can examine? Do the relevant people broadly agree, and where do they still differ? Who has to put the result into practice on Wednesday morning? A good answer to the first three is a fine reason to listen. A good answer to the fourth may involve inviting somebody else to speak.

In this room Bev knows things about the job that a Nobel laureate would have to ask her. Two hours of public use mean a person on duty the whole time. Someone needs to know what to do if the big entrance sticks or the upstairs lights trip. The building charges the scheme a total of £36 for each two-hour evening, staffing and running costs together. If Bev is doing the shift she wants to be paid for it. She’s willing to say so in front of Einstein, who has had the splendid advantage of remaining completely affordable since April 1955.

An opinion from somebody who’ll have to pay

A woman sitting near the biscuits has brought her son. It’s Kit, who can now play the piece almost all the way through and wants another crack at the bar his left hand still takes as a purely advisory suggestion. His mother would happily pay five pounds for a half-hour slot. The important thing for her is a reliable booking. She and Kit’s dad could split the cost. If they split the transport as well she could sometimes be home for the little one’s bedtime.

The trombonist, on the other side of the table, knows a boy who would stop coming if a fiver appeared on the door. He also suspects that a project which makes anybody prove they can’t afford a fiver to a committee of local acquaintances has priced the cheaper places considerably higher than five pounds. The committee sometimes meets in the little supermarket. There are things you don’t want to have gone into with a stranger you are going to encounter for the rest of the year buying kitchen roll.

For a moment they sit there advancing different claims about what the same room is for. A reliable bookable place to practise. A place you can use regardless of how much you’ve got left on Tuesday. A properly paid shift. A use for the piano. A respite for everyone currently within earshot of a beginner on the trombone. Einstein’s face has the serene composure available to a person who doesn’t have to choose.

Would it help to know what Einstein thought about access to resources? As it happens, he left quite a forceful account of his political views. In the first issue of Monthly Review in May 1949 he published an essay making a case for socialism. He opens by asking what business somebody with his background has discussing the economic and social arrangements of a society. Far from assuming that physics accredits him to do it, he tries to explain why such questions concern people beyond the professional economists. He distinguishes the means science can help us understand from the ends a society decides to serve. He also raises a danger for the system he favours, the power a bureaucracy might accumulate. [3]

You can disagree with him. You can be an enthusiastic socialist and disagree with what he thinks the pitfalls are. You can think the whole programme undesirable. The argument survives his haircut coming off. It’s there in connected sentences with assumptions, historical observations and ethical commitments people can dispute. To read the essay is to grant him something the motivational poster takes away. He gets to be an adult with opinions he can be asked to defend.

Imagine the alternative position. A person should speak only on a subject in which they have superior formal credentials. This sounds splendid when a well-known footballer is wading into planetary physics. Carry it across the street and the mother of a child who uses a wheelchair can now be asked to leave the planning consultation because she hasn’t got an architecture degree. She may be the only person in the room who knows what the proposed entrance is like in the rain with one hand on the chair and the other trying to reach a button mounted behind the hinge. Her claim about that experience has a specific source of authority. She’s been there. She doesn’t have to be able to calculate the load on the lintel to tell you where they put the bloody switch.

Equally, having been there doesn’t give her a licence to guess the load on the lintel any more than being able to calculate the load gives the engineer the right to decide which children should get into the building. It would be convenient to rank everyone on one great staircase of intelligence and call for the highest available individual each time a decision had to be made. Real expertise has an awkward floor plan. The person who knows the structure of the building can sometimes stand at its door unable to get in.

What’s the attraction, then, of wanting our cleverest scientist to issue transferable rulings about how to live? There’s a kindly reason as well as the one involving a shortage of personal backbone. The world is full of terrible advice and we need shortcuts. A person who has demonstrably thought for themselves, stayed curious and survived being wrong in public is about as sensible a candidate as you can find if you want company while you think. Reading someone’s letters can even make you less afraid of uncertainty. Fine. Get company. Read a whole letter. Let yourself quarrel with a dead man for three pages until you see why he made the choice he did. That is very different work from hiring him as an invisible bouncer to prevent anybody quarrelling with you.

Fran looks down at the numbers. A further six evenings at £36 would cost £216. There’s £96 left in a fund that can be used for music. That leaves £120 to find. There are four half-hour slots a night. Sell all twenty-four at five pounds each and there’s your £120. You can just about see the headline forming in the treasurer’s mind. Affordable practice, paid staff, programme pays for itself. Where on this magnificent proposal is there space for the evening everybody gets flu?

Take it seriously for another moment. At eighteen paid bookings, five pounds each brings in £90. The shortfall is £30. People declining because of the fee make the shortfall worse, and the people with most to lose by a flat charge are also those least likely to attend a Thursday meeting to debate it. On the other hand Kit’s mum is right that a place is worth very little to a person whose buses run every half-hour if they can’t count on being able to use it. No money at the door may remove one barrier. Persistent chaos can put another one up in exactly the same spot.

The trombonist asks if he could buy a supporter slot at ten pounds. He can afford a tenner. He just doesn’t want to impose one on everybody else. Kit’s mum says they’d take a couple on that basis as long as they can book. There are others who like the idea. At this point the traditional committee procedure would be to multiply the number of enthusiastic noises by ten pounds, announce the project fully funded and leave somebody else to spend the first Thursday in March pursuing a noise for payment.

Bev proposes counting money when it is actually in the tin. They take a break. Eight of the twenty-four slots are reserved at the supporter price, £80 paid, refundable if the new block doesn’t run. That leaves £40 of the £120 gap. They could cover it by shifting £40 from the hall’s publicity budget. Sixteen slots would stay available to book at whatever price the player could manage, including zero. If anybody paid for one of those, the first £40 received could go back to publicity. They’d have a staffed trial the building could honour even if not one of those players had a penny.

There’s a cost to shifting the £40. Somebody mentions glossy leaflets, as if no right-thinking person could possibly object to printing slightly fewer bits of paper. The man with the trombone objects. He has a smartphone, but the people he first mentioned the room to read about practically everything on the noticeboard at the chemist’s. Kill the print budget entirely and the free practice space may become extremely convenient for everybody except the people you just made it free for.

Here the useful imaginative leap comes from a woman who knows where to get cheap black-and-white photocopies. They can keep a dozen single-sheet notices at the chemist’s, the launderette and a couple of bus stops where notices are allowed. They’ll lose the extra colour run Fran had hoped to do. There will be fewer notices in total. Somebody may miss one. The trombonist still prefers the flat charge with free places set aside to the plan on the table. With free places actually guaranteed, you could know exactly how many slots were protected. Good objection. The vote is close.

Fran votes for the supporter plan. Four to three. They’re going to run for six more Tuesdays. Every shift is funded. The sixteen remaining spaces are open for booking in person or by phone and anybody can ask for one at no charge. Nobody has to give a reason. They agree to look again after the block. The room hasn’t been secured for all time. It has been secured until eight o’clock on six dates Bev can write in her diary. In the life of an actual person, six dates in a reliable diary can be quite a consequential unit of eternity.

Who signs the notice

There’s something surprisingly uncomfortable about a small decision honestly recorded. Fran can set out her reasons. There is no further scientific breakthrough that could guarantee she gave the right weight to the people who will miss a notice, as against the people who might otherwise have missed the room. She would like it to be formally established that she’s on the right side. It would also be nice if a machine could tell her which custard cream in the packet has the highest social value. The presence of numbers within a problem is no guarantee that the whole problem has become an adding-up exercise.

Imagine two communities making different decisions on identical budgets. In one town, there’s a free room available elsewhere but it cannot be reserved. They may reasonably spend their £120 on a reliable bookable alternative. In another, the only other available place charges twenty pounds an hour and can be reached only by car. They might be more concerned to preserve some walk-in places. Both can get the arithmetic right. They’re also entitled to disagree about priorities in their own circumstances. If you want to change either decision, you’ll have to tackle that disagreement. Reciting a bigger scientist at them will mainly reduce the available time for tea.

The person who questions a quotation can also be the person who wants the room to fail. This is a small discomfort worth sitting with. Close readings and calls for better evidence can be a very respectable way of running down the clock. Every number is an estimate, every programme has unknown consequences, every person could in principle be asked a more carefully worded question. At some point the request for further information comes from a man with no conceivable answer under which he would vote yes. He is as much in hiding behind science as the woman who puts Einstein in charge of the biscuits.

Fair questions for him might be what evidence he would accept and how much it would cost to get it. A fair question for Fran is what she has just committed herself to finding out. On the day after the sixth Tuesday she’ll have bookings, payments received, slots unused, the publicity cost, and Bev’s record of what it actually took to run the room. She can leave a place for people who couldn’t book to say why. The people who never heard about the scheme at all will be largely missing from her figures, and of course she won’t know how many. Small marks of honesty like that will help the next meeting discern what it can learn from the previous one. They are considerably more labour than putting a physicist on a poster. They have the advantage of making it worth holding the meeting.

Kit is waiting by the door with his coat on. One of the hall’s music stands has a loose foot. The boy puts the stand where he wants it and the top tilts until the music threatens to slide off. Fran tears a strip from the blank margin at the bottom of the Einstein poster. Folded twice it fits under the foot. The music stays up. It is the distinguished visitor’s first demonstrable contribution to the scheme. They leave the picture on the wall. It looks rather good there.

On her way back from putting the chairs away, Fran finds the trombonist looking at the list of dates. He runs his finger down it twice and puts them in his phone. Opposite his name, on the original trial list, she recognises the note about his two buses. There’s something she’s been trying to turn into a small administrative misunderstanding all evening. It was her message on the hall website that said there would be music practice every Tuesday. There wasn’t an end date. He came on the seventh Tuesday because, as far as her own words were concerned, that was as much a Tuesday as any of the first six.

She goes to tell him what happened. She starts with “We weren’t clear” and realises that she’s managed to populate the vicinity with a whole new committee since he opened his mouth.

“I left the last date off the notice,” she says. “I’m sorry. You came here because of what I wrote.”

“Yes,” he says. “I thought it was me. I stood there pushing the handle up the way she showed us.”

She hadn’t pictured that. She’d pictured bad weather and wasted bus fares and a man fairly cross with the people who run a hall. She hadn’t pictured him at an unlit door, lifting the handle again in case he’d done it wrong the first time, wondering what elementary thing everybody here knew how to do except him. This is precisely the kind of testimony on which he is the leading available authority.

She offers to cover his fare. He’ll take it, he says. And he’d quite like the number of somebody he can call if it happens again. Fair enough. She writes her own under the six dates. They both read the revised notice, this time all the way to the bottom. When he leaves he takes two paper copies for the chemist.

Fran stays on for another minute. There’s a place on the new booking sheet for the name of the person responsible. She’s spent a good part of the evening trying to borrow a name impressive enough to go at the top. At the bottom, in her own rather sloping hand, hers will do.
