Chapter 10Your Job Is Not Your Soul

“I’m a technical author,” Sam says. His niece, who saw the whole performance, says, “He’s a duck.”

Both answers have evidence behind them. Only one of them gets Sam a mortgage interview.

The neighbor has come to borrow a folding chair. She has flour on her sleeve from Dan’s kitchen and five minutes to kill before she has to get back next door. “A technical author,” she says. “What do you write?”

“The little book that comes with the furniture.”

“Oh, God,” she says. “I owe you an apology.”

Everybody who hears what Sam does has either lost the little book or sworn at the person who wrote it. He quite likes this part of the conversation. The other person has spent a Sunday on the floor among pieces of a wardrobe. He knows the floor. For a moment they have somewhere to meet.

It’s the half second before he explains the title that interests him. He enjoys that half second too. Author sits there in the air. For all this woman knows, there are novels. Perhaps a small but prestigious prize. He could have said he writes the instructions for putting furniture together. That’s the sentence he would use with a child, a foreigner or anyone else he especially wanted to understand him. Given a fellow adult and a perfectly good cup of coffee, he went for the version with a little balcony from which he could look down.

His niece wants paper. Sam produces a business card from his wallet. Dan’s eldest turns it over and draws the duck while Sam demonstrates the left wing. When she gives it back, the animal looks as if it has been in a bar fight and declined medical attention. She is pleased with the likeness. Sam puts the card away.

The neighbor’s name is Fran. She works three mornings a week at a pharmacy. The chair is for her father, who has come to live with her and needs somewhere to sit while he changes his shoes. She’s ordered a hallway bench. Until it arrives, getting him out of the house involves dragging a dining chair to the front door while he assures her with increasing heat that he has managed to put on his shoes since before she was born.

Sam could listen to Fran say works three mornings a week and come away with the impression that she has the other four to herself. All the work required to get a second adult dressed, fed and down the front steps would fit quite neatly into the pause after pharmacy. It fits there because nobody pays her to do it. Hand her father’s shoes to an employee and, without so much as disturbing a shoelace, the family would have produced a job.

A perfectly good use for a title

If you smell burning on a plane and someone asks whether there’s a pilot on board, that’s a poor time to say we are all so much more than our professions. There are things we want to know quickly about strangers. Can you land this? Who can explain the thing I’m about to sign? Which one of you can turn the water off? A job title lets you draw on years of somebody’s practice before you even know their name.

A title can matter to the person who earned it, too. Think of the first time an apprentice is given a key and left to open the shop alone. Yesterday somebody stood behind her listening for the moment she would need help. Today the owner is having breakfast across town. The key still catches in the lock. The floor still wants sweeping. But a relationship has changed, and she can feel the change in her pocket. If she goes home proud to call herself a mechanic, there is something rather mean about reminding her that careers don’t matter in the grand scheme of things. A customer trusted her with the car he drives his kids around in. How grand a scheme were you hoping for?

Sam remembers the first instruction he wrote that actually helped somebody. A customer had called about a drawer that wouldn’t close. Sam had the fancy sectional drawing open on his monitor. The customer had a wooden box stuck halfway into another wooden box and a phone wedged between his ear and his shoulder. After a couple of barren minutes discussing the orientation of the left runner, Sam asked him to slide a finger along the metal until he felt a little wheel. Was the wheel near his knee or near the wall? A pause. A rolling sound. The drawer went in.

Sam went home and told Jo about that sound. He couldn’t really convey it. It had been a tiny noise over a bad connection, followed by a man laughing because he could finally put his socks away. The pleasure was having found the sentence that worked from the other end of the phone. Sam would happily do that kind of thing until he’s old. He might also, should his salary stop, give it up in favor of the kind of thing that pays for groceries. Both facts deserve to survive the discussion.

Trouble starts on the day you take that key home and notice that nothing else on the ring feels quite as substantial. Work offers an extraordinarily convenient arrangement for anyone who’s uneasy about whether he deserves his place in the world. At nine a person expects you. By eleven you have been useful. At three somebody who annoys the hell out of you has done you the great kindness of requiring your opinion. Then you come home to somebody you love and discover that all she wants is to spend the evening with you. On certain days this feels like being asked to perform a difficult piece of music after they’ve taken away the score.

You can see the attraction of doing one more email. It has a subject line. You can finish it. The recipient may even write brilliant, thanks, which is quite a lot of admiration to collect for attaching the right PDF. Meanwhile a person on the sofa has begun a story whose relevance to you is unclear and may remain unclear for some time. Listening is going to require skills for which no employer has yet thought to give you a badge. One of them is staying in your seat while somebody else gets to the point.

The cost of that last email is seldom visible to the person who sends it. From his side he is a conscientious adult winding up a small piece of business. From the sofa it is the third time the blue light has come on during the same story. A week later he complains that they don’t really talk anymore. Somewhere in the correspondence there is documentary proof that he had an excellent reason every single time.

The two screws

On Monday morning Sam’s team is trying out a new way of producing instructions. A program can turn the manufacturer’s parts list, assembly notes and house style into a first draft. On the table is the sample product, a low oak bench designed for a narrow hall. Fran’s father could sit on it. Sam would prefer that the first member of the public to try be a gentleman to whom he has made fewer personal introductions.

The first draft arrives while the kettle is boiling. It’s good. Worse, as far as Sam’s feelings are concerned, it’s good in a way he recognizes. Whoever prepared the house style had supplied examples of his old guides. There is his habit of identifying a part by what the reader can see from the floor. There is the little pause before a difficult step. There, mercifully, is a reference to securing the rail after the seat has gone on. He has found an error. His first sensation is relief.

Imagine being the customer who discovers that inaccessible screw on Sunday evening. Sam ordinarily likes this person. At the moment he could kiss them on the mouth for the career opportunity presented by their ruined Sunday.

Ellen, who draws the diagrams, suggests that they build the thing. This is the part of a career in publishing that nobody mentioned at school. Two grown adults who own remarkably expensive computers spend the next twenty minutes on their knees. They fit the legs, slot the narrow rail across the frame and put on the seat. Sam reaches for the two remaining screws. The holes he needs are directly underneath a handsome expanse of oak.

Ellen lifts the seat. He looks into the gap. The drawing on the screen shows the fixing holes beautifully. Of course it does. For clarity the drawing leaves out the seat.

There is a small audience for the demonstration. Sam enjoys the way the room goes quiet while he explains. Then Ellen opens a guide they published two years ago for an earlier version of the bench. The steps run in the same order. At the foot of the file, in the little box for final approval, are Sam’s initials. It’s a difficult moment for those of us who have come to watch humanity triumph over the machine. Humanity would like to take an early lunch.

The error can be fixed in either guide by reversing two steps. The more interesting question is what the company has been buying from Sam all these years. Some hours it was sentences. Some hours it was the ability to hear, in an ordinary customer’s description of a drawer, what view the customer had of the part. Some days he had a handsome drawing and signed off on a daft piece of work. A claim that his occupation made him uniquely wise would have to account for the previous edition of that bench.

There is evidence that a program can make a real difference to some of the things people are paid to write. In a 2023 experiment, Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang assigned professional writing tasks to 453 college-educated people and randomly gave half of them access to ChatGPT. Average completion time fell by 40 percent and quality scores rose by 18 percent in the experiment. Those were bounded writing assignments with outputs people could rate. The researchers didn’t hire several hundred people to run a furniture company for a decade and then see what happened to the complaints department. [1]

Here is what the distinction means to the person holding the screwdriver. Suppose producing and checking a small guide usually takes eight staff-hours. Two go on the first draft. Six go on getting answers from the manufacturer, checking the drawings, test assembly and revisions. Suppose the new tool cuts the two drafting hours to one. Leave the other six unchanged for the moment. The guide now takes seven hours. Half the drafting time has gone away. Across the whole job the saving is one hour out of eight, or 12.5 percent. If checking the draft introduces an extra hour of work, the saving disappears. If the tool also helps with revisions, the saving grows. You’d have to time the work to find out. Those eight hours belong to this example. The numbers in the experiment belong to the people who took part in it.

One hour saved on each of eight guides buys an entire working day. That seems worth having an argument about. The team could spend it letting an unfamiliar pair of hands try each product. They could take on an extra commission, train the junior illustrator or let everybody go home early enough to see a child in a deeply unsettling school production of Cats. They could also do the same amount of work with fewer paid hours. Arithmetic has located the day. Whoever has a say in the business decides who gets it.

At Sam’s meeting the junior illustrator’s chair is actually empty. The previous occupant left in February and the company has been putting off hiring a replacement. No little brass plate marks the place where somebody might by now have been learning to read a sectional drawing upside down. The chair won’t write a furious account of its layoff. It can quietly fill up with coats. It’s possible for an occupation to change shape without anyone in the room receiving a dramatic brown envelope. The next person trying to get into the room may nevertheless have quite a different experience.

On his way back to his desk Sam prints both versions. He puts the one with his initials underneath. A man can make considerable ethical progress and still have a clear preference about the order in which two sheets of paper come out of a printer.

A woman whose job involved dots

We’ve had some practice at mistaking the description of a task for the description of the mind doing it. At Harvard College Observatory, Henrietta Swan Leavitt worked with astronomical photographs on glass. Consider what it took to watch a star change brightness by that means. You could compare plates taken on different nights. There were a great many dots, the exposures could differ, and a particularly interesting dot might be next to several others. Long before anybody had a grand thought about the universe, somebody had to be certain they were looking at the same damned dot. Harvard’s plate collection still preserves the materials from that work. [2]

In 1912 the observatory published Leavitt’s measurements of twenty-five variable stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud. She had arranged them in order of how long each took to cycle through its changes in brightness. A pattern showed up. Among these stars, those with longer periods tended to be brighter. Since the stars in the same cloud were at roughly the same distance from Earth, the difference in observed brightness offered a clue to a difference in the light they actually emitted. [3]

Distance is the nuisance here. Picture two equally luminous stars nearby, with clear space between us and them. One is twice as far away. The same light spreads over a sphere with four times the area, so each square inch of telescope catches a quarter as much from the farther star. A dim dot leaves an awkward question about how much light left home in the first place. With Leavitt's relationship, once calibrated, a Cepheid's rhythm helps you answer it. Reading the clock in a dot of light helps you measure the distance to the thing that made it. [3], [4]

Look at who gets which end of the 1912 circular. Leavitt is credited in its opening sentence. The name at the bottom is the director's, Edward C. Pickering. [3] It's satisfying to give her her due. Less satisfying is the bargain you can accidentally offer the woman at the next desk. Keep all those glass plates safe, put in thirty years, and if you'd kindly discover a law of the universe on the way out we'll concede that your life amounted to something.

Fran would quite like somebody to be capable of covering her next pharmacy shift. It's pleasant to think you could have an appendectomy without a colleague leaning in to ask where you keep the labels.

When Fran sits on the floor to help her father into his shoes, somebody else could do the practical part. There are mornings when she wishes fervently that somebody else would. But her father is embarrassed about his swollen feet in a particular way around his daughter because he can remember holding her feet in the palm of one hand. She knows which of his old jokes lets him accept the help. A paid carer could come to know him well and build a relationship of their own. Even the best job description in the world couldn’t transfer that earlier Saturday morning from one childhood into another.

What gets to be particular about you has a great deal to do with time. You happened to be there, you paid attention, you acquired a history with somebody. A skill can be shared and a post can pass to the next capable person. History behaves rather differently. The new technical author could learn to write the same sentence Sam wrote about the drawer. He couldn’t go home all those years ago and tell Jo about the sound it made.

What you lose on a Monday morning

There is a cheap version of this argument available to anybody whose salary is due on Friday. It goes something like this. You have confused your job with your identity. Try cultivating interests outside work. Have you considered pottery? It’s striking how often, in this account, the appropriate response to the loss of an income is to buy a wheel.

Take away a job somebody loves and you take away more than the line they use to introduce themselves. At half past nine on Monday they knew whose coffee would be going cold by the printer. There was a part of the week when somebody was waiting for something only they knew how to finish. Even the colleague who had been telling the same story since 2011 supplied a dependable human presence at ten past twelve. The newly unemployed person may find, to their private disgust, that they miss the end of the story. They never thought to find out which exit he took in the rental car.

Then there’s the loss of an ordinary future tense. When you had the job, you’d say in the fall we’re moving upstairs, or wait till you meet the new apprentice. There was a place reserved for you in a thing that hadn’t happened yet. On the first free Monday you can still picture all of it. The others may even send photographs from upstairs, out of affection. It can feel like getting postcards from a country where you used to live and whose language you apparently taught half the population.

A person can grieve that loss without having made a philosophical mistake. They can also be angry about the loss of money. Money buys distance from the boss who puts his hand where it doesn’t belong. It buys an afternoon off to take a father to his appointment. Sometimes it buys the chance to leave a flat with mold growing behind the baby’s bed. Suggesting that anybody in that position learn to derive less of their worth from income manages to be spiritually ambitious and quite extraordinarily thick.

What deserves a closer look is the moment a perfectly sound sentence about the world changes into a questionable one about a human being. I’ve lost the person who read my drawings can be true. I’m frightened I won’t be able to pay for the flat can be true. If those two sentences run together late enough at night, out comes I’m a burden. Now a prediction about next month’s bills is being used to settle how much space a body is entitled to occupy on earth. You’d want a considerably better accountant before signing off on that conclusion.

Here is a small test. Pick the person you’d most want to hear from if you were sitting alone in a hospital waiting room at three in the morning. Think for a second about what they’d actually do. They might bring you a sandwich you’re too wound up to eat. They might sit there reading an atrocious magazine and occasionally tell you which celebrity has had a kitchen remodeled. If you’re lucky, they’re the sort of person in whose company you could fall asleep for twenty minutes and wake up still feeling like yourself. Consider what happens to the answer if you learn that this person was laid off yesterday. There is probably still room on the plastic seat.

Now run the thing in the other direction. The last time you were the one needing that seat, did you think of some impressive way to earn it first? Would you ask someone else to? A surprising number of decent people maintain, somewhere in the fine print, a private exemption from the ordinary kindness they extend to everybody else. Their friends can get sick, go broke, produce nothing of distinction and still come round on Sunday. They themselves are on a provisional contract subject to quarterly review. Whoever drew up that arrangement drives a remarkably hard bargain for somebody who also has to live under it.

The part you take home

One objection before we send Sam back into the meeting. A job can become a splendid alibi. Somebody chose to keep the old drawing in circulation. Somebody will decide whether the junior post gets filled. It would take considerable nerve for a manager to leave a decision like that on Friday, drive home and claim that his real self begins at the garden gate. If his choices affect the hours another person gets paid for, that person has a legitimate interest in who he is between nine and five. The hours at work belong to his life too. He can answer for what he does in them and still have a life to go home to when they end.

Sam takes both guides to the meeting. To his considerable disappointment the ceiling stays up when he admits he signed the old one. Ellen says she can put the assembly trial in the schedule if they give her a second person for an hour and send an actual sample before the brochure is due. The person who controls the schedule asks which other job should move to make room. A rather boring conversation follows about Thursdays. This is what an improvement can sound like from inside an organization. Two people agree on a Thursday and somebody writes it down.

The company will use the program for first drafts on the next batch. They’ll keep a note of the time spent checking and correcting them. Sam gets to show a junior colleague how to watch a stranger use a guide without leaping in to rescue them halfway through the interesting mistake. There’s still an empty chair by the coats. They haven’t resolved that question. He’d have liked a firmer promise about his own future too. He takes some comfort in knowing that the next person who buys the bench has an improved chance of getting up off the floor before bedtime.

If he lost the position tomorrow, some of what he learned would go with him. The knack of turning a page round to see it from somebody else’s side could travel quite a long way from furniture. Other things would be harder to carry. The workshop key ought to go back. So would the weekly hour with Ellen that has allowed them to become friends without ever quite arranging to be friends. They’d have to do what other adults do and each propose three incompatible dates for a drink until one of them dies.

On his way home he pulls out the business card to scribble something down. There’s the duck. He’d forgotten that she gave it a little stitched patch on its bad wing. This is a piece of information about the animal that the original performer had no idea about. His niece has been attending to a slightly different duck all these years. He sends her a photograph and asks what happened to the wing.

The reply comes back on Dan’s phone. He fell off his chair at work. A second message follows. He works at a pond.

Even the bloody duck has a job.

Sam slips the card back into his wallet, the patched wing facing out.

The bench arrives at Fran’s a few weeks later. Different company, different fittings. She puts it together herself. When Sam next goes round to Dan’s, the old man is sitting in the doorway working his left heel into a shoe. Fran starts to bend and catches herself. He’s got it. She stands watching a piece of work she no longer has to do. It’s a small triumph which, according to the usual systems for counting these things, has generated precisely nothing.

That evening Jo hands Sam a letter. Another year in the flat, at a higher rent. He turns it over to work out what their pay will cover. Jo has already found a cheaper place. “Read the bus times before you fall in love with it,” she says. The early one would get her to work. Coming back she'd miss the connection by six minutes. “I'd like to get home in time to eat with you. Can we put that on your bit of paper too?” Sam moves his thumb to give her room. She writes down when the next bus comes.

For once the question in front of him has the decency to come with a number.
