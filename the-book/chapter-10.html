Chapter 10Your Job Is Not Your Soul

“I’m a technical author,” Sam says. His niece Nora, who saw the whole performance, says, “He’s a duck.”

Both answers are accurate. Only one of them goes on a mortgage application.

Fran has come from next door to borrow a folding chair. She has flour on her sleeve from Dan’s kitchen and a coffee she hadn’t planned to stay for. “A technical author,” she says. “What do you write?”

“The little book that comes with the furniture.”

“Oh, God,” she says. “I owe you an apology.”

Everybody who hears what Sam does has either lost the little book or sworn at the person who wrote it. He likes this part of the conversation. The other person has spent a Sunday on the floor among pieces of a dresser. He knows the floor. For a moment they have somewhere to meet.

He likes the half second before it more. The title is his own. The company’s word for him is documentation. He says author and leaves a gap, and in the gap people are free to picture a tweed jacket, a distant gaze, a drink at eleven. He makes good manuals. He also knows exactly how long a person can wait before supplying the second word.

Nora wants paper. Sam produces a business card from his wallet. She turns it over and draws the duck while he demonstrates the left wing. When she gives it back, the animal looks as if it’s been in a bar fight and lost. She’s pleased with the likeness. Sam puts the card away.

Fran is fifty-three and works three mornings a week as a pharmacy technician. The chair is for her father, Walt, who is eighty-one, lives with her now and needs somewhere to sit while he changes his shoes. She’s ordered a hallway bench. Until it arrives, getting him out of the house involves dragging a dining chair to the front door while he tells her with increasing heat that he was putting on his own shoes before she was born.

Sam hears three mornings a week and concludes that she has the other four to herself. All the work of getting a second adult dressed, fed and down the front steps fits neatly into the pause after pharmacy. It fits there because nobody pays her for it. Hand Walt’s shoes to an employee and, without disturbing a shoelace, the family would have produced a job.

A perfectly good use for a title

If you smell burning on a plane and someone asks whether there’s a pilot on board, that’s a poor time to say we are all so much more than our professions. A job title lets you draw on years of somebody’s practice before you know their name.

It can matter to the person who holds it, too. Sam remembers the first instruction he wrote that helped anybody. A customer had called about a drawer that wouldn’t close. Sam had the fancy sectional drawing open on his monitor. The customer had a wooden box stuck halfway into another wooden box and a phone wedged between his ear and his shoulder. After a couple of barren minutes on the orientation of the left runner, Sam asked him to slide a finger along the metal until he felt a little wheel. Was the wheel near his knee or near the wall? A pause. A rolling sound. The drawer went in.

Sam went home and told Jo about that sound. He couldn’t really convey it. It had been a tiny noise over a bad connection, followed by a man laughing because he could finally put his socks away. The pleasure was in finding the sentence that worked from the other end of the phone. Sam would happily do that until he’s old. He would also, should the salary stop, drop it for whatever pays for groceries. Both facts deserve to survive the discussion.

Work like that has a property home life lacks. It tells you when you’re done. A drawer goes in and a man laughs. At home Sam can listen to Jo for forty minutes and be left with a damp dish towel and a matter that will come up again on Thursday. Given the choice, he’s been known to send one more email from the sofa in the name of keeping them fed. He has an excellent reason every time. Jo has the count.

The two screws

On Monday morning Sam’s department tries out a new way of producing instructions. A program takes the manufacturer’s parts list, assembly notes and CAD files, adds the house style, and returns a first draft, words and exploded drawings both. The sample product is a low oak bench for a narrow hall. Walt could sit on it. Sam would prefer that the first member of the public to try it be a gentleman whose daughter he hasn’t met.

The draft arrives while the coffee is brewing. It’s good. Worse, it’s good in a way he recognizes. Whoever set up the house style fed the thing his old guides. He sees his habit of naming a part by what the reader can see from the floor. He sees the little pause he leaves before a difficult step. The operations manager reads a paragraph aloud and says it sounds exactly like him. Sam feels this in his chest like a hot drink.

Then, mercifully, step nine. Secure the rail after the seat has gone on. He’s found an error, and his first sensation is relief.

Imagine the customer who discovers that inaccessible screw on a Sunday evening. Sam ordinarily likes this person. At the moment he could kiss them on the mouth for the career opportunity presented by their ruined Sunday.

Ellen suggests they build it. Ellen is forty-five and has drawn the diagrams in every guide Sam has written. Since February, when the junior illustrator left and the company kept finding reasons not to hire another, she’s drawn all of them alone. Test builds are a Thursday job, and neither of them wants to wait for Thursday. The two of them carry the box down to the workshop, clip on the first-name tags the company requires down there, and spend twenty minutes on their knees. They fit the legs, slot the narrow rail across the frame and put on the seat. Sam reaches for the two remaining screws. The holes he needs are directly underneath a handsome expanse of oak.

Ellen lifts the seat. He looks into the gap. The drawing on the screen shows the screw holes beautifully. Of course it does. For clarity the drawing leaves out the seat.

A small audience has come down to watch. Sam enjoys the way the room goes quiet. He sets the two screws on the oak and points at them. “Who trained this thing?”

Ellen takes a printed guide off the shelf, the one they published two years ago for an earlier version of the bench. The steps run in the same order. At the foot of the last page is the box for final sign-off. APPROVED BY, it says in print, and after that, in his own ballpoint, SAM. The same three letters are clipped to his shirt. The program has no arms. If it had, it would be holding the page up for him. It’s a difficult moment for those of us who came to watch humanity triumph over the machine. Humanity would like to take an early lunch.

Ellen picks the screws up off the seat and sets them on the old page so it won’t curl. Sam can read his name in the gap between them. The drawing on that page is hers, and it leaves out the seat too. Neither of them mentions it. Only one of them signed.

A program can make a real difference to some of the writing people are paid for. In a 2023 experiment, Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang gave professional writing tasks to 453 college-educated people and randomly gave half of them access to ChatGPT. Average completion time fell by 40 percent and rated quality rose by 18 percent. Those were short assignments with outputs a grader could score. The experiment can’t say what happens to a furniture company’s instructions over ten years.[1]

The operations manager runs her own experiment for six weeks, with a stopwatch. A small guide has always taken about eight staff-hours. Two are Sam’s first draft. Three are Ellen’s drawings. Three are the part nobody admires: questions to the manufacturer, the test build, revisions. With the tool, Sam’s two hours become one. Ellen’s three become one, because the program pulls its exploded views straight out of the CAD files and all she has to do is clean them up. The guide now takes five hours. Three saved out of eight is 37.5 percent, and two of the three are hers. Ten guides go out in an ordinary week, so thirty hours of drawing have become ten.

Those are this company’s hours. The 40 percent belongs to the people in the experiment. And the stopwatch has a blind side. It runs at a desk and stops when the file leaves the building. The three checking hours look the same as before, but they’re the hours whose whole product is that nothing goes wrong on somebody’s living-room floor, and nothing is hard to time. The screwdriver travels on without the clock.

The junior illustrator’s chair is still by the coat rack. No little brass plate marks where somebody might by now have been learning to read a sectional drawing upside down. The chair won’t write a furious account of its layoff. It’s quietly filled up with coats. An occupation can change shape that way, with nobody in the room handed anything. The next change needs a form.

The form reaches Sam on a Friday. Hours saved per guide: three. Errors caught before print: one. At the bottom is the box, because the pilot can’t become the process without the name of the person responsible for the content, and that’s him. The manager has clipped on a sample of the tool’s latest draft with a note. Honestly can’t tell which ones are yours anymore. She means it as praise and he takes it as praise.

He doesn’t sign for the three hours. He signs because the thing writes like him. Somebody took every sentence he ever published and built a machine that treats them as the correct way to speak, and now he’s being asked whether the machine is any good. It’s hard to vote against your own voice, particularly when it’s up for promotion. He does notice that the line for hours saved doesn’t say whose. He tells himself they’ll still need Ellen on Thursdays. He writes SAM in the box. I’d like to say I would have asked. Show me a machine that has plainly read everything I ever wrote and I’ll hold the door for it.

A woman whose job involved dots

The name at the bottom of the page and the person who did the looking have been coming apart for a long time. At Harvard College Observatory, Henrietta Swan Leavitt worked with photographs of the sky on glass. To catch a star changing brightness she compared plates taken on different nights. The dots were many, the exposures differed, and an interesting dot might sit beside several others. Long before anybody had a grand thought about the universe, she had to be certain she was looking at the same damned dot. Harvard’s plate collection still preserves the materials from that work.[2]

In 1912 the observatory published Leavitt’s measurements of twenty-five variable stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud. She had arranged them by how long each took to cycle through its changes in brightness, and a pattern showed up. Among these stars, the ones with longer periods tended to be brighter. Since stars in the same cloud are at roughly the same distance from Earth, the difference in observed brightness was a clue to a difference in the light they actually gave off.[3]

A dim dot leaves you asking whether the star is faint or only far away. With Leavitt’s relationship, once calibrated, a Cepheid’s rhythm helps answer that, and so helps you measure how far away the thing is.[3], [4]

Look at who gets which end of the 1912 circular. Leavitt is credited in its opening sentence. The name at the bottom is the director’s, Edward C. Pickering.[3] It’s satisfying to give her her due. Less satisfying is the bargain you can accidentally offer the woman at the next desk. Keep all those glass plates straight, put in a career, and if you’d kindly discover a law of the universe on the way out we’ll concede that your life amounted to something.

Try that deal on Ellen, whose drawings have never carried her name. A guide has one name on it, and it’s in the box. Or try it across the pharmacy counter. Fran will be entitled to some pride in a shift well worked if she discovers a new antibiotic on her lunch break. The right answer to an offer like that is to take the full lunch break.

When Fran sits on the floor to help Walt into his shoes, somebody else could do the practical part. Some mornings she fervently wishes somebody would. But Walt is embarrassed about his swollen feet in a particular way around his daughter, because he can remember holding her feet in the palm of one hand. She knows which of his old jokes lets him accept the help. A paid caregiver could come to know him well and build something of their own. No job description could move those earlier mornings from one childhood into another.

What gets to be particular about you has a great deal to do with time. You happened to be there, you paid attention, you acquired a history with somebody. A skill can be copied, and Sam’s now has been. The program can produce his sentence about the little wheel. It can’t have gone home that night and failed to describe the sound to Jo.

The bench arrives at Fran’s a few weeks into the pilot. Different company, different hardware. She puts it together herself. The next time Sam is at Dan’s, Walt is sitting in the doorway working his left heel into a shoe. Fran starts to bend and catches herself. He’s got it. She stands there watching a piece of work she no longer has to do. A job has just disappeared, and because nobody was paid for it this counts as a small triumph. By the usual systems of counting it has produced nothing at all.

What you lose on a Monday morning

Three Mondays after Sam signs, Ellen is asked to step into the small conference room at nine-thirty. It’s the room they book on Thursdays to write up the test builds. The operations manager is there with a woman from human resources and a folder. The position of technical illustrator has been eliminated. Her last day is the Friday after next. The company is grateful for her years of service, a number the woman from human resources has to look down to find. It expects to need somebody to check the program’s drawings, paid by the job, and hopes she’ll think about it. In the meantime, could she write up how she checks one, in case somebody else has to? The meeting takes eleven minutes. In the folder is a sheet headed Navigating Your Transition.

So Ellen will spend her last two weeks writing the instructions for doing without her. They’ll need an editor. The company has one.

Sam hears at ten. The manager stops by his desk to say it was a hard call and the numbers made it for them. He waits until she’s gone and opens the pilot file. He knows what’s at the bottom of the form. He looks anyway, the way you press a bruise. APPROVED BY SAM.

His first feeling, ahead of any of the decent ones, is relief that the room was booked for Ellen and not for him. His second is a quick audit. Two drafting hours became one, and nobody has asked yet what he does with the other. His third is about Ellen. He notices the order they arrived in.

Take a job from somebody and you take more than the line she uses at parties. At nine-thirty on a Monday Ellen knew whose coffee would be going cold by the printer. Some part of every week, somebody was waiting for a thing only she knew how to finish. Even the colleague who has been telling the same rental-car story since 2011 was a dependable human presence at twelve-ten. She may find, to her private disgust, that she misses the story, and that she never did learn which exit he took.

Then the future tense goes. In the fall we’re moving upstairs. Wait till you see the new plotter. She had a place reserved in things that hadn’t happened yet. People will send her photographs from upstairs, out of affection. It’ll be like getting postcards from a country where she used to live and whose language she apparently taught half the population.

And the money, which is no philosophical mistake. Money is the afternoon off to take a parent to an appointment. It’s the option of leaving an apartment with mold behind the bed. The sheet in the folder suggests she use this time to explore interests outside work. By Wednesday two colleagues have separately mentioned pottery. It’s striking how often the recommended response to losing an income is to buy a wheel.

The hour to watch is the one late at night when a true sentence about the world turns into a false one about a person. I’ve lost the people who read my drawings is true. I’m scared about the rent is true. Run them together at three in the morning and out comes I’m a burden, a forecast about next month’s bills used to settle how much room a body is entitled to take up. Ellen wouldn’t let a friend say it. Decent people keep a private exemption from the kindness they extend to everybody else. Their friends can go broke and still come over on Sunday. They themselves are on a provisional contract, subject to quarterly review.

Soon somebody will ask Ellen what she does and the usual answer won’t be available. She’ll still be the person who said build it and lifted the seat. That goes home with her, along with a working knowledge of what a stranger on a floor can’t see. None of it pays the electric bill, which is the trouble with being more than your job. The surplus comes in a currency nobody takes.

The part you take home

One objection, before anybody gets comfortable. A job makes a fine alibi. Somebody kept the old drawing in circulation. Somebody decided the junior chair would go on holding coats. Somebody signed. It would take nerve for any of them to drive home on Friday and announce that his real self begins at the front door. If what a man does between nine and five decides whether another person gets paid, she’s entitled to an opinion about who he is during those hours. The job isn’t Sam’s soul. The handwriting in the box is still his.

He doesn’t go to the manager. He works out what he’d say, and every version ends with her asking whether he’s offering his own hours instead, and he isn’t.

At lunch he goes to Ellen’s desk and tells her he signed the form. He tells her because she’ll see it, and because he would like to be told it’s all right.

“I know,” she says. “Your name’s on everything.”

He says he’s sorry. It’s true, and it takes about a second. She says, “I know you are,” which he’ll spend some weeks trying to hear as forgiveness. Then she opens her drawer and hands him the two screws from the bench. “Keep these on your desk. It still can’t see under the seat.”

The Thursday hour goes with her. It’s how the two of them became friends without ever arranging to be friends. From now on they’ll have to do what other adults do and each propose three incompatible dates for a drink until one of them dies.

In the parking lot that evening he takes out a business card to write something down and gets the wrong side. He’s looking at the duck. He’d forgotten that Nora gave it a little stitched patch on its bad wing. The original performer never supplied that detail. She’s been attending to a slightly different duck all these years. He sends her a photograph and asks what happened to the wing.

The reply comes right back. He fell off his chair at work. A second message follows. He works at a pond.

Even the damn duck has a job.

At home he tells Jo about Ellen, and then about the box. She still has her coat on. “Did you know they were her hours?”

“I knew they were hours.”

Jo doesn’t help him with that. She hands him a letter. Another year in the apartment, at a higher rent.
