Chapter 04The Universe Owes You Absolutely Nothing

At 3:40 in the morning Mara is in a hospital corridor with her father’s glasses in her pocket. He died twenty minutes ago. Mara wears glasses herself. Nobody has come to collect his, or to explain what a person does with a second pair for the rest of her life. Down the hall her mother sits in the family waiting room with Mara’s aunt and a form on a clipboard. Neither of them reads much English, and the interpreter went home at midnight. Outside the window at the end of the corridor a traffic light changes from red to green. No cars go through.

Mara is a pharmacist. She explains fine print to strangers all day, and she could read that form in ninety seconds. What she can’t do is say it. She grew up answering her mother in English. Her stock of her mother’s language covers the kitchen, the weather and, thanks to work, twice a day with food. It has no word for autopsy.

She keeps coming back to one plain, furious sentence. This should not have happened.

She could mean that somebody missed something. That’s a question about the care he got, and she has every right to ask it. She could mean that people who love each other ought to get more time. Nobody in this building can do anything about the second meaning. A complete and accurate account of how her father died would still leave her holding the glasses.

The vending machine by the elevators takes her quarters and gives her nothing. Mara hits the front of it once, hard, with the flat of her hand. A bag of chips drops into the tray. Somewhere in her there’s still an animal for whom anger now and then gets a result. The animal’s record is mixed. In 1995 the Consumer Product Safety Commission knew of at least thirty-seven people killed since 1978 by soda machines they’d rocked for a free can or their money back.[1] Mara stopped at one, which is within the therapeutic range. A person could spend a lifetime acquiring that expectation, one obliging machine at a time, and then one night come up against the size of the thing that won’t give.

Her whole trade runs on the expectation. Eight hours a day she hands people a promise in a small paper bag. Take as directed. The leaflet stapled to the bag lists what might go wrong, in order of likelihood, and she has read a thousand of them. None ends with a line saying that the patient may do everything right and die anyway at 3:20 on a weeknight.

Look at the two things that have gone wrong for her in the last half hour. When the machine keeps her money, Mara can say it owes her, and she’s right. It has a posted price, an owner and a sticker with a phone number for complaints. Her father’s death has none of the three. Put a phone number on the side of the Milky Way and most of us would have a few things ready to say. Until somebody does, the numbers that matter tonight are the ones in Mara’s phone. She texted one of them at 3:25.

Did he smoke?

I should say what I’d be doing if I were standing in that corridor, because it isn’t pretty. I’d be running a background check on the dead man. Did he smoke? How old was he, exactly? Sometimes a real question is in there. Mostly I’m shopping for the detail that will let me finish my cheeseburger in peace. Somebody has lost her father, and I’d like some sign that mine is exempt.

Picture two people in two rowboats. One boat is filling up, and its owner is bailing as fast as he can. The owner of the other is standing with a clipboard, taking careful notes on the leak. He hasn’t looked down at his own shoes in a while.

Mara is running the same check on herself, and she’s better equipped for it than I am. She knows every pill her father took. She filled most of them. Standing by the machine with a bag of chips she doesn’t want, she goes down his list in order. The blood thinner. The two for his pressure. The one for his stomach, which she wondered about in March and then didn’t. She’s been through the list twice. She’ll go through it again, because the alternative is worse. If she missed something, then fathers die of mistakes, and mistakes can be caught. It’s a terrible offer, and tonight she’d take it. She’d rather it were her fault than nobody’s.

She’d like to see for herself, and physics will go a surprising distance with her. A perfect mirror parked ten light-years away would send back light that left here twenty years ago, and with a good enough telescope she could watch her father in the backyard setting out two cups for lunch, though to make out the cups the mirror would have to be wider than the orbit of Mars.[2], [3], [4] She could watch him pour. She couldn’t ask him what he wanted to drink. Two wishes have been traveling together all night. One is to know what happened, and a record can sometimes help with that. The other is to be with him. It seems cruel to say how far apart those two are. It would be crueler to sell her a bigger telescope.

All Saints’ Day

People have been taking notes on other people’s leaks for a long time. The most famous case began in Lisbon, in church.

On the morning of November 1, 1755, at about twenty minutes to ten, most of Lisbon was at Mass. It was All Saints’ Day. The churches were full and the candles were lit. The earthquake brought the roofs down on the congregations. People who got out ran for the open ground by the harbor, and about forty minutes later the sea came in over it. The candles had tipped, and fire took much of what was left. Tens of thousands of people died. The records were poor, and nobody has ever settled the number.[5]

Then came the explanations, and they had an awkward fact to work around, which was where the dead had been kneeling. Preachers said the city had been punished for its sins. A Jesuit named Gabriel Malagrida published a pamphlet on the true cause of the earthquake, and the true cause was the anger of God.[6] In a house outside Geneva, Voltaire got the news and wrote to a friend wondering what the preachers would say now, especially if the palace of the Inquisition turned out to be still standing.[7]

The fashionable philosophy of the day had a smoother line than the preachers. This was the best of all possible worlds, and every private misery served some general good. “Whatever is, is right,” Alexander Pope had written. Within weeks Voltaire had a long poem against that sentence. It came out in 1756 with a subtitle that states his business, an examination of the axiom “All is well.” Did Lisbon have more vices, he asks, than London or Paris? Lisbon is in ruins, and in Paris they’re dancing.[8] Three years later he put the earthquake into Candide, where a philosopher named Pangloss explains to the survivors that it’s all for the best.[9]

In August 1756 Rousseau wrote him a long letter back. Nature, he pointed out, hadn’t stacked twenty thousand houses six and seven stories high on that spot. If the people had spread out and built lower, the damage would have been far smaller.[10] It’s the man with the clipboard, in better prose. Annoyingly, he had half a point, and somebody in Lisbon had already found the useful half. In January 1756 the king’s minister, the man later known as the Marquis of Pombal, sent the country’s bishops thirteen questions for their parish priests to answer. What time did the earthquake begin? How long did it last?[11] That form asked what happened. It didn’t ask whose sin it was. Downtown Lisbon went back up with a timber cage inside the walls of its buildings, meant to flex and hold in the next one.[12]

So Lisbon is where I’d send anybody who thinks the world keeps moral accounts. The ground took the faithful at prayer on a holy day, and no version of “all is well” has known what to do with that since.

The earthquake shows that the ground owed Lisbon nothing. It doesn’t show that nobody owed Lisbon anything. The thirteen questions and the timber cages came out of the same disaster, and they were people deciding what they owed each other and whoever would live there next. A poem can’t make that decision. Somebody has to want the walls to hold. And none of this is for Mara. Tens of thousands of dead in 1755 don’t make one man in a hospital bed any smaller, and anyone who hands her Lisbon tonight is the fellow in the dry boat. I brought it for you and me.

The interpreter went home at midnight

Mara goes back to the family waiting room. It has a box of tissues, a painting of a sailboat and a laminated card about parking validation. Her mother is holding the pen the way you’d hold something that belongs to somebody else. Her aunt has put on her reading glasses to look at a page in a language she can’t read. The form has eleven boxes. It would like to know about an autopsy. It would like the name of a funeral home, from a woman who was married until half an hour ago. Twice now a man from the night staff has stuck his head in to ask whether it’s ready.

Mara puts the chips on the table, since she can’t think what else to do with them. Her aunt, who hasn’t eaten since noon, opens the bag. Then Mara tries the first box herself. She gets as far as they want to know if and runs out of language. What’s left is the vocabulary of a seven-year-old asking for more rice. Her mother pats her hand, which is worse.

Nearly three centuries ago David Hume noticed a small trick in moral arguments. A writer goes along describing how things are and then, with no change of tone, starts telling you how they ought to be.[13] Try it on this room. The interpreter went home at midnight. So the family should sign the form without understanding it. Something went missing between those two sentences.

Supply the missing piece and watch what happens. If people ought to understand what they’re signing, the interpreter’s dark office is a reason to find another interpreter, or to let the form wait until morning. If what matters is getting the clipboard back before the shift changes, the same dark office points the other way. The hour of the night, the eleven boxes and the pen are facts. They’ll sit there until the sun comes up without choosing between those two. Somebody has to want something.

In this case somebody already did, and wrote it down. Under federal civil rights rules, a hospital that takes federal money owes people who don’t read English an interpreter, promptly and at no charge.[14] Nobody found that in the sky. People argued for it, and it’s exactly as strong as the people on shift. At four in the morning the rule says promptly and the door says 9 to midnight.

So the wanting has a price. An interpreter by phone costs the hospital money. Somebody has to stay on hold for one, and tonight that somebody has other rooms. The nurse who was with Mara’s father at the end is standing at the desk with a receiver wedged against her shoulder, writing in a chart with her free hand. She’s been on hold since ten to four.

A cynic has a theory about her. She’s protecting the hospital, or herself. It could be true. Notice, though, how well the theory performs without anybody having to look at the nurse. It would explain her just as neatly if she’d hung up. A theory that has every decent act accounted for in advance is one more dry boat. Its owner stays out of the water and never finds out what people do.

What the nurse is doing is hope in a form you could photograph. A tired woman, a phone, hold music. For once in this corridor, whether somebody answers could change what happens next.

Two paper cups

Mara’s friend arrives at ten past four carrying two paper cups. She stopped at the gas station on the way, because she has had this hospital’s coffee and didn’t think tonight needed it. She also has the first half of a sentence about everything happening for a reason. It’s Pope’s line, cut down to fit a sympathy card. Then she sees that Mara has hooked one arm of her father’s glasses over the neck of her T-shirt, to keep both hands free, and the sentence stays where it is. She sits down next to her on the bench outside the family waiting room.

After a while Mara asks which one is tea.

Her friend turns one of the cups so the mark on the lid faces Mara. They sit there a while longer.

When somebody says they don’t know what to do in a room like this, I believe them. I also know what’s hiding in my own version of the sentence. I’d like the woman whose father has just died to find me an assignment I can excel at. Mara has already handed out the only job there is. She asked which cup had the tea. The rest of us will have to live with being overqualified.

The sentence her friend swallowed would have been an explanation, and every explanation offered in this corridor wants the same thing from Mara. It wants her to agree that tonight makes sense. The preachers wanted that from Lisbon. I wanted it from a dead man’s smoking habits. Mara wants it from a list of prescriptions. The tea doesn’t want anything.

It would be easy to conclude that the opposite of explaining is watching. The man with the clipboard, told that leaks have no moral, can go right on standing in his boat, and now he gets to feel like a realist. The universe owes you nothing is true of the ground under Lisbon. It’s true of whatever stopped Mara’s father’s heart. Said by one person to another, across a counter or a kitchen table, it’s usually somebody declining to get wet.

Her friend has no theory about any of it. She has the other cup, and she’s on the bench.

What the form wanted

At 4:35 the nurse comes down the hall with her own cell phone held flat on her palm, on speaker. The desk phone doesn’t reach the waiting room, so when a person finally came on the line she gave him her number. A man’s voice says good morning in Mara’s mother’s language. Somewhere behind him a dog is barking. Mara’s mother sits up straight. Nobody in this building has spoken to her in whole sentences since midnight.

He takes the form one box at a time. Mara follows along in English and hears, in a stranger’s voice, the words she didn’t have. So that’s autopsy. Her mother asks him to say it again. Then she says no.

Mara knows what’s in that box. Her list is in it. It’s the one way anybody would ever check, drug by drug, whether the pharmacist in the family missed something. She has the English to object. She doesn’t have the box. It belongs to the woman who was married to him, and that woman wants him left alone. Hume said you’d need a want, and here’s one. It settles the matter. Mara can still ask the doctors on Monday what they saw, and she has every right to. The answer her list was after isn’t coming.

The funeral home takes longer. The aunt knows of one, and the interpreter spells it for the nurse. The last box is belongings. One watch, the man reads. One wallet. One pair of eyeglasses. Mara knows that word. It’s a kitchen word. He was always leaving them by the stove.

The nurse has been through the plastic bag twice. Mara’s mother looks at her daughter’s collar. Then she signs for three things, two of them in a bag, and hands back the pen.

The nurse thanks the man on the phone. He says he’s sorry for their loss, in both languages, and goes back to his dog. She picks up the clipboard. Her shift ends at seven. It started at seven. She has spent forty-five minutes of it, one sixteenth, on hold for a family she’ll never see again. No box on the form records that.

Sure

At a quarter past five they’re all out on the sidewalk. Her aunt is taking her mother home and staying. The light changes again. Still no cars.

Her friend asks if she should drive. Mara wants to drive her own car. She also wants somebody behind her the whole way, because she doesn’t trust herself to unlock her front door and walk in alone. She can’t find a way to say the second part out loud. Her friend says she’ll be right behind her, and goes to get her keys out of a cup holder full of gas station napkins.

Nothing in the sky requires that of anyone. The universe that owed her father nothing owes Mara no headlights in the mirror. What she has in their place is a promise, a human invention you can’t weigh on any scale in her pharmacy. Break a promise to follow somebody home from the hospital at five in the morning, though, and you can find its consequences sitting in a parked car.

A promise won’t fix the hospital. Tomorrow Mara will have a drawer to open and a pair of glasses to put somewhere. But a promise covers the eleven minutes from the curb to her kitchen, and that’s the trip she can’t make alone.

“Will you come up?” she texts from the driver’s seat, still parked.

The reply is one word. “Sure.”

Tonight Mara knows exactly what those four letters mean. One night in October the same word will light up a different phone across town and cost its owner the whole evening.
