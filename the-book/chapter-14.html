Chapter 14You Have Less Time Than You Think

On the lid of the film can, in her mother’s handwriting, it says Fran falls in pond. No date, no location. The family archivist knew what would bring in the crowds.

Fran tips the reel out onto the table. She’s fifty-three. Her father, Walt, is eighty-one, and he remembers the pond. It belonged to a man who kept ornamental fish, although by the time they got Fran out there was very little ornamental about any of them. He can’t remember whether the film shows her going in. Somebody may have done the decent thing and put down the camera.

“Would you mind telling that story again while I record it?” she asks.

“Why?”

“So we’ve got it.”

“We’ve got you. You were right in the fucking pond.”

She’s been clearing the last boxes from his old place. Since he came to live with her she’s bought a bench for the hallway and learned which mornings he wants a hand and which mornings he’ll swat the hand away. She has also come to regard her father as a closing window for historical research. Only he knows the names of the people in these films. She’s bought small sticky labels. She has a list of thirty-one questions. This is what happens when a pharmacy technician is allowed to bring her professional strengths to bear on the extinction of everything she loves.

As Fran writes, something made of stainless steel descends toward the box. It has two handles and a number of small holes through which the inhabitants of a distant planet have traditionally drained spaghetti. Walt has hung the colander upside down on a piece of thread.

“Does that look like a spaceship to you?”

“Depends what their defense budget is.”

While she’s been sorting the films he shot fifty years ago, he’s been wondering whether he might make another one.

In six weeks, on a Sunday evening in late September, the neighborhood is holding a film night in the courtyard behind the community center. Anybody can submit something up to three minutes long, handed in at the center by the Monday before the show. Fran is one of the three suckers with a key who volunteered to organize it. Walt wants to enter a picture about a visitor from space who has crossed the appalling black distances between stars in search of one striped sock. He has worked out how to suspend the spaceship above the clothesline. What he needs from his daughter, apart from the sock, is a performance of sufficient gravity to make the audience believe a second sock is at imminent risk.

She’s touched. She also has twenty-eight cans to get through and labels on four of them. She puts a label on the colander to remind herself it belongs in the kitchen.

“There’s no hurry on our thing, is there?” she asks.

He gives the spaceship a little more slack.

How long do you have?

Suppose I ask how long you have left with somebody you love. If it’s an older parent you might do a quick, furtive piece of actuarial work. Subtract their age from the age at which their toughest aunt finally went into the ground. Add a couple of years for giving up cigarettes. Knock a few off for everything they put on toast. The Social Security Administration will do the math properly if you ask. Its tables give a man of eighty-one, on average, between seven and eight more years.[1] Call it four hundred Sundays. That average covers every man of eighty-one in the country, the ones who’ll bury their cardiologists and the ones who won’t see the end of the month, and it has no way of telling Fran which kind is holding the thread. Four hundred is also a number so comfortable that nobody has ever done anything on account of it.

Now suppose I ask how many afternoons you and that person are going to spend making a movie about one pair of socks. That sounds absurdly specific. Good. You can actually begin to answer it.

Fran writes the six Sundays before the deadline on a sheet of paper. She and her dad could do an hour and a half on a Sunday afternoon. Six times ninety is 540 minutes. Nine hours to produce a three-minute masterpiece.

The second Sunday is gone before she starts. Mara, the pharmacist who sat in a hospital corridor this summer with her dead father’s glasses in her pocket, buried him last week, and Fran is covering every shift a technician can cover, Sunday inventory included. On the fourth she’s seeing a friend who’s driving two hours to get here. Walt looks over her shoulder and puts a finger on the fifth. That afternoon he has a ballgame. He’s going next door to watch it with Dan, who has recently bought a television large enough to show a third-base coach’s poor life choice forming behind his eyes.

“You could record it.”

“I could record you and watch the game live.”

That leaves three afternoons they’re both willing and able to give the film. Four and a half hours. Half of the original nine went away without an accident, a medical emergency or an act of God. The fifth Sunday disappeared because a man would like to have a beer next door and express himself freely about the bullpen. That counts. His remaining time belongs to him, including the part his daughter thinks he could spend more memorably.

Today is the first of the three. They’ve spent forty minutes with the cans. They could go on for another fifty, and nobody could call it a wasted afternoon. If they do, the film gets two sessions, three hours altogether. Fran does the math twice, the way she counts tablets. Nobody has received a diagnosis. A quantity that felt enormous in the unit six weeks is now small enough to be lost because nobody thought to charge a phone. It hasn’t escaped her that the four hundred are made of the same Sundays.

A lot of what passes for the wisdom of mortality changes the unit in the other direction. How can you worry about a petty slight when your life is an eyeblink in the history of the cosmos? Easily, it turns out. You can explain thirteen-odd billion years to a man at breakfast and he’ll still have room in him to resent whoever put an empty jar back in the fridge. A vast number can make him feel magnificently small. It can’t settle who’s going to buy mustard.

A tiny number does strange things too. Tell Fran she might not have many Sundays left with her father and see what sort of daughter she tries to become. Every trip he makes to the kitchen is now potentially the last decent conversation. He comes in looking for the little batteries and is met by the face of a woman determined to make this one count. Imagine needing two AAAs under that sort of pressure.

She’s already been doing something like it. She’s asked about the day he met her mother three times this month. He’s told the story three times, patiently, with small changes in the weather. The last time, halfway through, he asked if she’d ever wondered why movie aliens land in Washington so often, given the easier parking elsewhere. She steered him gently back to the bus stop where he first saw her mother. If this does turn out to be one of the last good afternoons, he might like some say in what counted.

Two hundred seconds

Walt holds one of the old reels up to the light. They were shooting on Super 8. A standard fifty-foot cartridge holds about 3,600 frames. At eighteen frames a second that lasts 200 seconds, three minutes and twenty. At twenty-four it lasts 150, two and a half minutes.[2] It’s one of the few household systems in which demanding that things move faster has an immediate and honest effect on the time available. There’s less of it.

When a three-year-old is considering whether to fall into a pond, those fifty seconds have artistic consequences. Hold the trigger down while she’s considering and you may run out of film before she’s made up her mind. Release it and you can be certain she’ll take the plunge in the gap. Working with children introduces a malevolent intelligence into an otherwise tractable engineering problem.

The cartridge had a physical end, so every press of the trigger chose which stretch of an afternoon went through the gate. On a phone Walt could point the camera at the pond for half an hour, and somebody would sit through the water later, hunting for the one wet child. Saving everything for somebody else to sort out is the attic method. Fill it, then die before anybody can ask you to help.

Fran has done this with pictures of her own. Her phone holds eight photographs of one birthday cake and fourteen more in which a fork has appeared. They’re all in vigorous health on a server somewhere. Years from now somebody may dearly wish to see the face of the woman who baked that cake. As far as the photographs are concerned, it was produced by a fork.

The boring parts deserve their keep all the same. One of the reels in the box is labeled Garden, nothing much. Walt had it transferred years ago. Fran puts the file on the television. A woman in a cardigan shakes dirt from a bunch of radishes, one quick movement away from her shoes. Walt has begun to look past the radishes, toward something near the edge of the picture. Fran watches his eyes, then looks where he’s looking. On a step at the back, her mother is trying to fasten a sandal with one hand while she eats something with the other. It’s a private, ungainly little operation. Nobody thought it worth filming. It’s the part Walt plays twice.

How much more of that is in the box? Lots, maybe. Her mother is gone. Nobody can go out to the garden next summer and request a retake of the sandal business. Fran is right to want to keep the cans. She still has one hand on the box when her father asks if the kitchen gloves are supposed to smell like that.

She has a real choice. Her dad can tell her which garden this was while he still remembers. He can also be a guy on a bench with an improbable idea involving kitchen gloves. A daughter could spend so long collecting proof that a man once existed that for his last ten years she only ever meets him in the past tense.

“Hold on,” she says. “What’s the glove for?”

“The alien. I thought people would accept the space travel more readily if they didn’t have to accept my hand as well.”

She tries one on. Move three fingers and keep the other two still, and it has a surprisingly authoritative walk. They give the creature a practice run along the dish rack. It stops under the faucet to reconsider its mission. By the time it has investigated the dish towel they’ve been at it for twelve minutes. Nothing has been recorded. Fran notices that she’s having a good time and immediately feels the familiar urge to get her phone, a development the creature observes with contempt from the far side of the toaster.

The gentleman who would miss the show

In 1716 Edmond Halley published instructions for doing something spectacular with a few minutes that belonged to people in the future. From Earth, on the rare occasions when everything lines up, Venus crosses the face of the Sun as a small dark spot. Observers at widely separated places see the planet trace slightly different paths against the solar disk. Halley proposed timing how long the crossing took from different sites. From the differences, the geometry of the observations and the known relative sizes of the planetary orbits, astronomers could work toward a distance to the Sun in terrestrial units.[3]

You can get the beginnings of the idea without a planet. Hold your thumb at arm’s length and look at it with one eye, then the other. It jumps against the far wall. Give the astronomers a baseline the size of a good chunk of the Earth, exchange the thumb for Venus and make sure somebody brings a clock. More mathematics lies between there and a distance to the Sun than it would be polite to hide in the word just.

The transit he planned for was due in 1761, with its twin in 1769. Subtract 1716 from 1761. Even if the Royal Society had been unusually generous about conference travel, Halley was unlikely to be at the eyepiece. The man writing the instructions was already approaching sixty. He made it to eighty-five, an outstanding performance for 1742 that still left him nineteen years short. It wasn’t the only appointment he made and missed. He had predicted that the comet now named after him would come back in 1758. It did, on schedule, sixteen years after his funeral.[4] In the paper he took particular care over where other people might go. He wanted observations from several places, partly because he had considered a set of astronomical bodies of modest size which could nevertheless wreck the project. Clouds.

One of the astronomers who went out for the transits was Guillaume Le Gentil, and clouds were only part of his trouble. He sailed from France to catch the 1761 transit at Pondicherry, in India, and found the British had taken the town. Venus crossed the Sun while he was still at sea, on a rolling deck where nothing could be timed. So he stayed in the region for the twin. Eight years. He wanted to watch from Manila and was ordered back to Pondicherry. On the day, it rained in Pondicherry. Manila was clear. When he got home in 1771 he learned he’d been declared dead, and it cost him a great deal of money to establish otherwise.[5] The weather settled whether he got an observation. He’d settled in advance that he’d be there to try. Fran, who has volunteered to screen films outdoors in a city where rain sometimes comes sideways, will shortly have occasion to appreciate the distinction.

Halley’s proposal was still worth making. Nobody knows whether he enjoyed the morning he wrote it. Maybe he had a toothache. He still had something more interesting to do than count the dawns he personally had left. He could hand strangers forty-five years off a piece of work he wanted done, in an enterprise whose result he’d never receive. Most of us manage the opposite. We leave one small unmarked key and enjoy the posthumous destruction of an entire weekend.

Fran’s sticky labels are Halley’s kind of work, notes for whoever opens the box after the one man who knows the names can’t be asked. Walt has been offered the desk. He’s eighty-one, and he’d rather be at the eyepiece.

The afternoon nobody is allowed to save

On the second of their three Sundays the phone is fully charged. The weather is fine. Walt has prepared a list of shots on the back of a pharmacy bag. Fran gets as far as putting two kitchen gloves in her back pocket and then finds herself crying in the closet under the stairs. It’s cramped in there because it’s also the only place they’ve found for the vacuum.

Nothing terrible has happened. She has worked eleven of the last fourteen days. Yesterday she went to three stores to find the kind of socks her dad can put on by himself. She’s up twice most nights, and she can feel the creak of his bedroom door as a distinct event in the muscles of her back. For two weeks customers have asked after Mara, heard about her father, and then turned to look at Fran, who is known to have one of her own at home. On Wednesday a woman leaned across the counter and told her that one day she’d give anything to have these days again. Perfect. Fran can now fail at the difficult job of looking after her dad and at the additional job of enjoying it enough to satisfy a stranger’s preview of her grief.

She promised she’d go out with the gloves. The inconvenience of the tears is almost worse than the feeling behind them. He’s done nothing wrong. A good man is sitting in the hallway with a list, and she would pay actual money to spend an hour in a place where nobody is waiting pleasantly for her to come out.

He knocks on the closet door. “Is the vacuum in a meeting?”

“I want an hour to myself.”

“All right.”

She’s been in there long enough to imagine at least seven worse reactions. It hadn’t occurred to her that he might just go and put the radio on. A person can be inconsiderate that way, carelessly wasting several minutes of your best rehearsed grievance.

She makes a sandwich and takes it up to the little park. For a while she sits watching a pigeon fail to get purchase on a slippery metal sign. She doesn’t take a picture of it, learn from it or come home with renewed clarity about her priorities. The pigeon has a bad afternoon. She has a sandwich. If that’s hard to enter in the credit column of a human life, consider what sort of life you’re asking a person to live.

At home Walt has drawn a row of small boxes across the pharmacy bag. He’s done something clever with the last one. If the striped sock disappears up past the top of the phone screen, nobody needs to see what happens inside the spaceship. The audience will supply millions of dollars of special effects at its own expense. Underneath he’s written alien sighs. Fran would like to know how an alien sighs.

“Well, it’s come all this way and they don’t match.”

She sits down beside him. “I’ve got half an hour if you want to try the clothesline.”

They get the first shot. That’s it. The clock is firm on the matter. Next door, through an open window, Dan is giving an umpire the benefit of his experience. Somebody takes in a package. The end of the afternoon feels nothing like a montage of irreplaceable moments, and they’re both grateful.

Helene Fung, Laura Carstensen and Amy Lutz asked people to say, on paper, whose company they’d choose under different imagined time horizons. When the future was presented as limited, younger and older participants alike chose the familiar. Asked to imagine an expanded future, older participants lost the preference for familiar partners they’d shown in the ordinary condition.[6] Nobody’s date of birth changed between the questions. Only the road people pictured ahead changed, and that was enough to change who got a chair. A reminder of the end can send Fran to the phone to call the friend she’s been missing. It can also send her into the closet with one more obligation for the heap.

One question works better for her than What if Dad died tomorrow? She has had plenty of practice imagining that, thank you. At three o’clock, on their last clear Sunday, she takes her father his phone and asks, “Which shot do you actually want me in?”

He wants her face. Until now she’s assumed he only required the use of one of her hands. He wants the moment where she notices the sock is gone. He’s been trying, with no success, to get onto his own face the particular expression she makes when something ordinary has gone completely to hell. It’s fondness and exasperation and about thirty years of dealing with the public. Apparently the Earth’s last line of defense against an alien incursion looks a lot like a woman discovering the supplier has sent suppositories in a box labeled For external use.

“Who’s going to work the alien while I’m doing that?”

“Have you met Dan? Man’s had his hand up over the fence all week.”

A second and a half of Earth

The backyard is large enough for a clothesline, a bench and an aggrieved pigeon that appears to have taken the unusual step of changing parks to follow her. Walt gets comfortable on the bench and braces the phone in both hands. Fran crouches behind the laundry basket to send the gloved invader exploring along the line. Dan has come over from next door to manage the colander, and he holds the thread like a man who bills by the hour. They’ll change jobs for her close-up. The sun catches the metal just right and for a moment the entire interstellar mission looks convincing. Then the lid of a compost bin blows through the shot at a velocity well beyond the capabilities of their spacecraft.

They get it on the fourth take. The fifth is technically better but Walt likes the fourth, because for the tiniest instant before the alien lifts the sock, the fingers of the glove hesitate. Watching it, you begin to care whether this preposterous animal gets away. It appears to know that stealing is wrong. It also has several small cold feet at home.

Now she stands up. He turns the camera on her. She tells him she’s going to try a few different reactions to the theft, and on the third she hears a noise from behind the phone. It’s her father trying very hard not to laugh. There goes the face. He lets the camera run for a second and a half while she looks straight at him.

Late that night they sit down to edit. The final film comes to two minutes and eleven seconds. They call it The Other One. Walt wants to cut the part where she looks at him laughing. It breaks the fiction. Fran finds to her surprise that she agrees. She saves the outtake in a separate file, though. Some things she’d like people in folding chairs to see, and a second and a half of Earth she’d like for herself.

The deadline has cost them something. On paper they had nine hours. The film got a little over two of them. The cans got forty minutes and four labels, which leaves twenty-four that only one man alive can label. One of the twenty-four shows a side street Walt recognizes but can’t name. He gives it a try after dinner, and another the next morning. He can tell her it has two syllables and starts with a B. That much is normal. In 1966 the psychologists Roger Brown and David McNeill read people the definitions of rare dictionary words and found that those stuck with a word on the tip of the tongue could often give its first letter and its number of syllables anyway.[7] Walt is after a street from fifty years ago. He has the outline. The name doesn’t come.

A mean little story is available in which this proves she should have spent those Sundays on the archive after all. Run that argument in reverse. If they’d labeled everything and never made the film, no file would be sitting on the phone. No alien caught in the act of having a conscience. Nobody would know what had gone missing, and they’d be serenely confident they’d made the responsible choice.

She may regret it anyway. People do put off a question until the one person who knew the answer can’t be asked. If Fran is saving a conversation for a day when everybody is brave and the groceries have put themselves away, next Tuesday is worth a look. What the fact of death won’t tell her is whether next Tuesday belongs to the old footage, the new film, the friend driving two hours, or a sandwich nobody asks her to share.

Fran takes the label off the colander. Walt has left the thread on it, in case of demand for a sequel. She writes The Other One, final on a fresh label and puts it on the little drive where they’ve saved the film. Her job for the film-night committee this week is to collect the entries. On Monday morning she walks his film over to the center. Five other films are in the envelope already. She likes that. For two minutes and eleven seconds on Sunday evening, some people who have never had the use of her father’s imagination will get to sit inside it.

Back home he asks if she has time to look at Fran falls in pond. She does. He threads the old projector and she pulls the curtains. It turns out the film has no pictures of the incident itself. The camera swings down after a glimpse of water. Somebody’s feet skid on wet grass. Whoever was holding the camera made a decision and the film goes black.

“Was that you?” she asks.

“Who else was going to get you out?”

She runs it back a little. Once more the picture tips over and the sky disappears.
