Chapter 14You Have Less Time Than You Think

On the lid of the tin, in her mother’s handwriting, it says Fran falls in pond. No date, no location. The family archivist knew what would bring in the crowds.

Fran tips the reel out onto the table. Her father remembers the pond. It belonged to a man who kept ornamental fish, although by the time they got Fran out there was very little ornamental about any of them. He can’t remember whether the film shows her going in. Somebody may have done the decent thing and put down the camera.

“Would you mind telling that story again while I record it?” she asks.

“Why?”

“So we’ve got it.”

“We’ve got you. You were right in the fucking pond.”

She’s been clearing the last boxes from his old place. Since he came to live with her she’s got him a bench for the hall, worked out which mornings he wants a hand and which mornings he will quite reasonably swat the hand away, and acquired the unsettling conviction that her father constitutes a rapidly closing window for historical research. There are people in these films whose names only he knows. She’s bought small sticky labels. She’s got a list of questions. This is what happens when a woman who works in a pharmacy is allowed to bring her professional strengths to bear on the extinction of everything she loves.

Halfway through Pond, something made of stainless steel descends towards the box. It has two handles and a number of small holes through which the inhabitants of a distant planet have traditionally drained spaghetti. Her father has hung the colander upside down on a piece of thread.

“Does that look like a spaceship to you?”

“Depends what their defence budget is.”

While she’s been sorting through the films he made fifty years ago, he’s been wondering whether he might make another one.

There will be a neighbourhood film night in six weeks, in the little courtyard behind the community hall. People can submit something up to three minutes long. Fran is one of the three mugs with a key who have volunteered to organise it. He wants to enter a picture about a visitor from space who has crossed the appalling black distances between stars in search of one striped sock. He has worked out how to suspend the spaceship above the washing line. What he needs from his daughter, apart from the sock, is a performance of sufficient gravity to make the audience believe a second sock is at imminent risk.

She’s touched. She’s also got twenty-seven more tins in the box. She puts a label on the colander to remind herself it belongs downstairs.

“There’s no hurry on our thing, is there?” she asks.

He gives the spaceship a little more slack.

How long have you got?

Suppose I ask you how long you have left with somebody you love. If it’s an older parent you might do a quick, furtive piece of actuarial work. Take their present age away from the age at which their robust aunt finally went into the ground. Add a couple of years for giving up cigarettes. Knock a few off for everything they put on toast. Even as you’re doing it, you know what a daft way it is to come up with a number. You may also find yourself prepared to defend the answer against a lower bid.

Now suppose I ask how many afternoons you and that person are going to spend making a movie involving one pair of socks. That sounds absurdly specific. Good. You can actually begin to answer it.

Fran puts the six Sundays before the submission deadline on a sheet of paper. She and her dad could do an hour and a half on a Sunday afternoon. Six times ninety gives them 540 minutes. Nine hours to produce a three-minute masterpiece. By their own estimation this puts them some distance ahead of the average television production, most of which appears to have been written in the lift.

She has an extra shift for stocktaking on the second Sunday. On the fourth she’s meeting a friend who’s travelling two hours to see her. Her dad looks over her shoulder and points to the fifth. There’s a football match on. He’s going next door to watch it with a neighbour who has recently acquired a television large enough to see a striker make a poor life choice behind his own eyes.

“You could record it.”

“I could record you and watch the football in person.”

That leaves three afternoons they’re both willing and able to give the film. Four and a half hours. Half of the original nine went away without a single unforeseen accident, medical emergency or act of God. The fifth Sunday disappeared because a man would like to have a beer next door and express himself freely about the left back. That counts. His remaining time belongs to him, including the bit his daughter thinks he could spend more memorably.

Today is the first of the three. They have spent forty minutes with the tins. They could go on for another fifty, and that would be a perfectly defensible use of an afternoon. If they do, the film gets two sessions, three hours altogether. Fran does the calculation twice. There’s no bad news in it. Nobody has received a diagnosis. A quantity which felt enormous in the unit six weeks has got down to the size of something that could be lost because nobody thought to charge a phone.

A lot of what passes for the wisdom of mortality consists of changing the unit in the other direction. How can you worry about a petty slight when your life is an eyeblink in the history of the cosmos? Quite easily, it turns out. You can explain thirteen-odd billion years to a man at breakfast and he will still have physiological room in him to resent the person who put an empty jar back in the fridge. A vast number can make him feel magnificently small. It can’t settle who’s going to buy mustard.

And a tiny number can do strange things too. Tell Fran she might not have many Sundays left with her father and see what sort of daughter she tries to become. Every visit is now potentially the last decent conversation. He comes into the kitchen hoping to find where she keeps the little batteries and is greeted by the face of a woman determined to make this one count. Imagine needing two AAAs under that sort of pressure.

She realises she’s already been doing something like it. She’s asked about the day he met her mother three times this month. He’s told her the story three times, with reasonable patience and small changes in the weather. On the last occasion, halfway through, he asked if she’d ever wondered why film aliens land in the United States quite so often, given the availability of easier parking elsewhere. She’d brought him gently back to the bus stop at which he first saw her mother. Now she wonders which part of that conversation he’d have chosen to repeat.

If you knew this was your last afternoon with somebody, wouldn’t you want them to tell you something important? Possibly. If it was your last afternoon and you were the other person, wouldn’t you like some say in what counted?

Two hundred seconds

Her father holds one of the old reels up to the light. They were shooting on Super 8. A standard fifty-foot cartridge at eighteen frames a second gives you roughly three minutes and twenty seconds of running time. Run it through at twenty-four frames a second and you get about two and a half minutes.[1] This is one of the few household systems in which declaring that things need to move faster has an immediate and wonderfully honest effect on the time available.

Think of a rough allowance of 3,600 frames. Eighteen pictures each second uses them up in 200 seconds. At twenty-four you’ll be through the same 3,600 in 150. When a three-year-old is considering whether to fall into a pond, that difference begins to have artistic consequences. Hold the trigger down while she’s considering it and you may run out of film before she’s made up her mind. Release the trigger and you can be certain she’ll take the plunge in the gap. Working with children introduces a malevolent intelligence into an otherwise tractable engineering problem.

The cartridge had a physical end. When her dad paid for one he was buying the chance to choose which small stretches of an afternoon would go through the gate. On a phone, he could point the camera at the pond for half an hour. The limit would move down the line. Somebody would have to sit with half an hour of water later, trying to locate the single wet child. Saving everything for your future self to sort through is very like giving that person a loft full of boxes and then dying before you can be asked to help.

Fran has done this with pictures of her own. If you’re in them and would like copies, do please specify whether you want the first eight photographs of the birthday cake or the fourteen in which a fork has appeared. Each of those pictures cost almost nothing to take. They’re all in vigorous health on a server somewhere. On a perfectly serviceable afternoon several years from now, somebody may dearly wish to see the face of the woman who baked that cake. As far as the photographs are concerned it was produced by a fork.

There’s a good reason to keep even the boring bits. One of the reels in the box is labelled Garden, nothing much. Her father had it transferred years ago. Fran puts the file on the television. A woman in a cardigan shakes dirt from a bunch of radishes, one quick movement away from her shoes. Dad has begun to look past the radishes, towards something near the edge of the picture. Fran watches his eyes, then looks where he’s looking. On a step at the back, her mother is trying to fasten a sandal with one hand while she eats something with the other. It’s a private, ungainly little operation. Nobody considered it worth filming. It’s the part her dad plays twice.

How much more of that might she find in the box? Loads, perhaps. The woman is gone. There will be no going out to the garden next summer and politely requesting a retake of the sandal business. Fran is quite right to want to keep the tins. She has one hand still on the box when her father asks if the kitchen gloves are supposed to smell like that.

And here she has a genuine choice. Her dad can tell her which garden this was while he still remembers. He can also be a chap on a bench with an improbable idea involving kitchen gloves. You could spend so much time collecting evidence that a person once existed that for their last ten years you only ever meet them in the past tense.

“Hang on,” she says. “What’s the glove for?”

“The alien. I thought people would accept the space travel more readily if they didn’t have to accept my hand as well.”

She tries one on. When you move three fingers and keep the other two still, it has a surprisingly authoritative walk. They give the creature a practice run along the draining board. It stops under the tap to reconsider its mission. By the time it’s investigated the tea towel they’ve been at it for twelve minutes. Nothing has been recorded. Fran notices that she’s having a good time and immediately feels the familiar urge to get her phone, a development which the creature observes with contempt from the far side of the toaster.

The gentleman who would miss the show

In 1716 Edmond Halley published instructions for doing something spectacular with a few minutes that belonged to people in the future. From Earth, on the rare occasions when everything lines up, Venus crosses the face of the Sun as a small dark spot. Observers at widely separated places see the planet trace slightly different paths against the solar disc. Halley proposed timing how long the crossing took from different sites. From the differences, the geometry of the observations and the known relative sizes of the planetary orbits, astronomers could work towards a distance to the Sun in terrestrial units.[2]

You can get the beginnings of the idea without a planet. Hold up your thumb at arm’s length and look at it first through one eye and then the other. It appears to jump against the far wall. Your eyes have supplied the two viewpoints. Give the astronomers a baseline the size of a good chunk of the Earth, exchange the thumb for Venus and make sure somebody has brought a clock. There is more mathematics between there and a distance to the Sun than it would be polite to conceal in the word just.

The transits he discussed were due in 1761 and 1769. Subtract 1716 from 1761. You can see why, even if the Royal Society had been unusually generous about conference travel, Halley was unlikely to be at the eyepiece. The man writing the instructions was already approaching sixty. He lived to see neither event. In the paper he took particular care over where other people might go. He wanted observations from several places, partly because he had considered a set of astronomical bodies of quite modest size which could nevertheless wreck the project. Clouds.

What interests me here is the kind of pleasure available to Halley at his desk. We don’t know whether he had a delightful morning writing the thing. Perhaps he had toothache. But there’s an activity on offer more interesting than adding up how many exquisite dawns he might personally have left. He could make it possible for somebody else, forty-five years later, to do a piece of work he wanted done. He could be a participant in an enterprise whose result he would never receive. You do this on a humbler scale when you leave useful notes in a drawer. Some of us do the opposite by leaving a small unmarked key and enjoying the posthumous destruction of an entire weekend.

A reminder that your time is short can make the borders of your life feel terribly tight. Better get my experiences in. Better see my places, finish my work, reach the person I was always going to be, ideally before my knees disqualify me from parts of Italy. Yet one of the things a finite life can do is make a good start on a thing someone else will carry on. That uses an afternoon too. It may use the afternoon you had available for Italy. It offers a rather different answer to the question of what’s worth putting in it.

Halley’s proposal would still have been worth making if every astronomer who went out with his instructions had seen a majestic display of cloud. Weather would have settled whether they got an observation. They had to settle in advance whether they would be there to try. Fran, who has volunteered to screen films outdoors in a country where rain sometimes comes sideways, will shortly have occasion to appreciate the distinction.

The afternoon nobody is allowed to save

On the first of their remaining Sundays the phone is fully charged. The weather is fine. Her father has prepared a list of shots on the back of a pharmacy bag. Fran gets as far as putting two kitchen gloves in her coat pocket and then finds herself crying in the cupboard under the stairs. It’s quite cramped in there because it is also the only place they have found to put the hoover.

Nothing dreadful has happened. She spent the morning changing a delivery after a supplier sent the wrong size of packet. Yesterday she went into three shops to find the sort of socks her dad can put on himself. She promised she’d go out with the gloves. The inconvenience of the tears is almost worse than the feeling behind them. He has done nothing wrong. There’s a perfectly good man sitting in the hall with a sheet of paper, and she would pay actual money to spend an hour in a place where nobody is waiting pleasantly for her to come out.

When you are looking after somebody, being told to cherish every minute can put you in an extraordinary bind. You may be getting up twice in the night. You may feel the approach of the key in the lock as a distinct event in the muscles of your back. In the morning a kindly acquaintance can lean across the pharmacy counter and tell you that one day you’ll give anything to have these days again. Perfect. You can now fail at the difficult job of looking after your dad and the additional job of enjoying yourself enough to satisfy his future corpse.

He knocks on the cupboard door. “Is the hoover in a meeting?”

“I want an hour to myself.”

“All right.”

She’s been in there long enough to imagine at least seven less satisfactory reactions. It hadn’t occurred to her that he might go and put the radio on. A person can be inconsiderate in this fashion, quite carelessly invalidating several minutes of your finest courtroom work.

She makes herself a sandwich and takes it up to the little park. For a while she sits watching a pigeon fail to get purchase on a slippery metal sign. She doesn’t take a picture of it, learn from it or come home with renewed clarity about her priorities. The pigeon has a bad afternoon. She has a sandwich. If you find it hard to put that in the credit column of a human life, consider what sort of life you’re asking a person to live.

At home her dad’s drawn a series of small boxes across the pharmacy bag. He’s done something clever with the last one. If the striped sock disappears up past the top of the phone screen, nobody needs to see what happens inside the spaceship. The audience will supply millions of pounds of special effects at its own expense. Underneath he’s written alien sighs. Fran would like to know how an alien sighs.

“Well, it’s come all this way and they don’t match.”

She sits down beside him. “I’ve got half an hour if you want to try the washing line.”

They get the first shot. That’s it. The clock is quite firm on the matter. The neighbour next door thumps the wall to convey that something has happened in the football. Somebody takes in a parcel. The end of the afternoon feels nothing like a montage of irreplaceable moments, a fact for which they’re both grateful.

We can say something more precise than people value time differently as they get older. Psychologists Helene Fung, Laura Carstensen and Amy Lutz asked people about preferred social partners under different imagined time horizons. When the future was presented as limited, both younger and older participants preferred familiar company. Asking older participants to imagine an expanded future removed the preference for familiar partners they’d shown in the ordinary condition.[3] These were preferences expressed under study conditions. Nobody was followed into a pub to have their choice of drinking companion marked for emotional authenticity.

It’s an interesting result even if you never intend to spend a minute of your old age with a person you already know. Your date of birth stayed the same while you read those last two sentences. Yet imagine you’re moving to another city on Monday and see who jumps to mind for Saturday lunch. Now imagine you’ve just arrived and will be living there for twenty years. A stranger with something absorbing to tell you about the place might be the better sort of company on the second Saturday. The clock has hardly moved. What you imagine lies beyond it changes who gets a chair.

It follows that reminding yourself of an ending is doing a particular piece of work on your preferences. It can help you see which familiar person you’ve been missing. It can also make a long apprenticeship feel like a poor investment when you’d have enjoyed the first lesson for its own sake. You might ring an old friend. You might go into the cupboard and add another obligation to the heap. It matters what you ask a reminder to do.

There’s a question that works better for Fran than What if Dad died tomorrow? She has quite enough practice imagining that, thank you. At three o’clock, on their last clear Sunday, she takes her father his phone and asks, “Which shot do you actually want me in?”

He wants her face. Up till now she’s been working on the reasonable assumption that he merely requires the use of one of her hands. He wants the bit where she notices the sock has gone. He’s been trying, with no great success, to get onto his own face the particular expression she makes when something ordinary has gone comprehensively to shit. It’s fondness and exasperation and about thirty years of dealing with the public. Apparently the Earth’s last line of defence against an alien incursion looks remarkably like a woman discovering the supplier has sent suppositories in a box labelled For external use.

“Who’s going to work the alien?”

“Have you met our neighbour? Man’s had his hand up all week.”

A second and a half of Earth

The courtyard is large enough for a washing line, a bench and an aggrieved pigeon who appears to have taken the unusual step of changing parks to follow her. Her father gets a comfortable position on the bench and braces the phone in both hands. Fran crouches behind the laundry basket to send the gloved invader exploring along the line. The neighbour manages the colander. They’ll change jobs for her close-up. The sun catches the metal just right and for a brief moment the entire interstellar mission looks convincing. Then the lid from a compost bin blows through the shot at a velocity well beyond the capabilities of their spacecraft.

They get it on the fourth take. The fifth is technically better but her dad likes the fourth because for the tiniest instant before the alien lifts the sock, the fingers of the glove hesitate. Watching it, you begin to care whether this preposterous animal gets away. It appears to know that stealing is wrong. It also has several small cold feet at home.

Now she stands up. He turns the camera on her. She tells him she’s going to try a few different reactions to the theft, and on the third one she hears a noise coming from behind the phone. It’s her father trying very hard not to laugh. There goes the face. He lets the camera run for a second and a half while she looks straight at him.

Much later they sit down to edit. The final film comes to two minutes and eleven seconds, which means a film about an extraterrestrial sock thief has exercised greater editorial restraint than practically any director trusted with a major superhero franchise. They call it The Other One. Dad wants to cut the bit where she looks at him laughing. It breaks the fiction. Fran finds to her surprise that she agrees. She saves the outtake in a separate file, though. There are things she would like people in folding chairs to see, and there is a second and a half of Earth she would quite like for herself.

The deadline has cost them something. They have labelled just four of the twenty-eight tins. One shows a side street her dad recognises but can’t name. He has a go after tea, and another go the next morning. The name simply doesn’t come. There’s a mean little story available in which this proves she should have spent those Sundays on the archive after all. Let’s see how comfortably that argument runs in reverse. If they’d labelled everything and never made the film, there’d be no file on the phone. No alien caught in the act of having a conscience. We’d be unaware of what had gone missing and therefore serenely confident they’d made the responsible choice. That’s an advantage lost things enjoy when they’ve never existed.

Of course you can regret something you freely chose. You can wish you’d asked a question when there was time. There are important things people genuinely put off until they can no longer do them. If there’s a conversation being kept indefinitely for a day when everybody is brave and the groceries have put themselves away, it may be worth looking at next Tuesday. What the fact of death won’t tell you is whether next Tuesday belongs to the old footage, the new film, your friend who is coming two hours by train, or a sandwich nobody asks you to share.

Fran takes the label off the colander. Her dad has left the thread on it, just in case there’s demand for a sequel. She writes The Other One, final on a fresh label and puts it on the little drive where they’ve saved the film. Her job for the neighbourhood committee this week is to collect the entries. On Sunday morning she walks his round to the hall. There are five other films in the envelope already. She likes that. For two minutes and eleven seconds one evening soon, some people who have never had the use of her father’s imagination will get to sit inside it.

Back home he asks if she’s got time to look at Fran falls in pond. She has. He threads the old projector and she pulls the curtains. It turns out there are no pictures of the incident itself. The camera swings down after a glimpse of water. Somebody’s feet skid on wet grass. Whoever was holding the camera made a decision and the film goes black.

“Was that you?” she asks.

“Who else was going to get you out?”

She runs it back a little. Once more the picture tips over and the sky disappears. The best thing anybody did for her that afternoon took place in the part that nobody can watch.
