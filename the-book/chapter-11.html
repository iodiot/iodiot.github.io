Chapter 11The Rent Is Too Damn High, and So Are Your Expectations

The table is set for four in the apartment Sam and Jo might move into. Four wineglasses, four cloth napkins folded into fans, four little plates for the bread. Jo is still in the hall. The table is up against the door.

“How do the guests get in?” she asks. She can get one arm through the gap, far enough to lift a bread plate and hold it out to the leasing agent.

He looks around as if the previous tenants may have left instructions. He folds down one end of the table, flattening two fans. Jo comes in. He puts the end back up. Three people are in the room now, and escape depends on the cooperation of a man who works on commission.

Sam takes a picture. Later, when he looks at it, what’s going to bother him is how much he still wants this room.

The apartment costs $120 a month less than the new rent their landlord wants. It has a patch of afternoon sun, a spare hook by the tub and a closet deep enough for a vacuum to retire to with its dignity intact. The landing smells of old cooking grease. The agent opens a window. Somewhere immediately below them a man begins explaining, with great patience and precision, exactly where the driver can stick his van.

Jo tries the bedroom window herself. It closes properly. She lies on the bed for a moment, coat and all. The agent doesn’t know what to do with his hands. At the animal hospital she sleeps when the schedule says so. If you’ve never spent a year sleeping above a store’s roll-down shutter, you may think this is eccentric behavior. If you have, you’d want another five minutes and the chance to hear somebody make a delivery.

Sam understands about the window. His thing is harder to explain. From the kitchen he can see both ends of the table at once. He pictures himself coming through with a hot dish held in a towel. He even knows which towel. He has yet to identify the food, or the guests, or how any of them got in. But he can see himself as clearly as if he were standing at the far end of the room looking back.

They’ve left the car outside the building. Jo wants to walk down to the bus stop before they go, because three days a week that’s how she’d be getting here. They own one car, and it goes with whoever leaves first in the morning. On her late shifts that’s Sam, who has never once offered to trade.

On the way down the hill Jo asks who he’d invite.

“Dan, for a start.”

“When did you last ask him over to our place?”

He has to go back a bit. A birthday? That was at Dan’s. One afternoon his brother came by for a drill. Sam didn’t offer him a cup of coffee because clothes were drying in the kitchen. Dan has two daughters. It is unlikely the sight of damp underpants would have ended the visit on medical grounds.

“I’d like to be the one who has people over,” Sam says.

“Oh,” says Jo. She’d thought he wanted a table.

Six minutes late

They get to the stop at 7:16, which is when her bus from the hospital would drop her here after a late shift. The bus she’d change onto leaves at 7:10. Next one at 8:10. The stop has a bench, a fluorescent light and a poster inviting local residents to have their say. Somebody has had theirs in marker. Jo puts a hand on the seat to check for rain. It comes away wet.

The arithmetic is easy, which is one of the more dangerous kinds. Sam does it on the back of the landlord’s letter. Moving saves $120 a month, which is $1,440 a year. Jo works three late shifts a week. An hour lost on each is about twelve hours a month. He divides and writes down $10 an hour. Jo borrows the pen and draws a small head next to the $10.

“Who’s that?”

“Me. Thought it might help.”

He’d treated the money as theirs and the hour as a naturally occurring gap in the landscape. At the stop it’s easier to see who would spend it. If the first bus runs early she might make the 7:10 some nights. A shift that runs over would put it out of reach, and a dog that has eaten a sock at 6:25 doesn’t consult the schedule. She could walk the rest. They try the walk while they’re there. Halfway up the hill Sam is obliged to revise several opinions he formed at sea level.

Jo doesn’t mind time alone. On a moving bus she can read forty pages of a mystery in which a small-town church organist turns out to have known an astonishing number of people worth murdering. She can’t do that at a wet stop. Nor can she pick up a prescription on the way, call her sister in private or take the dish out when the timer goes. An hour has a location. It comes with a pair of shoes and sometimes a very full bladder. Twelve hours in a lump on a Saturday would be a different gift.

You can put a price on nearly anything if the other person will sit still long enough. Would Jo take ten dollars to wait here tonight? Possibly. They could use ten dollars. Would she sign over every late-shift evening in February for a stack of tens? She wants to see February first. One of those evenings might be Sam’s birthday. One might follow a fight, when she’d like him to spend an hour imagining what she’s going to say.

Even the ten bucks is provisional. She’ll have vacation days, swapped shifts, kind drivers and one glorious evening when the bus breaks down in exactly the right direction. Still, the couple at the wet stop know a great deal more than the two people who liked a sunlit room an hour ago.

Sam wants one more number before they go. “What does staying leave us?”

“Depends what breaks.”

It takes him a second to realize that’s her answer.

The money left over

At home Jo gets out three months of bank statements. Sam opens two beers. At the old rent, in an ordinary month, they had about $165 left after the bills, food, gas and the things they knew were coming. The proposed increase on their current place is $120. Staying would bring the ordinary $165 down to $45. They do have some savings. Enough for a broken appliance. Enough for a weekend away. It is unfortunately the same money. Sam draws a line through the name of a little town on the coast where Jo had found a room over a bakery. He liked the idea of waking up to bread. He liked it much better when he thought they were only talking about buses.

Their $45 has company, and Jo looks it up. For 2024, the Census Bureau’s American Community Survey put the national median gross rent, rent plus utilities, at $1,487 a month. Renter households had a median income of about $4,537 a month. The rent comes to 32.8 percent of it.[1] Two medians set side by side describe a market. No single household has to match both.

Sam distrusts the percentage. He invents two households on $2,000 and $5,000 a month and has both pay 35 percent in rent. One is left with $1,300 and the other with $3,250. Try being the one with the bad tooth in each kitchen. A percentage is a good way to put two things on a common scale. It is also a superb place to hide $1,950.

With $45 to spare, the question of what Sam wants from a dining table ought to be closed. A renter choosing between a place that smells of mildew and one that eats the grocery budget doesn’t need a lecture on the spiritual dangers of granite countertops. Adjust the tenant’s sense of gratitude all you like. The mold stays in excellent spirits.

And yet one of the things Sam wants really is the look on his brother’s face. It would be convenient if being squeezed by a housing market exempted a person from also being a vain little shit. I’d welcome that exemption myself. Imagine the paperwork it would save.

He tries something at the kitchen counter. He pulls up the photograph he took and puts his thumb over the place settings. Jo wants to know why he’s pawing at his phone this time.

“Trying to see the room empty.”

She finds the floor plan instead. Empty, the room is still good. He could work there while she slept off a shift. Nora and June could get out the paints without somebody having to build a shelf out of the bread box. Jo could sit down with her sister and close a door. They spend ten minutes finding uses for the room. Then he goes back to the picture and has to admit what the glasses are for. He wants his older brother to stand in that doorway, count four matching stems and quietly revise his opinion of how Sam is doing. He’d want it even if everyone were drinking tap water. Here we have, regrettably, a man. Several motives can operate him at once.

An eighteenth-century shirt

In 1776 Adam Smith devoted some space in The Wealth of Nations to a laborer’s shirt. He was writing about taxes. To decide which goods could be taxed as luxuries, he needed an account of what people could reasonably be expected to go without. For much of Europe in his day, he thought, a laborer who appeared in public without a linen shirt risked being taken for someone whose poverty discredited him. Smith put the shirt among the man’s necessities.[2]

The telling detail is where the shirt does its work. Less between the man and the weather than between the man and his neighbors. His skin could perform its assigned tasks without linen. The neighbors, according to Smith, were prepared to perform theirs by drawing conclusions about his character. The garment got him through a public encounter without having to answer a question nobody would have the decency to ask out loud. If he spent money to secure that freedom, who exactly was going to tell him he’d been silly to care what people thought?

Sam’s glasses look like the same purchase. He’d like to show his family he’s all right, and that has a history. When he and Jo first moved in together, Dan brought something every time he visited. A bag of groceries. A frying pan. Once, an enormous pack of toilet paper, carried up their street past a full bar patio with the composure of a man bearing his country’s flag into a stadium. They needed the stuff. Sam loved him for it. He would also like, once, to feed his brother without his brother first buying the food.

Smith’s laborer, though, was dressing for strangers who really would draw the conclusion. Sam is setting a table for a man who once strained pasta through Sam’s pillowcase and neglected to mention it for eleven years. Dan has never charged admission to his good opinion. The neighbors Sam is dressing for live mostly in Sam.

“Does he know you want to feed him?” Jo asks.

“Do I call him up and announce I’m ready to purchase my own toilet paper?”

“You could ask him for dinner.”

She says it without much emphasis. She’s getting up to go to bed. The sentence stays at the table with him.

The empty part at the top

The next morning an agent sends Sam pictures of another apartment he hasn’t asked about. It costs $90 more a month than the proposed rent on the one they already have. Three of the photographs are of the kitchen faucet. It has a little hose you can pull out to send water all over an area previously safe from water. Sam looks at that kitchen, then at the cheap apartment, then at theirs. He feels a distinct pang about their faucet. Yesterday he could not have described it to a police sketch artist.

Christopher Hsee once gave people an enjoyable imaginary problem. They were to picture buying ice cream on a summer day beside Lake Michigan. One serving had seven ounces of ice cream heaped above the rim of a five-ounce cup. The other had eight ounces sitting in a ten-ounce cup. When separate groups considered one serving apiece, people put a higher price, on average, on the smaller helping. In the same paper the preference reversed when people saw the two offers together.[3] A close replication in 2023 also found that the overflowing portion had the advantage when each person judged one offer on its own, though the effect was smaller than in the original.[4]

Nobody had to eat the cardboard to get this result. The rim did the work. Show a person only the seven-ounce helping and the vendor has done a generous thing right in front of them. Show them only the eight-ounce helping and they can describe with some precision the location of their missing ice cream. You could serve them the extra ounce and send them home aggrieved.

Sam’s faucet was a full cup until one with a hose sat down next to it. What an animal to entrust with a lease.

An apartment doesn’t come in ounces. Some features Sam and Jo can compare. Can the two of them do different things behind closed doors? Can Jo get home after a late shift? Does the window keep the shutter noise out? Then come the things photographs make easy to size up. A three-page listing full of beautifully framed appliances can answer a question they never thought to ask and leave them none the wiser about what a Tuesday feels like. Jo opens the floor plans of all three places and makes a pot of coffee. After ten minutes she has crossed off the most expensive one. Its front door opens onto a flight of stairs far steeper than theirs, the kind her father couldn’t manage. Sam has forgotten the pull-out faucet. Nobody has even had to kill him.

The three plans look agreeable side by side until Jo checks the listing for the cheaper apartment at lunchtime. It now says application received. Somebody else has apparently managed to get through the door. The agent offers to send pictures of similar properties when any come up. Sam recognizes the particular rage of being told by a person who no longer has anything you want that he’ll keep you on file forever.

So much for comparing your options and lifting the right life out of the display case. A stranger turned up at nine with references and a deposit. They may be very nice. They are welcome to lose a napkin behind the radiator. Sam closes the listing. The landlord still wants an answer by Friday.

A table does have its uses

“Can we try Sunday here?” he asks Jo. “Have Dan over. See what it is I think we’re missing.”

“Which part am I trying? You being charming while I pull somebody’s socks off the drying rack?”

This is a fair question. A large share of his plans for them to become different people have involved her doing most of the laundry. He calls his brother himself. He says they’ll have to eat in the kitchen. He’s trying to sound casual and achieves roughly the tone you’d use to disclose a rash. Dan says Sunday would be great. Could he bring June? Nora has a birthday party and Carla has a bird walk she’s been promised since March. Did they want him to pick anything up on the way?

“Just yourselves,” says Sam. After a moment he adds, “And that folding chair, if you’ve still got it.” This feels surprisingly good. He needs the chair. He can supply the potatoes.

We’ll have people over had spent years wandering contentedly among the kitchen displays. Dan and June are coming at one on Sunday has already located a shortage of chairs and a bag of potatoes. A date does that to a wish.

Some wants come through this kind of attention looking better than before. In the expensive apartment a square of cobalt tile sat above the sink, and Jo looked at that photograph longer than she looked at the stairs. Wanting a thing because it’s beautiful needs no further excuse. If one blue tile in a thrift store made her happy enough to carry it home in her coat, good for her. At $90 a month on top of a rent that already frightens them, the same blue is a different promise.

Jo also wants a little room to herself some evenings. Sam has been thinking of that almost entirely in terms of how many rooms their money can buy. On Wednesday he gets home and finds her on the bedroom floor talking to her sister. She’s leaning against the side of the bed because the room has nowhere else to sit. He can hear his own name through the door. Along with a flare of professional interest in the subject under discussion, he feels a sudden embarrassment about all the times she’s taken her calls outside to give him a quiet table to work at. She’d do it even in a palace if he let her. Square feet would help. So would getting his coat and going out for milk.

On Thursday they ask the landlord if he’ll take a smaller increase. They offer an extra $70 a month. He sends back two sentences thanking them for looking after the property and confirming the full amount. Jo sets the letter down and cries. Only for a minute. She’d been looking forward to that room over the bakery.

Sam doesn’t tell her they can have a nice time at home. He’d been looking forward to the trip too. They decide to pay the higher rent and stay. They can cover the increase from what they were saving, keep their present routes to work and give themselves a few months to look for somewhere else. They put a date in the calendar to run the figures again after the winter bills. Another household on the same wages with a smaller cushion could take one look at that $45 and choose the bus stop. Jo might choose it herself if one of their paychecks stops, which this month has quit sounding like a figure of speech. They make the decision they can afford to make on Thursday. It still spoils Thursday.

On Saturday Sam notices the name of the town on the coast under his line on the landlord’s letter. He starts to erase it. Then he remembers that he did it in pen, and that rubbing at a thing until it has a hole in it is an approach to home repair Dan has specifically advised against. He puts the letter away. He has a chicken to buy.

Sunday at one

June has brought her own knife. It’s purple plastic with a serrated edge, and according to her father it was the settlement in a negotiation that opened with whether she could bring the cat. June is cleared for bananas, cucumbers and anything boiled. Sam hands over a pot of boiled red potatoes. She halves every one, lines them up on the sheet pan cut side down and informs him that the knife is not a toy and he may not borrow it. Jo has put the drying rack in the bedroom, where with luck it will refrain from drying clothes in a socially embarrassing manner until four.

They learn some things about the kitchen. It seats four if everybody who wants a drink says so before sitting down. The oven opens if Dan tucks his left leg under his right. A child’s drawing will go on the stained part of the wall, although the artist may reasonably object when you try to hide a much larger stain behind her very small horse. For a moment four people are chewing and the room sounds exactly as Sam imagined the other room would sound. It’s brief. He’d forgotten that other people chew.

He watches his brother’s face for the look. It doesn’t come. Dan hasn’t examined the room at all. He’s busy with the chicken.

Sam takes his phone out for a photo of the table. Just as he presses the button Dan passes the potatoes to Jo. All you can see in the picture is the cuff of his brother’s sweater and a bowl of potatoes traveling west.

Later the two men carry the folding chair down the stairs. The vinyl seat has a split in it where somebody has tried to fix it with the wrong kind of tape. The edge catches on Sam’s sleeve. Dan frees it. He asks whether Sam remembers the year the two of them ate Thanksgiving dinner standing at opposite ends of their mother’s ironing board.

“Course I do. The gravy went in the little holes.”

“I thought that was the best one,” Dan says.

Sam had spent much of that Thanksgiving worrying about whether his friends would find out. For years one of the facts about his family was that they’d been the people who had to eat off an ironing board. He’d had no idea the boy standing at the other end had been having the best one.

Dan hoists the chair into the trunk. “You know,” he says, “you can call us when it’s just you at home. The kids don’t have to be there. You don’t have to cook.”

It’s a small opening. Sam could tell him that he misses the time when they could sit next to one another doing absolutely jack. He could ask how his brother is. Instead he tells him to watch the chair leg against the window. Dan shuts the trunk carefully. They stand there for another moment, very well informed about the condition of the glass.

Upstairs Jo and June have started on the dishes. Sam can hear June giving clear and binding instructions concerning the purple knife, which is washed by hand. His brother’s car is still at the curb while Dan looks for something on the passenger seat. The passenger door is open. Sam goes back down and gets in.

“What did you like about that Thanksgiving?” he asks.

Dan puts his keys on the dash.
