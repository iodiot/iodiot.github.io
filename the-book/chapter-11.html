Chapter 11The Rent Is Too Damn High, and So Are Your Expectations

There are four places laid for dinner in the flat Sam and Jo might move into. Four wineglasses, four napkins folded into bishops’ hats, four little plates for the bread. Jo is still in the hall. The table is up against the door.

“How do the guests get in?” she asks.

The agent looks around as if the previous tenants may have left instructions. He folds down one end of the table, sacrificing two bishops. Jo comes in. He puts the end back up. There are three of them in the room now, and escape depends on the cooperation of a man who works on commission.

Sam takes a picture. Later, when he looks at it, what’s going to bother him is how much he still wants this room.

The flat costs £120 a month less than the new rent their landlord wants. It has a patch of afternoon sun, a spare hook by the bath and a cupboard deep enough for a hoover to retire to with its dignity intact. There’s a flat smell of old cooking fat on the landing. The agent opens a window. Somewhere immediately below them a man begins explaining, with great patience and precision, exactly where the driver can stick his van.

Jo tries the bedroom window herself. It closes properly. She lies on the bed for a moment, coat and all. The agent doesn’t know what to do with his hands. If you’ve never spent a year sleeping above a shop shutter, you may think this is eccentric behaviour. If you have, you might like another five minutes and the opportunity to hear somebody make a delivery.

Sam understands about the window. His thing is harder to explain. From the kitchen he can see both ends of the table at once. He pictures himself coming through with a hot dish held in a towel. He even knows which towel. He has yet to identify the food, or the guests, or how he got through the fucking door. But he can see himself as clearly as if he were standing at the far end of the room looking back.

On the walk to the bus stop Jo asks who he’d invite.

“Dan, for a start.”

“When did you last ask him round to ours?”

He has to go back a bit. A birthday? That was at Dan’s. There was the afternoon his brother came by for a drill. Sam didn’t offer him a cup of tea because there were clothes drying in the kitchen. Dan has two children. It is unlikely the sight of a pair of damp underpants would have ended the visit on medical grounds.

“I’d like to be the one who has people over,” Sam says.

“Oh,” says Jo. She’d thought he wanted a table.

Six minutes late

They get to the stop at 7:16. The bus Jo would change onto after a late shift leaves at 7:10. Next one at 8:10. The 7:10 would get her to the new flat about the same time her usual bus gets her home now. There is a bench, a strip light and a poster inviting local residents to have their say. Somebody has had theirs in felt tip. Jo puts a hand on the seat to check for rain. It comes away wet.

An easy bit of arithmetic, which is one of the more dangerous kinds. Compared with staying put at the proposed rent, moving saves £1,440 over a year before moving costs or changes in bills. Three late shifts a week. Miss all three connections and she gets home an hour later three times. In four such weeks, twelve hours. Divide the £120 by twelve and you get a tenner an hour. Sam writes that down. Jo borrows the pen and draws a small head next to the tenner.

“Who’s that?”

“Me. Thought it might help.”

He’d treated the money as theirs and the hour as a naturally occurring gap in the landscape. At the stop it’s easier to see who would spend it. If the bus before the connection runs early enough, she might catch it some nights. A shift that overruns a little would put it beyond reach. They could walk part of the way. They decide to try the walk while they’re there. Halfway up a long hill Sam is obliged to revise several opinions he had formed at sea level.

There are things Jo quite likes doing on her own. On a good bus she can read forty pages of a murder mystery in which the village organist turns out to have known a frankly astonishing number of people worth murdering. She can’t do that at the stop in the rain. Nor can she collect the prescription on the way, phone her sister in private or take the hot dish out when the timer goes. An hour has a location. It comes with a pair of shoes and sometimes a very full bladder. A lump of twelve hours on a Saturday would be an altogether different gift.

You can put a price on pretty much anything if the person you’re talking to will sit still long enough. Would Jo take ten pounds to wait here tonight? Possibly. They could use ten pounds. Would she sign over every Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday evening in February for a stack of ten-pound notes? She wants to see February first. What if it’s Sam’s birthday on one of the Thursdays? What if they have a row and she’d quite like him to spend an hour imagining what she’s going to say?

Even the tenner is provisional. Rent is due in calendar months. Four weeks is a sample of her rota. There will be leave, changed shifts, unexpectedly kind drivers and one glorious evening when the bus breaks down in exactly the right direction. The calculation has earned its place on the letter. Now they know which uncertainty to investigate. The couple at the stop in the rain know a great deal more than the two people who liked the look of a sunlit room on a screen.

Sam wants one more number before they go. “What does staying leave us?”

“Depends what breaks.”

It takes him a second to realise that’s her answer.

The money left over

At home Jo gets out three months of bank statements. Sam puts the kettle on. At the old rent, in an ordinary month, they had about £165 left after the bills, food, travel and the things they knew were coming. One month’s figure looked unusually healthy until Jo remembered the electricity payment had gone out on the first of the next month. It only takes midnight to turn a financial genius into a man who owes the electricity company £84.

The proposed increase on their current place is £120. Staying would bring the ordinary £165 down to £45. They do have some savings. Enough for a broken appliance. Enough for a weekend away. It is unfortunately the same money. Sam draws a line through the name of a little town by the sea where Jo had found a room over a bakery. He liked the idea of waking up to bread. He liked it much better when he thought they were only talking about buses.

There is a temptation, in a book with a title like the one at the top of this chapter, to sit a tenant down for an honest discussion about their attitude. Before we embark on that very affordable intervention, here’s one real number. For the financial year ending in 2024, Britain’s Office for National Statistics compared the average monthly private rent in England, £1,232, with a median monthly gross household income of £3,396 among private renters. The rent came to 36.3 percent of that income.[1] Those are two statistics brought together to describe a market. The survey didn’t follow one conveniently typical family home and watch them post precisely thirty-six pence of every pound through a landlord’s letter box.

The distinction matters. Take two invented households on £2,000 and £5,000 a month. Both pay 35 percent in rent. The first has £1,300 left, the second £3,250, before whatever else has to come out. Try buying the same replacement pair of school shoes with what’s left in each kitchen. Try being the one with the bad tooth. A percentage is a good way to put two things on a common scale. It is also a superb place to hide £1,950.

For a renter choosing between a place that smells of damp and one that takes the entire grocery budget, a lecture on the spiritual dangers of wanting granite worktops has arrived at the wrong address. You could remove every advertisement from the country and that renter would still need somewhere dry to sleep. A change in pay, a repair, a shorter wait for a home or a bus that runs in the evening could alter the choice. An adjustment in the tenant’s sense of gratitude would leave the mould in excellent spirits.

And yet one of the things Sam wants really is the look on his brother’s face. It would be convenient if the existence of a housing market in considerable distress exempted everybody in it from occasionally being a vain little shit. I would personally welcome that exemption. Imagine the paperwork it would save.

He tries something at the kitchen counter. He pulls up the photograph he took. Puts his thumb over the place settings. Jo wants to know why he’s molesting his phone this time.

“Trying to see the room empty.”

She finds the floor plan instead. Empty, the room is still good. He could do some work there while she slept. His niece could get out the paints without somebody having to construct a shelf out of the bread bin. Jo could sit down with her sister and close a door. They spend ten minutes finding uses for the room. Then he goes back to the picture and has to admit that a small persistent part of him would like visitors to see the matching glasses even if everyone is drinking tap water. Here we have, regrettably, a man. Several motives can operate him at once.

An eighteenth-century shirt

In 1776 Adam Smith devoted some space in The Wealth of Nations to a labourer’s shirt. He was writing about taxes. To decide which goods could be taxed as luxuries, he needed an account of what people could reasonably be expected to go without. For much of Europe in his day, he thought, a labourer who appeared in public without a linen shirt risked being taken for someone whose poverty discredited him. Smith put the shirt among the man’s necessities.[2]

The remarkable detail is where the shirt does its work. Put it on a peg. The man is on one side and his neighbours are on the other. His skin could perform its assigned tasks without linen. The neighbours, according to Smith, were quite prepared to perform theirs by drawing conclusions about his character. The garment could get him through a public encounter without having to answer a question nobody would have the decency to ask out loud. If he spent money to secure that freedom, who exactly was going to tell him he’d been silly to care what people thought?

Try taking an invitation for dinner when the small amount you can spend is scheduled for the electricity. You might have enough food to reciprocate if the guests are two adults who like lentils. You might worry that one of them will say, very kindly, “Well, look at what you’ve managed to do with the place.” A guest can put quite a lot of daylight between managed and place. Recognising the possibility takes some knowledge of other people. Declining every invitation for six months on the strength of a remark no guest has made takes something else.

The cruel bit is that those two skills feel much the same from inside your head. You remember the pause someone once left in a sentence about your mother’s house. You have no intention of hearing that pause in a sentence about yours. Fair enough. But who’s coming for dinner on Sunday? The person who made the remark? Your brother who once used your pillowcase to strain pasta and neglected to mention it for eleven years? There are people who charge admission to their good opinion. Check which one of them you’ve been setting a place for.

Jo once worked with a woman who arranged all her video calls against the same stretch of blank wall. One afternoon the camera tipped. Half a metre to the left of the white rectangle a boy was eating cereal in his school trousers. Half a metre to the right there was a rack of clean washing. The woman put the camera back. Jo’s first thought was that the boy had got very tall. Her second was that she’d spent months talking to someone in what she had unconsciously pictured as an empty office. That little rectangle had bought the woman peace from other people’s guesses about whether she could do her job in the vicinity of a sock.

Sam’s glasses can look suspiciously like the same purchase. He’d like to show his family that he’s all right. That has a history too. When he and Jo first moved in together Dan used to bring something every time he visited. A bag of groceries. A frying pan. Once, an enormous pack of toilet rolls, carried through a crowded pub on the way to their place with the composure of a man bearing his sovereign’s colours. They needed the stuff. Sam loved him for it. He’d also like the privilege of feeding his brother without his brother first buying the food.

“Does he know that?” Jo asks.

“Do I ring him up and announce I’m ready to purchase my own bog roll?”

“You could ask him for dinner.”

She says it without much emphasis. She’s getting up to go to bed. The sentence stays at the table with him.

The empty bit at the top

The next morning an agent sends Sam pictures of another flat he hasn’t asked about. It costs £90 more a month than the proposed rent on the one they already have. There are three photographs of the kitchen tap. It has a little hose you can pull out to send water all over an area previously safe from water. Sam looks at that kitchen, then at the cheap flat, then at theirs. He feels a distinct pang about their tap. Yesterday he could not have described it to a police sketch artist.

Christopher Hsee once offered people a rather enjoyable imaginary problem. They were to picture buying ice cream on a summer day beside Lake Michigan. One serving had seven ounces of ice cream heaped above the rim of a five-ounce cup. The other had eight ounces sitting in a ten-ounce cup. When separate groups considered one serving apiece, people put a higher price, on average, on the smaller helping. In the same paper the preference reversed when people saw the two offers together.[3] A later team also found the overflowing portion had the advantage when each person judged one offer on its own.[4]

Nobody had to eat the cardboard to get this result. The rim did the work. If all I can see is the seven-ounce helping, that vendor has done a generous thing right in front of me. If all I can see is the eight-ounce helping, I can describe with some precision the location of my missing ice cream. You could serve me the extra ounce and send me home aggrieved.

I would like to report that knowing about this experiment makes me immune to it. Try putting twelve chips in a little paper cone, then spreading the same twelve chips out on one of those enormous white restaurant plates. I can produce a sophisticated argument about rising prices before I’ve counted to thirteen. What an extraordinary animal to entrust with a mortgage.

A flat doesn’t come in ounces. There are features Sam and Jo can actually compare. Can the two of them do different things behind closed doors? Can Jo get home after a late shift? Does the window keep the shutter noise out? Then there are things the photographs let them judge with terrific ease. A three-page listing full of beautifully framed appliances can answer a question they had never thought to ask and leave them none the wiser about what Tuesday feels like. A useful distinction emerges when Jo opens the floor plans of all three places and makes a pot of tea. After ten minutes she has crossed the most expensive one off. The front door opens onto a flight of stairs her father would have trouble with. Sam has forgotten the retractable tap. Nobody has even had to kill him.

There was something agreeable about seeing the three plans side by side. Until Jo checks the listing for the cheaper flat at lunchtime. It now says application received. Somebody else has apparently managed to get through the door. They can still register their interest. The agent offers to send pictures of similar properties when any become available. Sam recognises the peculiar rage of being told that a person who doesn’t want what you have can keep you on file forever.

There goes a lovely little fantasy about the sort of choice people get to make. You gather information, you identify your values, you reach into the display cabinet and lift out the appropriate life. Meanwhile a stranger turned up at nine with references and a deposit. They may be perfectly nice. They are welcome to lose a bishop down the back of the radiator. Sam closes the listing. The landlord still wants an answer by Friday.

A table does have its uses

“Can we give Sunday a trial run?” he asks Jo. “Have Dan here. See what it is I think we’re missing.”

“Which bit am I trying out? You being charming while I’m pulling somebody’s socks off the airer?”

This is an entirely fair question. On a remarkably large number of occasions his plans for them to become different people have involved her doing most of the laundry. He rings his brother himself. He says they’ll have to eat in the kitchen. He’s trying to sound casual about this and achieves roughly the tone you’d use to disclose a minor conviction. Dan says Sunday would be lovely. Could he bring the little one? The other kid is out with a friend. Did they want him to get anything on the way?

“Bring yourselves,” says Sam. After a moment he adds, “And that folding chair, if you’ve still got it.” This feels surprisingly good. He needs the chair. He can supply the potatoes.

There is a version of this argument that ought to irritate you. A man who can put Sunday lunch on the table discovers, with the help of the humanities, that he can put Sunday lunch on the table. Beautiful. Some of us have a housemate asleep in the kitchen after a night shift. Some of us would rather drop a piano on our feet than invite anybody into the place where we’re staying. The person who’d like a living room might actually be trying to have a conversation their child can’t hear. The person who wants a second bedroom might be trying to give that child somewhere to hear absolutely everything except that conversation.

Suppose someone wants an extravagant thing because it’s beautiful. That deserves a hearing too. Why should every pleasure appear before a tribunal and produce evidence of a disability or an economic benefit? In the expensive flat there was a square of cobalt tile above the sink. If one of those blue tiles in a junk shop made Jo happy enough to carry it home in her coat, good for her. If the only way to own that square of blue costs £90 every month on top of a rent they already find frightening, it might be a good day to look for a junk shop. Those are different prices for different promises.

What would help you recognise a promise worth taking seriously? Give it a date. We’ll have people round could spend twenty years wandering contentedly among the kitchen displays. Dan and his daughter are coming at one on Sunday has already located a shortage of chairs and a bag of potatoes. I want a place I can be proud of might mean that you want ten minutes with a friend in a room where you can say how bad things have got. It might mean you’d enjoy three seconds of envy from a former classmate with excellent teeth. Name the friend. Name the classmate. See which one you’d mind terribly if they had to cancel.

There are desires that survive this kind of attention and look better than they did before. Jo wants a little room to herself some evenings. Sam has been thinking of that almost exclusively in terms of how many rooms their money can buy. On Wednesday he gets home and finds her sitting on the floor in the bedroom talking to her sister. She’s leaning against the side of the bed because there’s nowhere else to sit. He can hear his own name through the door. He feels, along with a flare of professional interest in the subject under discussion, a sudden embarrassment about all the times she’s taken her conversations outside to give him a quiet table to work at. She’d do it even in a palace if he let her. Square metres would help. So would getting his coat and going out for milk.

On Thursday they ask the landlord if he’ll take a smaller increase. They offer an extra £70 a month. He sends back two sentences thanking them for looking after the property and confirming the full amount. Somewhere, one likes to think, there is a burning building with a reserved parking space for the phrase thank you for looking after the property. Jo sets the letter down and cries. Only for a minute. She’d been looking forward to that room over the bakery.

Sam doesn’t tell her they can have a nice time at home. He’d also been looking forward to the trip. They decide to pay the higher rent and stay. They can cover the increase from what they were saving, keep their present route home and give themselves a few months to look for somewhere else along it. They put a date in the calendar to run the figures again after the winter bills. Another household earning the same wages with a smaller cushion could take one look at that £45 of breathing room and choose the bus stop. Jo might take it herself if one of their shifts gets cut. They make the decision they can afford to make on Thursday. It still spoils Thursday.

On Saturday Sam notices the name of the town by the sea under his line on the landlord’s letter. He starts to rub it out. Then he remembers that this was done in pen and that rubbing at a thing until there’s a hole in it was an approach to household maintenance his brother had very specifically advised against. He puts the letter away. He has onions to buy.

Sunday at one

Dan’s daughter has brought her own knife. It’s purple plastic, has a serrated edge of considerable interest to the potato community, and according to her father represents a fairly considerable advance in the negotiations over whether she could bring the cat. She is delighted to discover that a cooked potato gives way under it. Sam sets one aside on a saucer for her to work on. Jo has put the airer in the bedroom, where with luck it will refrain from drying clothes in a socially embarrassing manner until four.

They learn some things about the kitchen. You can seat four if everybody who wants a drink tells you before you sit down. You can open the oven if Dan tucks his left leg under his right. You can put a child’s drawing on the stained bit of wall, although the young artist may reasonably object when you attempt to conceal part of a much larger stain behind her very small horse. There is a moment when four people are chewing and the room sounds exactly as Sam imagined the other room would sound. It’s brief. He’d forgotten that other people chew.

He takes his phone out for a photo of the table. Just as he presses the button Dan passes the potatoes to Jo. All you can see in the picture is the cuff of his brother’s jumper and several very large roast potatoes travelling west.

Later the two men carry the folding chair down the stairs. There is a split in the vinyl seat where somebody has tried to fix it with the wrong kind of tape. The edge catches on Sam’s sleeve. Dan frees it. He asks whether Sam remembers the year the two of them ate Christmas dinner standing at opposite ends of their mother’s ironing board.

“Course I do. The gravy went in the little holes.”

“I thought that was the best one,” Dan says.

Sam had spent much of that Christmas worrying about whether his mates would find out. For years one of the facts about his family was that they’d been the people who had to eat off an ironing board. He’d had no idea the boy standing at the other end, in the same stupid paper hat, had been having the best one.

Dan hoists the chair into the boot. “You know,” he says, “you can ring us when it’s just you in. The kids don’t have to be there. You don’t have to cook.”

There’s a faint invitation in this to say more. Sam could tell him that he misses the time when they could sit next to one another doing absolutely bugger all. He could ask how his brother is. Instead he tells him to watch the chair leg against the window. Dan shuts the boot carefully. They stand there for another moment, very well informed about the condition of the glass.

Upstairs Jo and his niece have started on the washing-up. Sam can hear the child giving clear and binding instructions concerning the purple knife. His brother’s car is still at the kerb while Dan looks for something on the passenger seat. The passenger door is open. Sam goes back down and gets in.

“What did you like about that Christmas?” he asks.

Dan puts his keys on the dash.
