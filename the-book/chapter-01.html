Chapter 01Congratulations, You're Conscious. Sorry About That.

You came in for a lemon. While you wait to pay for it, your brain would like to discuss something shitty you said three years ago.

On Tuesday afternoon you stand in the checkout line holding the fruit. The person ahead of you is paying in coins. Somewhere behind you a child is making the case for a cereal whose chief ingredient appears to be a cartoon tiger.

The peel has a nick in it. You press it with your thumbnail and get a little lemon oil on your fingers. For a second it’s summer in somebody’s kitchen. Your uncle Ben is there, pouring flour into a chipped yellow bowl for his mother’s pancakes, the thin kind that get lemon and sugar and no discussion. You used to be small enough to stand under his arm while he did it.

The smell lasts a second. The prosecution has the room for as long as it likes.

Three years ago, at a meeting, a coworker lost her place halfway through a presentation. You made a joke. People laughed. You can still see her hand resting on the page while she waited for them to stop. Now, between the gum and the batteries, you think of the thing you wish you’d said instead.

The cashier hasn’t even scanned the lemon yet. Your mind has convened a hearing, heard the evidence and scheduled a further hearing to find out why the first one took so long.

Congratulations. You are conscious. Sorry about that.

A minute in a grocery store bought you two afternoons, nearly two decades apart. In one you could almost feel flour on the fine hairs of your arm. In the other a woman had to stand in front of a conference table while it laughed. You can tell which of those trips you’d take again. The version of you doing the choosing showed up late. Both trips had already left.

The mind that watches itself

The smell of that lemon is already an experience. You can have it with your eyes closed, without knowing the name of the fruit or having a single theory about how a nose works. Then you can turn your attention to the experience itself. How odd, you think, that the smell came back more clearly than the kitchen. And how odd that a person can be moved by the memory of an uncle’s forearm. It was an ordinary forearm. You doubt Ben has written any poems about it.

Take the lemon seriously for a moment. A chemist could tell you about the oil released by the damaged peel. A scientist who studies smell could tell you how molecules in the air reach the receptors in your nose. You could learn a great deal from both and still be stuck on a peculiar question. How would you get that particular smell across to somebody who has never smelled anything?

In 1982 the philosopher Frank Jackson imagined a scientist called Mary who knows all the physical facts about human color vision while living in a black-and-white environment. Then she gets to see color. Does she learn something the instant she sees it? [1] Perhaps what she acquires is a way of recognizing an experience she could previously only describe. Perhaps the description really did leave something out. Philosophers are still arguing. Mary is a character in a thought experiment. Nobody has kept a woman in a monochrome apartment since 1982 on the off chance that philosophy might one day clear the paperwork.

Here in the store you have a distinction you can actually try out. For a moment pay attention to the smell. Then pay attention to what happened when you smelled it. The second task can take you a long way from the first. Soon you’re wondering why you haven’t called Ben, whether that makes you a bad nephew or niece, whether good relatives need a piece of fruit to remind them of each other. Meanwhile you’d have to sniff your fingers again to say much about the lemon.

That capacity to change what you’re attending to is useful. If you’re furious, noticing your fury can give you just enough time to put the phone down. Perhaps the message you composed at full boil makes a decent point. It could still stand to lose the part where you explain to another adult what their mother should have done differently in 1987. A person who can notice a thought while there’s still time to edit it has been handed something extraordinary.

Now notice what happens if the cashier catches you smiling at the memory of Ben. A good kitchen disappears and you start wondering what sort of lunatic smiles at a lemon. The same machinery that gave you the kitchen can turn an innocent bystander into a public relations problem without requiring the innocent bystander to look up.

Reflection, or the hamster with a law degree

Take the meeting. One detail keeps snagging at you, the hand on the page. On the first pass you remember something that had escaped you at the time. You got a laugh while another person waited to finish a sentence. Next time someone loses their place you could give them a moment, or ask whether they want the slide put back up. The memory has paid its way.

On the fifteenth pass you’re studying the same hand for a different purpose. You want to know how thoroughly she disliked you. There are no fresh witnesses at checkout. Your evidence consists of a three-year-old gesture and the testimony of a defendant who is also sitting as judge. By two in the morning the hamster has obtained a law degree and is billing you by the hour.

A respectable objection turns up here. If you hurt somebody, why should you get to stop thinking about it just because the thinking has become unpleasant? Perhaps the first fourteen passes were evasions. Perhaps the fifteenth is when you finally admit that, at the time, you knew exactly whose expense the laugh came at. People can spend years coming around to an admission like that. A clock would be a lousy instrument for telling honest reflection from going around in circles.

Try listening to the question instead. When the question was What did she have to do because of what I said?, you saw the hand on the page and understood something about the cost to her. When it became What sort of person would have said a thing like that?, a surprising number of hours became available for discussing your character. You could keep proceedings going until one of you dies of old age. Should it be you, see whether the executor can keep the case open. It would be a shame to let a little thing like the death of the accused bring anybody else’s needs back into the picture.

The difference matters if you’re considering an apology. Try writing her a few lines. The first version starts with what you did, passes through a detailed medical history of your conscience, and ends with the hope that she understands what a difficult three years it has been for you. Imagine opening that email five minutes before lunch. She went to work to do a presentation. Apparently the job also came with lifetime responsibility for the aftercare of the comedian who interrupted it.

The shorter version takes three sentences. You remember making a joke when she lost her place. You wish you’d given her a moment to speak. You’re sorry. Whether it would help her to hear from you now depends on things this little story hasn’t told us, including whether you still work together and what she’s already said about it. You have enough to make a start on your own behavior either way. She gets to have a response of her own, up to and including a blank look and a request to please pass the coffee.

Back at the checkout, the question worth taking home is small enough to fit on the receipt. Next time someone loses their place, can you bear three seconds in which nobody is laughing and nobody is looking at you? Three seconds would have given her a chance to find the line. In three years of thinking about that meeting, you’ve barely considered what she could have done with three seconds.

How many people saw that?

At the far end of the store somebody calls a name that sounds like yours. You turn, and for an instant a stranger in your brother’s coat becomes your brother. Then the stranger takes off his hat. It was a decent guess. You know the coat. Your brother shops here. All it took to settle the matter was a second look at the man’s face. If only the other guesses that pass through your head came with a man willing to turn around and take his hat off.

With people you love, a second look can be harder to come by. Suppose Ben leaves a message asking you to call him back. You can hear something in his voice. He’s in his late seventies, and you’ve reached the age at which a relative’s voicemail gets played twice, once for the words and once for the breathing. On the stairs up to your apartment you try to work out whether it’s the bad kind of tired. It might be. It could also be the voice of a man reading his own phone number off a scrap of paper because he still believes a telephone message requires a return address. You can turn the volume up. After a point all that gets you is a louder version of the same uncertainty. To find out whether he’s all right, somebody is going to have to get him on the phone.

Consider the speed at which you furnished the audience for that meeting. You remember hearing laughter. When you run the scene back, everybody seems to have understood the joke and filed the incident somewhere accessible. You could practically go around the room naming the people who now think slightly less of you. That’s a lot of intelligence to have gathered while staring at a woman’s hand.

Thomas Gilovich, Victoria Medvec and Kenneth Savitsky found a way to put a number on a small piece of this. In one experiment, students were sent into a room wearing a T-shirt with Barry Manilow’s face on it. It was a shirt the wearers had reason to feel conspicuous in. Afterward they estimated how many of the people in the room would be able to identify the person on the shirt. Their average estimate was about 46 percent. The observers’ average identification rate was about 23 percent. A wearer’s guess, on average, doubled the size of that particular audience. [2]

The finding has a tempting use. Divide your worst memory by two, round down for lunch breaks and declare yourself forgiven. Unfortunately for this attractive piece of mathematics, the experiment concerned remembering a face on a shirt. It established nothing about how your coworker experienced the wait for the laughter to stop. Even if every other person in the room had forgotten it by lunchtime, two people were still there who mattered to what happened.

It works the other way too. If you’re queer in a room where a remark about your partner can cost you your job, or the only woman at a table that greets every slip of yours with an indulgent smile, you have good reasons to pay attention to who noticed what. An experiment in a college classroom can show that people sometimes overestimate the attention they receive. It can’t tell you what any given room is safe for. The person who has to walk in there tomorrow already knows more about it than the study does.

Back at the checkout you can make a smaller, stranger concession. The others have Tuesdays, too. One may remember your joke exactly. Another may have spent the presentation trying to decide whether to leave early because his dad was in the hospital. A third may remember the slide because she found an error in it that she then had to spend Friday fixing. If your aim is to know what happened in their lives, sooner or later you’ll run out of things you can learn by staring harder at your own.

The witness gets to write the questions

Of course, your own memory would be an excellent place to start if you could just run it from the beginning with the sound on. You can see the chip in that yellow bowl. You can see which of your coworker’s fingers was holding the place on the page. A scene with production values like these feels expensive enough to be true.

In 1974 Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed people footage of car accidents and asked about the cars’ speed. A change in the verb in the question shifted the estimates people gave. In a second experiment, some participants were asked about cars that had smashed into each other, others about cars that had hit each other. A week later everyone was asked whether they had seen broken glass. Sixteen out of fifty in the smashed group said yes. Seven out of fifty in the hit group did. There was no broken glass in the film. [3]

Put the two answers on the same scale. Sixteen out of fifty is 32 percent. Seven out of fifty is 14 percent. Eighteen percentage points separate the groups on a question about glass that was never there. Most people in both groups still said no. If you want to know whether this particular person saw what they say they saw, the experiment gives you a reason to look closely at how you asked. It supplies no little red light that comes on when your uncle is wrong.

Now think about the questions you bring to a memory of your own. When did she look up again? leaves you searching for a detail. How could I have humiliated her like that? comes with part of the film already shot. The second question might turn out to be entirely fair. It would still be an odd choice for the opening question if you honestly wanted to hear her account. For three years you’ve been asking the witness leading questions, and you’re also the witness.

Family kitchens pose the same problem under much more pleasant conditions. Suppose the yellow bowl turns up in a photograph and it’s actually a colander. Or suppose Ben remembers the room fine and swears there was never a bowl of any kind. The smell on your thumb did something real for you this afternoon. That leaves plenty of room for honest disagreement about the kitchenware. You can love the memory and still be curious enough to make a phone call.

Richard Feynman put a particular obligation on the person telling the story. Speaking to Caltech graduates in 1974, he used an example that deserves to get back to a supermarket. He had heard an advertisement for cooking oil. What bothered him was the implication that the oil had a special advantage. If a competing oil behaved the same way under the same conditions, a customer might reasonably want to hear that part too. For Feynman, scientific integrity included volunteering information that would let somebody reach a different conclusion from your own. [4]

Take your account of the meeting and write the line of copy you’ve been leaving off the label. Perhaps it’s that your coworker had asked people beforehand to give her a moment if she lost her place, and you went ahead and got the laugh anyway. That would make the story a lot less comfortable. Perhaps she came up to you later and told you the interruption gave her time to find the page. That would make it less comfortable in an entirely different way if you’ve come to depend on this incident as proof of what a piece of shit you are. A person can get protective of their worst opinion of themselves. They’ve put in the hours.

You probably don’t have either of those pieces of information. Leave the space for them empty. That takes a peculiar kind of discipline for an animal that can supply the setting, the dialogue and both sides of a reconciliation without moving an inch closer to the register. The empty space is where another person gets a chance to surprise you.

The unreasonable privilege of noticing

The man ahead of you drops a coin. He watches it roll under the gum display and for a moment his shoulders go up around his ears. You’ve spent long enough in your own private movie theater this afternoon to recognize the look of somebody being observed by an imaginary crowd. Or perhaps his back hurts and he’s wondering how the hell he’s going to get down there. You bend down and pick up the coin. He thanks you, counts out what he owes, and leaves. You’ll never learn what story he was in. It turns out you could give him back his money anyway.

Consciousness undersells this pleasure. You can change the end of somebody’s afternoon before you’ve worked out the beginning. The coin goes back into the man’s palm. Among the people entitled to a theory of his childhood, you remain far down the list.

Another one is being interested in something that has no opinion of you whatsoever. Look through a telescope on a clear night. That smudge you can’t quite bring into focus might be a galaxy. People built instruments, argued about the measurements and worked out a history of the universe stretching back about 13.8 billion years. That age is an inference from observations within a cosmological model. Change the assumptions and you have to do the calculation again and see how well the model fits. [5] The child who used to stand under Ben’s arm belongs to a species that has managed to ask a question on that scale and get somewhere with it. For a minute you might enjoy that for its own sake.

Then somebody is bound to say that when you consider how small we are, the things you spend your days worrying about don’t matter. It’s a funny inference to draw from getting a better look at something. Suppose Ben calls while your eye is pressed to the telescope. He’s had a fall. He’s all right, he thinks, and he’d like you to come over. He mentions third that he’s calling from the kitchen floor. You’ve learned something new about what to do with the next hour. The galaxy is exactly where you left it. Nothing in its mass, distance or distinguished age has altered in the slightest.

From the fact that one thing is bigger than another you can establish which would need the larger box. Getting from there to what you owe your uncle takes a different kind of reasoning. He stood you under his arm in a kitchen when you were small. He’s asked for help. Those facts bear on the choice. If anything in astronomy can tell you whose turn it is to drive Ben home from the emergency room, it has been disgracefully withheld from families everywhere.

On some evenings, of course, Ben is fine and you could happily spend the whole night looking through the telescope. Take those too. For a while you forgot to check how you were doing as a person. You were taking a look at the universe, and the view held.

The clock inside the room

Back in the store you still want to know whose kitchen it was. For months now, whenever Ben has crossed your mind, you’ve thought about having a real catch-up. A real catch-up requires a quiet Sunday, two free hours and the mental condition of a person who has recently returned from a moderately expensive retreat. Sometimes you send him a picture of something instead. He sends back a thumbs-up large enough to have been applied by a man wearing oven mitts.

Try a little arithmetic. Suppose you and Ben talk twice a year. Give yourselves another five years at that rate and you have ten conversations. Make it once a month and the number becomes sixty. Five years is a number you picked out of the air for a man in his late seventies. Nobody offered you five years. How often you call is one of the few inputs to that calculation you can do something about this week.

Ten calls and sixty calls leave out the same awkward variable. Ben might like ten long conversations in which you actually listen. After sixty calls conducted while you unload the dishwasher, he may start screening them. What I like about doing the sum is that it gives you a unit you can spend this week. You can’t call your uncle for a year. You can call him on Sunday.

Think of the things we arrange beautifully in preparation for our own absence. Someone will have the password. Someone will know which trash can goes out on Thursday. Your inbox may still receive a reminder to complete the employee wellness survey on the morning of your funeral. It will be marked Action required. What an efficient little corner of the universe we have constructed. It can solicit feedback from a corpse. Making ten minutes to speak to a person while both of you are available takes a surprising amount of initiative.

That thought can go sour if you let it. A man eating lunch over the sink doesn’t need a voice in his ear asking whether this is how he would choose to spend the last sandwich of his life. Christ, let him eat the sandwich. A finite life includes doing the dishes, being annoyed about the dishes, and occasionally leaving them for the morning because somebody you like has pulled a chair up to the table. What finitude gives a decision is a cost. You’re entitled to decide that lunch is worth it.

The cashier takes the lemon. A small crescent of yellow has lodged under your thumbnail. While you wait for the receipt you open your calendar to Sunday and write three words in the empty square. Call Uncle Ben. You can put a call on the calendar. The entry gives you no say in whether he picks up or how much of that kitchen he remembers. You pay for the lemon.

On Sunday he picks up on the fourth ring. You had meant to work your way around to the bowl after asking how he was. What comes out is “Ben, did we ever have a yellow mixing bowl?”

“Blue,” he says, with the immediate confidence of a man who has been disagreeing with members of your family for longer than you’ve been alive.

“The one with the chip?”

A pause. “Hold on.” You hear a chair scrape, a cabinet door stick and then give. He sets something down beside the phone. “Blue,” he says. “Big chip by the rim. Your mother always turned that side to the wall.”

It hadn’t occurred to you that while you were busy having a childhood, Ben was acquiring a serviceable bowl. To you it has spent twenty years in a memory. He appears to have put it to work.

“How come you still have it?”

“Where else am I supposed to keep the screws?” He gives the bowl a little shake. You can hear them. “You got a minute?”

You sit down.
