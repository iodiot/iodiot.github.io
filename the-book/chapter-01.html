Chapter 01Congratulations, You're Conscious. Sorry About That.

You came in for a lemon. While you wait to pay for it, your brain would like to discuss something shitty you said three years ago.

On Tuesday afternoon you stand in the checkout line holding the fruit. The person ahead of you is paying in coins. Somewhere behind you a child is making the case for a cereal whose chief ingredient appears to be a cartoon tiger.

There’s a nick in the peel. You press it with your thumbnail and get a little lemon oil on your fingers. For a second it’s summer in somebody’s kitchen. Your uncle Ben is there, tipping flour into a chipped yellow bowl. You used to be small enough to stand under his arm while he did it.

Then the mind arrives with a clipboard.

Three years ago, at a meeting, a colleague lost her place halfway through a presentation. You made a joke. People laughed. You can still see her hand resting on the page while she waited for them to stop. Now, between the gum and the batteries, you think of the thing you wish you’d said instead.

The cashier hasn’t even scanned the lemon yet. Your mind has convened a hearing, heard the evidence and scheduled a further hearing to find out why the first one took so long.

Congratulations. You are conscious. Sorry about that.

You got two journeys out of a minute in line. A smell took you to a kitchen you would happily visit again. A memory took you to a meeting you would pay good money to have missed the first time. Both places were available to you while you stood perfectly still. And you could catch yourself traveling. That last ability is where things get interesting.

The mind that watches itself

The smell of that lemon is already an experience. You can have it with your eyes closed, without knowing the name of the fruit or having a single theory about how a nose works. Then you can turn your attention to the experience itself. How odd, you think, that the smell came back more clearly than the kitchen. And how odd that a person can be moved by the memory of an uncle’s forearm. It was a perfectly ordinary forearm. You doubt Ben has written any poems about it.

Take the lemon seriously for a moment. A chemist could tell you about the oil released by the damaged peel. A scientist who studies smell could tell you how molecules in the air come to stimulate receptors in your nose. You could learn a great deal from both of them and still want an answer to a peculiar question. How could you convey that particular smell to somebody who has never smelled anything?

In 1982 the philosopher Frank Jackson imagined a scientist called Mary who knows all the physical facts about human color vision while living in a black-and-white environment. Then she gets to see color. Does she learn something the instant she sees it? [1] You can take the question seriously whichever answer you favor. Perhaps what she acquires is a way of recognizing an experience she could previously only describe. Perhaps the description really did leave something out. Philosophers have been arguing about this for decades. Mary is a character in a thought experiment. Nobody has kept a woman in a monochrome flat since 1982 on the off chance that philosophy might one day clear the paperwork.

Here in the store there’s a distinction you can actually try out. For a moment pay attention to the smell. Then pay attention to what happened when you smelled it. The second task can take you quite far from the first. You are soon wondering why you haven’t called Ben, whether that makes you a bad nephew or niece, whether good relatives need a piece of fruit to remind them of each other. Meanwhile you would have to sniff your fingers again to say much about the lemon.

That capacity to change what you’re attending to is useful. If you are furious, noticing your fury can give you just enough time to put the phone down. Perhaps the message you composed at full boil makes a decent point. It could still do with losing the bit where you explain to another adult what their mother should have done differently in 1987. A person who can notice a thought while there’s still time to edit it has been given an extraordinary gift.

On the other hand, a person who can notice being embarrassed can also get embarrassed about the embarrassment. Now you’re judging yourself for going red and going a little redder because you know you’re doing it. A brief emotion gets a management team. Once the team is in place, it has the usual institutional interest in making sure the original problem lives long enough to justify next year’s budget.

Reflection, or the hamster with a law degree

Take the meeting. One detail keeps snagging at you, the hand on the page. On the first pass you remember something that had escaped you at the time. You got a laugh while another person waited to finish a sentence. Next time someone loses their place you could give them a moment, or ask whether they want the slide put back up. The memory has paid its way.

On the fifteenth pass you’re studying the same hand for a different purpose. You want to know how thoroughly she disliked you. There are no fresh witnesses at checkout. Your evidence consists of a three-year-old gesture and the testimony of a defendant who has also appointed himself judge. By two in the morning the hamster has obtained a law degree and is billing you by the hour.

There’s a respectable objection here. If you hurt somebody, why should you get to stop thinking about it just because the thinking has become unpleasant? Perhaps the first fourteen passes were evasions. Perhaps the fifteenth is when you finally admit that, at the time, you knew exactly whose expense the laugh came at. People can spend years coming round to an admission like that. A clock would be a fairly lousy instrument for distinguishing honest reflection from going round in circles.

Try listening to the question instead. When the question was What did she have to do because of what I said?, you saw the hand on the page and understood something about the cost to her. When it became What sort of person would have said a thing like that?, a surprising number of hours became available for discussing your character. You could keep proceedings going until one of you dies of old age. Should it be you, do see whether the executor can keep the case open. It would be a shame to let a little thing like the death of the accused bring anybody else’s needs back into the picture.

The difference matters if you’re considering an apology. Try writing her a few lines. The first version starts with what you did, passes through a detailed medical history of your conscience, and ends with the hope that she understands what a difficult three years it has been for you. Imagine opening that email five minutes before lunch. She went to work to do a presentation. Apparently the job also came with lifetime responsibility for the aftercare of the guy who interrupted it.

There is a shorter version. You remember making a joke when she lost her place. You wish you’d given her a moment to speak. You’re sorry. Whether it would help her to hear from you now depends on things this little story hasn’t told us, including whether you still work together and what she’s already said about it. You have enough to make a start on your own behavior either way. She gets to have a response of her own, up to and including a blank look and a request to please pass the coffee.

This is the distinction I’d keep from that whole expensive night with the hamster. A second look can turn up a missing fact, make room for another person or leave you with a question you could actually ask. Sometimes the most honest thing it produces is a decision to wait. The kind of brooding I mean by rumination keeps returning the same evidence with a demand for a verdict it can never supply. You can recognize that happening and still have trouble interrupting it. A person whose mind keeps dragging them back through something painful may need help and company with it. Knowing the name of the hamster gives you very little leverage over his working hours.

How many people saw that?

At the far end of the store somebody calls your name. You turn, and for an instant a stranger in your brother’s coat becomes your brother. Then the stranger takes off his hat. It was quite a good guess. You know the coat. Your brother shops here. All it took to settle the matter was a second look at the man’s face. If only the other guesses that pass through your head came with a man willing to turn round and take his hat off.

With people you love, a second look can be harder to come by. Suppose Ben leaves a message asking you to ring him back. You can hear something in his voice. In the lift on the way home you try to work out whether it’s the bad kind of tired. It might be. It could also be the voice of a man reading his own phone number off a scrap of paper because he still believes a telephone message requires a return address. You can turn the volume up. After a point all that gets you is a louder version of the same uncertainty. To find out whether he’s all right, somebody is going to have to get him on the phone.

Consider the speed at which you furnished the audience for that meeting. You remember hearing laughter. When you run the scene back, everybody seems to have understood the joke and filed the incident somewhere accessible. You could practically go round the room naming the people who now think slightly less of you. This is an impressive amount of intelligence to have gathered while staring at a woman’s hand.

Thomas Gilovich, Victoria Medvec and Kenneth Savitsky found a way to put a number on a small piece of this. In one experiment, students were sent into a room wearing a T-shirt with Barry Manilow’s face on it. It was a shirt the wearers had reason to feel conspicuous in. Afterward they estimated how many of the people in the room would be able to identify the person on the shirt. Their average estimate was about 46 percent. The observers’ average identification rate was about 23 percent. A wearer’s guess, on average, doubled the size of that particular audience. [2]

There’s a tempting use for this finding. Divide your worst memory by two, round down for lunch breaks and declare yourself forgiven. Unfortunately for this attractive piece of mathematics, the experiment concerned remembering a face on a shirt. It established precisely nothing about how your colleague experienced the wait for the laughter to stop. Even if every other person in the room had forgotten it by lunchtime, there were still two people there who mattered to what happened.

It works the other way too. If you’re queer in a room where a remark about your partner can cost you your job, or the only woman at a table that greets every slip of yours with an indulgent smile, you have good reasons to pay attention to who noticed what. An experiment in a college classroom can show that people sometimes overestimate the attention they receive. It can’t tell you what any given room is safe for. Good judgment leaves room for what the person who has to walk in there tomorrow already knows about it.

Back at the checkout you can make a smaller, stranger concession. The others have Tuesdays, too. One may remember your joke exactly. Another may have spent the presentation trying to decide whether to leave early because his dad was in hospital. A third may remember the slide because she found an error in it that she then had to spend Friday fixing. If your aim is to know what happened in their lives, sooner or later you’ll run out of things you can learn by staring harder at your own.

The witness gets to write the questions

Of course, your own memory would be an excellent place to start if you could just run it from the beginning with the sound on. You can see the chip in that yellow bowl. You can see which of your colleague’s fingers was holding the place on the page. A scene with production values like these feels expensive enough to be true.

In 1974 Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed people footage of car accidents and asked about the cars’ speed. A change in the verb in the question shifted the estimates people gave. In a second experiment, some participants were asked about cars that had smashed into each other, others about cars that had hit each other. A week later everyone was asked whether they had seen broken glass. Sixteen out of fifty in the smashed group said yes. Seven out of fifty in the hit group did. There was no broken glass in the film. [3]

Look at the people those figures leave out. Most people in both groups answered no. And nobody has to decide whether each mistaken yes came with a vivid private picture of glass. The experiment measured what people reported. What makes it unsettling is that two groups could watch the same film and, after one word differed in an earlier question, give different answers a week later about something you could go back and check.

Now think about the questions you bring to a memory of your own. When did she look up again? leaves you searching for a detail. How could I have humiliated her like that? comes with part of the film already shot. The second question might turn out to be entirely fair. It would still be an odd choice for the opening question if you honestly wanted to hear her account. For three years you’ve been asking the witness leading questions, and you are also the witness.

Family kitchens pose the same problem under much more pleasant conditions. Suppose the yellow bowl turns up in a photograph and it’s actually a colander. Or suppose Ben remembers the room perfectly well and swears there was never a bowl of any kind. The smell on your thumb did something real for you this afternoon. That leaves quite a bit of room for honest disagreement about the kitchenware. You can love the memory and still be curious enough to make a phone call.

Richard Feynman put a particular obligation on the person telling the story. Speaking to Caltech graduates in 1974, he used an example that deserves to get back to a supermarket. He had heard an advertisement for cooking oil. What bothered him was the implication that the oil had a special advantage. If a competing oil behaved the same way under the same conditions, a customer might quite reasonably want to hear that bit too. For Feynman, scientific integrity included volunteering information that would let somebody reach a different conclusion from your own. [4]

Take your account of the meeting and write the little bit of copy you’ve been leaving off the label. Perhaps it’s that your colleague had asked people beforehand to give her a moment if she lost her place, and you went ahead and got the laugh anyway. That would make the story considerably less comfortable. Perhaps she came up to you later and told you the interruption gave her time to find the page. That would make it less comfortable in an entirely different way if you’ve come to depend on this incident as proof of what a piece of shit you are. A person can get quite protective of their worst opinion of themselves. They’ve put in the hours.

You probably don’t have either of those pieces of information. Leave the space for them empty. That takes a peculiar kind of discipline for an animal that can supply the setting, the dialogue and both sides of a reconciliation without moving an inch closer to the register. Curiosity becomes possible in that little space. You’re interested in an answer you don’t already have, and there is another person in a position to surprise you.

The unreasonable privilege of noticing

The man ahead of you drops a coin. He watches it roll under the display of chewing gum and for a moment his shoulders go up around his ears. You have spent long enough in your own private cinema this afternoon to recognize the look of somebody being observed by an imaginary crowd. Or perhaps his back hurts and he’s wondering how the hell he’s going to get down there. You bend down and pick up the coin. He thanks you, counts out what he owes, and leaves. You will never learn what story he was in. It turns out you could give him back his money anyway.

This side of consciousness gets a bit less publicity. You can imagine an inconvenience from the height of another person’s shoulders. You can remember being small beside a man making a cake. You can hear a piece of music a friend loved, years after the two of you stopped speaking, and catch yourself waiting for the bit where she always started to sing. For a few bars, you get to hear something of what she heard. That is a remarkable use for the same equipment that once spent a whole evening interpreting the absence of an exclamation mark.

There’s also the sheer pleasure of being interested in something that has no opinion of you whatsoever. Look through a telescope on a clear night. That smudge you can’t quite bring into focus might be a galaxy. People built instruments, argued about the measurements and worked out a history of the universe stretching back about 13.8 billion years. That age is an inference from observations within a cosmological model. Change the assumptions and you have to do the calculation again and see how well the model fits. [5] The child who used to stand under Ben’s arm belongs to a species that has managed to ask a question on that scale and get somewhere with it. For a minute you might enjoy that just for its own sake.

Then somebody is bound to say that when you consider how small we are, the things you spend your days worrying about don’t matter. It’s a funny inference to draw from getting a better look at something. Suppose Ben rings while your eye is pressed to the telescope. He’s had a fall. He’s all right, he thinks, but he’d like you to come over. You have learned something new about what to do with the next hour. The galaxy is exactly where you left it. Nothing in its mass, distance or distinguished age has altered in the slightest.

From the fact that one thing is bigger than another you can establish which would require the larger box. To get from there to what a particular person owes his uncle takes a different kind of reasoning. Somebody cared for him when he was small. Somebody has asked for help. Those facts bear on the choice. If anything in astronomy can tell you whose turn it is to drive Ben home, it has been disgracefully withheld from families everywhere.

There are evenings, of course, when Ben is fine and you could happily spend the whole night looking through the telescope. Take those too. A book about the miseries a mind can inflict on its owner ought to make at least this much room for one of the pleasures. For a while you forgot to check how you were doing as a person. You were having a look at the universe, and the view held.

The clock inside the room

Back in the store you still want to know whose kitchen it was. For months now, whenever Ben has crossed your mind, you’ve thought about having a proper catch-up. A proper catch-up requires a quiet Sunday, two free hours and the mental condition of a person who has recently returned from a moderately expensive retreat. Sometimes you send him a picture of something instead. He sends back a thumbs-up large enough to have been applied by a man wearing oven gloves.

Try a little arithmetic. Suppose you and Ben talk twice a year. Give yourselves another five years at that rate and you have ten conversations. Make it once a month and the number becomes sixty. Five years is a number you picked out of the air. Nobody offered you five years. How often you call is one of the few inputs to that calculation you can do something about this week.

The arithmetic falls silent about quality. Sixty dutiful phone calls made with one eye on the clock could fairly easily make a man nostalgic for the ten. You may have an uncle you have excellent reasons for calling roughly once a geological epoch. Ben may ring you every day or live downstairs. The part of the calculation worth keeping is the unit. Years sound like an expanse of open country. Conversations are things you can remember spending, and then it matters a little more what you might like to spend one on.

Think of the things we arrange beautifully in preparation for our own absence. Someone will have the password. Someone will know which bin goes out on Thursday. Your inbox may still receive a reminder to complete the employee wellbeing survey on the morning of your funeral. It will be marked Action required. What an efficient little corner of the universe we have constructed. It can solicit feedback from a corpse. Making ten minutes to speak to a person while both of you are available takes a surprising amount of initiative.

That thought can go sour if you let it. A man eating lunch over the sink doesn’t need a voice in his ear asking whether this is how he would choose to spend the last sandwich of his life. Christ, let him eat the sandwich. A finite life includes doing the dishes, being cross about the dishes, and occasionally leaving them for the morning because somebody you like has pulled a chair up to the table. What finitude gives a decision is a cost. You’re entitled to decide that lunch is worth it.

The cashier takes the lemon. There’s a small crescent of yellow under your thumbnail. While you wait for the receipt you open your calendar to Sunday and write three words in the empty square. Call Uncle Ben. You can put a call on the calendar. The entry gives you precisely no say in whether he picks up or how much of that kitchen he remembers. You pay for the lemon.

On Sunday he picks up on the fourth ring. You had meant to work your way round to the bowl after asking how he was. What comes out is “Ben, did we ever have a yellow mixing bowl?”

“Blue,” he says, with the immediate confidence of a man who has been disagreeing with members of your family for longer than you’ve been alive.

“The one with the chip?”

There’s a pause. “Hang on.” You hear a chair scrape, a cupboard door stick and then give. He sets something down beside the phone. “Blue,” he says. “Big chip by the rim. Your mother always turned that side to the wall.”

There were all sorts of ways you pictured this call going. In a few you were funny. In one you managed to tell him exactly what those afternoons had meant to you, in the improbably handsome sentences people get to deliver in cars on the way home from saying the wrong thing. In none of them did Ben keep the bowl.

“How come you still have it?”

“Where else am I supposed to keep the screws?” He gives the bowl a little shake. You can hear them. “You got a minute?”

You sit down.
