Chapter 22Choose Your Suffering

By the time the charity walk begins, Dan has gotten a blister opening the box of blister bandages.

“Good,” says his brother Sam. “Gets the uncertainty out of the way.”

They’re in a parking lot on the coast at a quarter to eight in the morning. The sea is the color of a spoon. Across the road several dozen adults are pinning numbers to themselves in preparation for traveling eighteen miles on foot to a parking lot much like this one. A bus runs between the two parking lots every hour. Every great human undertaking deserves one fact that makes it look ridiculous.

Dan turns fifty next month. Five days a week he stands at a service counter and tells people, kindly, what something with that many miles on it is going to need. He’d like a photograph that says the estimate doesn’t apply to him. A man at a finish line, windburned, a number on his chest and eighteen miles behind him. It’ll go on the shelf behind the service desk, where the customers can look at it while he explains their brake pads and the two advisors who’ve started calling him “chief” can draw their own conclusions.

The money goes to the community pool’s fund for accessible locker rooms. It’s a good place for money, and he’s raised his share. But the pool would cash the checks if he took the bus. The eighteen miles are for the photograph.

He wants his younger daughter in it. June is eight. A man who finishes alone looks like a man with something to prove. A man who finishes with his kid looks like a father, and Dan would prefer to do his proving that way. To get her here he made a deal. Eight miles along the route, a bird blind overlooks a patch of tidal mud where avocets sometimes feed. She’s seen pictures of the way they sweep their upturned bills from side to side through the water. She wants to see one do it. He told her they’d stop there for an hour.

She’s brought her mother’s binoculars, a squashed roll, a pencil, a bird book with four paper slips sticking out of it and a small amount of faith in what her father means by an hour. Carla has the car today, along with Nora, who heard the word eighteen. That’s why this day has buses in it.

Dan has two maps. One is the organizers’ handout. The other is his own, a long strip of coast taped together from six printed sheets, with distances, projected times, rest stops and their arrival in the second parking lot marked in four colors. He opens it to show Sam mile eight, and a gust off the water takes it up over his head like a sail. He stands there holding both ends of eighteen miles. June walks in underneath with the binoculars up. She’s found a gull standing on a trash can. Her father hasn’t budgeted any time for the gull.

There’s an idea going around that you learn more about what you want in life from the hardship you’ll put up with for it than from your list of prizes. Fair enough. Plenty of people like the idea of writing a novel. Fewer like the idea of an evening spent hunting for a verb that doesn’t make a kiss sound like a drainage procedure. Wanting to have written a book is compatible with wanting to do almost anything except write the bastard. What you’ll sit through does tell you something your list of prizes left out.

Notice what’s tucked into put up with, though. A person may put up with pain because something matters to them. It doesn’t follow that the more it hurts the more it matters, or that pain is worth anything by itself, or that everyone within reach of the volunteer has volunteered too. Otherwise the most devoted thing Dan could do as a father would be to put a small stone in each of June’s shoes.

At the starting line the course marshal says people can leave the route at either of the marked bus stops. Tell a volunteer, so the organizers know who’s off the course. The pool gets the flat donations regardless. Some pledges are tied to miles, so check what your supporters promised. Nobody gets a prize for finishing first. Everybody who finishes gets a medal, and everybody who checks out gets a banana, wherever they do it. A heckler asks if he can have the banana now. The course marshal advises him to consider a career in management.

The part of the bill you ordered

Here’s a test for a hardship. Suppose somebody offered you the same result at half the pain. Would that spoil it?

Dan likes walking a long way under his own power. He likes how slowly the view changes, how his stride settles after a couple of miles, how a dot on the path ahead turns out to be a family having a severe discussion about a dog. Drive him to the finish and you’ve saved his feet and deleted what he came for. Loosen a lace that’s pinching and his project is intact. Anybody who thinks blisters are a tax on authentic experience can send the amount directly to the pool.

For June the goods are different. Walking is how you get to the blind with two people she likes. She knows enough about birdwatching to know an afternoon can go by without the bird you wanted making any effort to participate. Carla offered her an excellent close-up video from the comfort of the couch. She’s seen the video. She wants to sit on a bench with a slot in the wall in front of her and see what’s on the other side today. Gloves would spare her cold fingers and cost her nothing. Fast-forward the wait for a live bird and you’ve handed her the video again.

So some of the pain is packaging and some of it is the product. These two have come for different products, and so far only one of them has a map.

By the first mile Dan is out in front, setting a steady pace. June keeps up easily. Sam stops at a break in the brush to deal with a zipper. The group has gone quiet, the way people do when they’ve been loudly explaining why this is a great idea and have started breathing at the rate the idea requires.

In 1971 the philosopher Harry Frankfurt drew a distinction worth taking on a walk. You can want something, and you can have a view about which of your wants you’d like to be moved by. You want the cigarette, and you want the wish to quit to be the one that wins. Frankfurt calls a desire about which desire should govern what you do a second-order volition. It’s an ugly piece of equipment to carry in a backpack. It works when you unpack it.[1]

Dan wanted breakfast this morning. He also wanted to be a man who leaves the warm café and does the long walk he said he’d do. If the freshest impulse always got the deciding vote, he’d never get out of sight of the breakfast sandwiches. Freedom would be a thin thing if it meant taking orders from whichever part of you last saw a bun.

But a want doesn’t get wise by moving up a floor. Dan wants to be a man who finishes because he likes the walking. He also wants to be a man who finishes because of a counter at a dealership and a birthday with a zero in it. The second-order want can be a prick too. A man shouting instructions from a higher floor of the same building sometimes just has better acoustics.

Try a question no motivational poster has room for. If nobody ever found out how hard this was, would he still do it this way? For most of today the answer is yes. He’d walk this coast without a number on. The part that fails is the part with June in it. Nobody needs her at mile eighteen except the photograph.

At three miles a woman passes them at speed, stops at the next bench to photograph the plaque on it, and goes on. Dan catches up with her at a gate. She does this walk every year. A friend used to do it with her and can’t manage it now, so she brings home pictures of the bench inscriptions. One of the benches is for a small dog. Dan had taken her for the competition. She’s running a delivery service for a woman who likes benches.

At five miles June gets out the bird book. One paper slip marks the avocet. Another marks the pintail. A third marks an illustrated key to ducks as seen from behind, swimming away. The fourth slip has the number of the bus home on it, in case her feet get tired. Carla put that one in. She also made three lunches at six this morning and wrote a name on each. The man who chooses his suffering usually has somebody at home packing its lunch.

The suffering you already paid for

Around mile eight the path forks. The official markers climb to the ridge and carry on along the coast. A short spur drops to the blind. A bus stop by the visitors’ center makes this one of the places you can check out. The volunteer here has a clipboard, a crate of bananas and a folding stool that’s making a strong case for the shorter day.

It’s taken them two hours and fifty minutes to get here, what with the gate, the map and a debate about the minimum age for photographing a dead crab. Dan had allowed two forty. Ten miles remain. At three miles an hour that’s three hours and twenty minutes of walking. The bus he picked, the one that gets them home in time for a dinner reservation, leaves the far parking lot in four hours. That leaves forty minutes for everything that isn’t walking, which means lunch, checking out at the finish, finding the stop, and getting down to the blind and back. He promised an hour. He’s thinking ten minutes with the birds ought to cover it.

“Does he know about the bus?” June asks.

“Who?”

“The bird.”

Sam puts his bag down. “How long did you tell her?”

“An hour.” Dan studies the map. “About an hour.”

“You said an hour,” says June. She’s picking at the paper on her roll and doesn’t look at the map.

An hour was easy to promise at the kitchen table in November. Out here it costs twenty minutes more than he has, and that’s if an eight-year-old can do the next ten miles at three miles an hour. She can’t. He knows she can’t. Somewhere around mile thirteen he’d be carrying the binoculars, and around fifteen he’d be carrying her, and the photograph would show a child who’d been crying. He’d put it on the shelf anyway.

So here’s the bill. For Dan to get the picture he wants, June gives up fifty minutes of the only thing she came for. Then she walks ten more miles on eight-year-old legs, eighteen in all, to stand in a paper frame beside a man who needed her there. Nobody’s asked whether she has anything to prove.

Dan looks back down the path. They’ve done eight miles. He’s paid the entry fee, raised decent money and told half the service department about all eighteen. It’d be a waste to stop now, wouldn’t it? A waste of what?

Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer asked people to imagine they’d paid a hundred dollars for a ski weekend in Michigan and fifty for one in Wisconsin, then discovered both fell on the same weekend. Neither ticket could be sold or returned. The scenario said they expected to enjoy Wisconsin more. Many chose Michigan anyway. The authors also studied real theater subscribers who’d been charged different prices for season tickets. In the first months, the people who’d paid more went to more plays. In both cases a price nobody could get back was steering what people did next. The ski trip was imaginary, and in the theater the difference showed up only in those first months.[2]

Whatever Dan does with the next ten miles, he’s walked the first eight. The entry fee won’t improve the view from the ridge. Not everything behind him is sunk, though. The sponsorship on his page is flat donations except for one pledge. Mal, who used to beat him at pool on Thursdays, has put up a dollar for every mile Dan completes. Ten more miles means ten more dollars for the pool. That’s real, and it counts. It would take some nerve to tell June that a community pool’s locker rooms depend on her looking at a bird for less than ten minutes. She’d be entitled to ask how an organization that size came to rest its future on an eight-year-old with binoculars.

“I want the picture,” Dan says. “Me and her at the finish. I’ve been thinking about it since November.”

It’s the first true thing anybody’s said about the day, and all three of them look relieved to hear it. The pool, the birthday, the entry fee and a faint moral defect in people who ride buses are all relieved of the job of explaining what Dad wants. Dad wants a picture.

“I want my hour,” says June.

“I could stay with her,” says Sam.

Dan looks at him. “You wanted to do eighteen.”

“I wanted to come with you. I’ve come eight miles with you. If you want me for the other ten, ask.”

It hadn’t occurred to Dan that his brother’s company was something he’d have to ask for. He’d checked three names on the entry form. Two of those people had been in the room at the time.

Who gets told they chose it

Before a family argument on a pleasant walk turns into a theory of the good life, look at where the money is going. The locker rooms are for people who need a wider door, a bench at the right height, a hoist, and sometimes an hour and a second person to get changed for a swim. Nobody tells them they chose it. Nobody sponsors it by the mile. Tell a woman working nights and a second job because the rent went up that she chose her suffering, and you can point to two contracts with her signature on them. You’ll also learn how short the distance is between a technical truth and the door.

A chosen hardship is one you can leave at a marked bus stop, with a banana. The word choice is only as good as the exits, and most of the hard things in a life don’t come with a course marshal.

Even on this path the choosing has been uneven. Dan chose eighteen miles. June chose a bird. Sam chose his brother. Carla chose none of it and made the lunches. One test for a hardship somebody’s proud of is whether the other people in it are allowed to change their minds. Carla saw to that with a bus number on a slip of paper. Dan never got around to saying it out loud.

“You sure you don’t mind staying?” he asks Sam.

“I’d like to see this duck.”

“It’s a shorebird,” says June. “The pintail’s the duck.”

“I’m glad we’re allowed one.”

So Dan will walk the rest at his own pace. Sam and June will check out here and take the bus home when they’ve had enough. The exchange about whose phone is charged, when Carla expects a text and which adult is delivering which child to which house takes longer than the arithmetic about walking pace, and it’s more useful. Dan signs June off the route on the volunteer’s sheet and tightens the strap on her binoculars. She loosens it as soon as he’s gone.

Sam wonders whether he made it too easy. By offering to stay, he let his brother off deciding what the promise was worth. He also wonders whether the visitors’ center sells fries. A man can hold a serious concern about his brother’s moral development and a strong private interest in fries.

Inside the blind are a bench, a long slot in the wall and a whiteboard where people write what they’ve seen. An avocet was here yesterday morning. The pencil on its string has been worn down to the size and color of something the last bird left behind. June sits with the binoculars almost out through the slot. Sam takes the bench beside her. Their feet clear the floor by different amounts.

Behind them, on the boardwalk by the open door, a small brown shorebird stands for most of a minute and studies their backs. Neither of them turns around. It’s the closest any bird will come all day, and it never makes the whiteboard.

For ten minutes nothing happens. Then something happens which June identifies as a small plastic bag. Then the plastic bag moves under its own power and is promoted to something alive. Sam is beginning to understand the respect birdwatchers have for a person who can identify an ass.

The story your feet tell later

Up on the ridge Dan is enjoying about half of his walk, which is a good return on a walk. The wind has gone around to the side. Every so often a gap in the cloud drops sunlight on a patch of water, and one piece of the gray surface lights up as if somebody had a lamp under it. He finds a speed he can keep. For a few minutes his sense of being a man with something to prove shrinks to a pair of legs that work.

Then comes a long fenced detour around a soft stretch of the bluff trail. No view. Nothing is being tested except how a man of nearly fifty feels about wire mesh in quantity. He’ll leave it out when he tells the story. He still has to walk it.

The exchange rate between a hard experience and the story you tell about it afterward deserves some suspicion. In a study by Daniel Kahneman and colleagues, people went through two uncomfortable cold-water episodes. The longer one was the shorter one plus an extra stretch in which the water got a little less cold. Asked which they’d rather repeat, many picked the longer one. The extra time hadn’t undone any of the discomfort. It had improved the ending, and the ending did the remembering. These were brief episodes in a lab. Nobody measured whether a cheerful dessert justifies twenty years of a miserable marriage.[3]

This matters to Dan because he’s been composing the ending since November. The day was built backward from a picture at a finish line, and a good ending can launder a lot of afternoon. If June were beside him in it, they might both enjoy telling the story eighteen months from now. That wouldn’t make her ten miles any shorter while she was walking them.

The best story I know about what suffering buys is about three eggs. On June 27, 1911, three men from Robert Scott’s last expedition left their hut in Antarctica to walk to Cape Crozier, more than sixty miles away, in the dark of the polar winter. Emperor penguins lay their eggs in midwinter, and Cape Crozier was the only place anybody knew they did it. The naturalist Edward Wilson hoped the embryos would show how birds had evolved from reptiles. He took Henry Bowers and Apsley Cherry-Garrard. They hauled their sleds for nineteen days to get there. The temperature fell to seventy-seven and a half degrees below zero Fahrenheit. A blizzard tore their tent away, and they found it two days later, half a mile off. They got back on August 1 with three eggs.

Wilson and Bowers died the following year with Scott, on the way back from the South Pole. In 1913 Cherry-Garrard, the last of the three, carried the eggs to the Natural History Museum in London. He wrote up the visit himself, as a short play, so we have only his side. The first official he met said, “What do you want? This ain’t an egg-shop.” The chief custodian took the eggs. Cherry-Garrard asked for a receipt. He was told it wasn’t necessary. He stayed until he had one. The final report on the embryos came out in 1934 and concluded that they hadn’t added much to what was known about penguins.[4]

The museum didn’t pay by the degree. What a thing cost you is your own business, and the man at the desk is only looking at the egg. The odder part is that Cherry-Garrard never called the journey a waste. What he was glad of, in the book he wrote about it, was the two men he’d hauled with. He also wrote this: “If you march your Winter Journeys you will have your reward, so long as all you want is a penguin’s egg.” I read that as a man who went for an egg and came home valuing something else. You can read it sourer.

Those were three grown men who each chose the journey, for a scientific question that could have come out the other way. None of them brought a child. And it’s told by the one who lived to write it well, which is how most stories about worthwhile suffering reach us.

Dan’s egg is a photograph. It’ll be received about the way the museum received the eggs, by people waiting to hear about their brakes.

By mile sixteen he’s being passed by people he passed this morning. It bothers him less than he expected. His knees have opinions. His right foot would like to revisit the socks. He still wants the last two miles, and it’s worth something to find out, sixteen miles in, that he wants what he said he wanted at the start. On another day the job would be knowing where the next bus stop is.

He reaches the second parking lot about twenty minutes before his bus. A volunteer writes down his number and hands him a tin medal. A bright paper frame has been set up for finish photographs. He asks the woman behind him to take his. She takes two, one of them the right way up. He looks at it on the bus. A windburned man of forty-nine, alone in a paper frame, with a tin medal and room beside him. It proves he walked eighteen miles. It’s less clear who asked. Mal owes the pool eighteen dollars, and June didn’t have to earn the last ten of them.

She and Sam are already home when he calls. They stayed in the blind for forty-seven minutes. They saw a pintail and a thin brown bird nobody could put a name to. No avocet. Did she mind? Of course she damn well minded. She’d gone to a lot of trouble to see an avocet. Adults like to turn every disappointment a child has into something more nourishing. Sometimes you go to see a bird, the bird is elsewhere on business, and the day has one small, fair sadness in it about a bird.

Sam says he suggested the bus twice. She was cold, and he offered her his gloves. She took the gloves and turned down the bus. At forty-seven minutes she stood up and said she was done, with thirteen of her sixty unspent. It was her hour, and she spent what she wanted of it. Dan looks at the photograph again. He wouldn’t know that about her if she were standing in it.

“Will you go back with me?” she asks him.

“To the blind?”

“Yes.”

“What if it’s not there again?”

“Dad. I know how birds work.”

His first idea is to plan it around the tide, the bus and a stretch of coast he missed on the detour. His first idea would be improved by letting somebody else have the second. He asks how long she wants to go for.

“I don’t know. Do you have a morning?”

He checks. Next Saturday he has a whole morning. He writes the date down. For once he leaves the far end of the appointment empty.
