Chapter 22Choose Your Suffering

By the time the sponsored walk begins, Dan has got a blister opening the packet of blister plasters.

“Good,” says his brother Sam. “Gets the uncertainty out of the way.”

They are in a car park on the coast at a quarter to eight in the morning. The sea is the colour of a spoon. On the other side of the road several dozen adults are pinning numbers to themselves in preparation for travelling eighteen miles on foot to a car park remarkably similar to this one. Between the two car parks there is a regular bus service. Every great human undertaking deserves the presence of one fact that makes it look fucking ridiculous.

Dan wants to walk all eighteen miles. He’s fifty next month and he likes the idea that on his fiftieth birthday he’ll have this day behind him. The money goes to the local swimming pool’s fund for accessible changing facilities, which seems to him a good destination for money and, at this hour, a rather better destination for Dan. He’s been doing the miles. He knows what his feet will tolerate and has opinions on socks that would surprise a man who spent the first half of his life buying them at petrol stations.

Sam said he’d come with him. So did Dan’s younger daughter. In the case of the younger daughter an additional arrangement has been entered into. Eight miles along the route there’s a hide overlooking a patch of tidal mud where avocets sometimes feed. She has seen pictures of the way they move their extraordinary upturned bills from side to side through the water. She wants to see one do it. Her dad said they could stop there.

She has brought her mother’s binoculars, a squashed roll, a pencil, a bird book with four paper slips sticking out of it and a small amount of human faith in what the word stop means.

Dan has two maps. One gives eighteen miles of coast. The other is the same map in a waterproof case with the distances, projected walking times, comfort stops and expected arrival in the second car park marked in four different colours. By eight his daughter has been round the car park twice to look at a gull standing on a salt bin. Her father has not budgeted any time for the gull.

There’s a philosophical idea going round that a better way of asking what you want in life is to ask what you’re prepared to suffer for. Fair enough. People are keen on the idea of writing novels and considerably less keen on spending a pleasant evening trying to find a verb that doesn’t make a kiss sound like a drainage procedure. Wanting to have written a book is widely compatible with wanting to do almost anything except write the bastard. What you are willing to sit through does tell you something your list of attractive outcomes may have left out.

Notice the danger tucked into the phrase prepared to suffer. A person may be willing to put up with pain because something matters to them. It does not follow that the more they suffer the more it matters, that pain itself confers moral value, or that anyone within reach of the person who volunteered has also volunteered. Otherwise the most committed act of parenthood available to Dan would be putting a small stone in each of his daughter’s shoes.

At the starting line the marshal says people can leave at either of the signed bus stops if they’ve finished for the day. Tell a marshal so the organisers know who’s left the route. The pool will receive the flat donations in any event. Some people have pledges tied to miles, so check what your sponsors actually said. There are no prizes for getting to the finish first. There is a biscuit for everybody who checks out, wherever they do it. A heckler wants to know if you can get the biscuit now. The marshal advises him to consider a career in management.

The bit of the bill you asked for

Try a small exercise. Think of the last difficult thing you were glad you did. There’s a fair chance the difficulty has something to do with the gladness. You saved for a journey, looked after a friend, went back into a room where people had last seen you behaving like a distressed walrus. You may legitimately be proud of the cost. It took something out of you which you could have kept.

Now suppose somebody offered to give you the same result at half the cost. Would it spoil the thing? If the journey meant taking your dad to visit his brother, a cheaper train fare probably wouldn’t have made the visit spiritually worthless. If the aim was to learn how to play the last passage of a piece of music and somebody suggested replacing you with a recording for that bit, they would have misunderstood the goods being ordered. In one case you’d happily take the discount. In the other, part of the value lives in acquiring the capacity to do it yourself. Some pain can be removed at a profit. Some difficulty comes sewn into the particular experience you want. You need to be fairly specific before the question about suffering tells you much.

Dan likes walking a fair distance under his own power. He enjoys the slow change in the set of things he can see, the feel of his stride settling down after a couple of miles, the way an object you first notice as a dot on the path ahead eventually turns out to be a family having quite a severe discussion about a dog. Delivering him to the finish in a taxi would preserve his lunch booking and eliminate a good part of what he came for. Offering to loosen a lace that was pinching his foot would leave his project in excellent condition. An enthusiast with a theory that blisters are a tax on authentic experience may wish to send the relevant sum directly to the swimming pool.

For his daughter the goods are different. Walking is an affordable way to get to the hide with two people she likes. She’s learnt enough about birdwatching to know that an afternoon can go beautifully by without the bird you wanted making any attempt to participate. The waiting can be uncomfortable. Her mother has offered to show her an excellent close-up video from the comfort of their settee. She has seen the video. She wants to sit on a bench with a hole in the wall in front of her and see what’s on the other side today. You can spare her cold fingers with a decent pair of gloves. You can’t fast-forward the wait for a live bird without providing a different experience.

By the first mile Dan is out in front, setting a sensible, steady pace. His daughter can keep up easily enough. Sam stops at a gap in a hedge to get a zip undone. There’s that peculiar silence which occurs when a group of people who were loudly explaining why this is a tremendously good idea have started to breathe at the rate the idea requires.

In the 1970s the philosopher Harry Frankfurt made a distinction that’s handy to take out for a walk. You can want to do something, and you can have a view about which of your wants you wish would move you to act. You can want the cigarette and also want the desire to give it up to be the one that gets its way. Frankfurt calls a desire about which desire should actually govern your action a second-order volition. It’s an ugly piece of equipment to carry round in a rucksack. It does a respectable job when you unpack it. [1]

Dan wants breakfast. He also wants to be somebody who gets out of the warm café and does the long walk he said he’d do. If whichever impulse is freshest at any instant always gets the casting vote, the man’s unlikely to get out of sight of the bacon rolls. He can endorse a larger undertaking when he’s rested, remind himself of it when he’s grumpy and be pleased later that he went on. It would be a very thin version of freedom if it meant being permanently at the mercy of whichever part of yourself had last seen a bun.

But look at all the ways a person can acquire a higher opinion about what ought to move them. You can want to be the person who keeps going because you love the work. You can also want to be that person because your dad called you soft in front of a changing room full of boys when you were thirteen and part of you has been hoping to send him a retrospective invoice ever since. The fact that you can organise your first desire under the direction of a second does not establish that the second one is wise. The second can be a prick too. A man barking instructions from a higher floor in the same building sometimes just has better acoustics.

Ask a version of the question no motivational poster has room to print. If nobody got to find out how hard this was, would you still want to do it this way? There’s no single right answer. A public promise may have value precisely because somebody can count on it. A sponsored walk depends, up to a point, on people hearing that you did it. But if all that remains of the attraction when the audience goes away is a long cold afternoon you thoroughly dislike, at least you know which part of the enterprise you’re buying.

At three miles a woman passes them at an impressive clip, pauses at the next bench to take a photograph of a commemorative plaque, then goes on. Dan catches up with her at a gate. She does this walk every year. There’s a small dog buried at the far end of it. She carries the picture home to a friend who can’t manage the walk any more and collects the bench inscriptions. Nine times out of ten when you imagine somebody else is in an interesting competition with you it turns out they are running an entirely independent service for a woman who likes benches.

At five miles Dan’s daughter produces the bird book. She’s put a paper flag at the avocet. Another marks the shelduck. There’s a third at an illustrated key to the things you can identify by their backsides as they swim rapidly away. Sam hadn’t known how badly his education had let him down in the matter of duck arses. The fourth flag is where she has written the number of the return bus in case her feet get tired. Her mother put that one in.

The suffering you already paid for

There’s a branch in the path around mile eight. Follow the official markers up to the top of the ridge and the route continues along the coast. Take the short spur down and you come to the hide. A bus stop by the visitors’ centre makes this one of the places you can sign out. The marshal has a clipboard, the promised biscuits and a folding stool which is making a surprisingly convincing case for the shorter day.

They’ve taken two hours and fifty minutes to get here, with the gate and the map and a discussion about the minimum age at which it’s acceptable to photograph a dead crab. Dan had allowed two hours and forty. Ten miles remain. At three miles an hour that’s three hours and twenty minutes actually walking. If they want to catch the bus he picked to get them home for a booking later, they have four hours left. Forty minutes for everything else, including lunch, checking out at the finish, finding the stop and getting down to the hide and back. He’s thinking ten minutes with the birds ought to cover it.

“Does he know about the bus?” his daughter asks.

“Who?”

“The bird.”

She has an objection of some mathematical force. You can schedule a bus and willing adults with watches can schedule a walk. Inserting an appointment for an avocet into the remaining interval involves quietly awarding yourself command of an additional species.

Sam has put his bag down. “How long did you actually tell her we’d stop?”

“Well, I didn’t specify.”

Exactly. To Dan, stop meant stop walking for a bit. To his daughter it meant the part of the day they had been walking towards. If you ever find yourself chairing a meeting between these two interpretations, bring biscuits.

Dan looks back along the path. They’ve already come eight miles. He’s paid the entry fee, raised a decent amount of money and told several people at work about all eighteen. It would be a waste to stop, wouldn’t it? Here a remarkably slippery little word has turned up. Waste of what?

Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer asked people to imagine paying a hundred dollars for a ski weekend in Michigan and fifty for one in Wisconsin, discovering too late that both fell on the same weekend. Neither ticket could be sold or returned. In the scenario the purchaser expected to enjoy Wisconsin more. Even so, many respondents chose the more expensive Michigan trip. The price already paid had changed which weekend seemed sensible to take. The authors also studied real theatre subscribers given different prices for season tickets. In the initial months, those who’d paid more attended more performances. Theatre attendance can be a perfectly good thing. The particular point of interest was that what people did afterwards was affected by a price they could no longer alter. [2]

Whatever Dan does with the next ten miles, he’s walked the first eight. The original fee doesn’t improve the view from the ridge. His calf isn’t going to reimburse him if he collects the second stamp. But the promised donations per mile, if any, might make a real difference to what the pool receives. A promise freely made to meet somebody at the finish can give them an interest in what you do now. Past effort may also have made you stronger and better placed to do something you would enjoy. Saying previous costs are sunk doesn’t require you to take a blow to the head and forget you have a past. It requires some care about which bits of the past bear on an outcome you could still affect.

The sponsorship on Dan’s page is all flat donations except one. An old friend has offered a pound for every mile he completes. Ten miles would make another ten pounds. That’s real, and he should include it. It would be a bit rich to inform his daughter that a community pool’s entire accessible changing programme depends on her looking at a duck for less than ten minutes. She’d be quite entitled to consider the financial circumstances that led an organisation of this size to place its future in the hands of an eight-year-old with field glasses.

“I want to walk the whole thing,” he says. “I’ve been looking forward to it.”

All three of them look rather happier on hearing that sentence. He’s removed the pool, the birthday, the registration fee and a faint, unspecified moral defect in people who take buses from the job of explaining why Dad wants to carry on. He wants to carry on.

“I want to stay a while,” she says.

“I could stay too,” says Sam.

Dan looks at him. “You wanted to do eighteen.”

“I wanted to come with you. I’ve come eight miles with you. If you want me to go on, ask me properly.”

It hadn’t occurred to Dan until now that he might ask his brother for company and hear the answer as a favour. He had ticked all three names on the entry form. Two of the people concerned had even been in the room at the time. Excellent. At what point did the tick acquire custody of the three people?

Who gets told they chose it

Before we let a family squabble about a pleasant walk turn into a general theory of the good life, consider the person working a night shift and a second job because the rent went up. Tell her that she chose this suffering and you’re technically able to point to two contracts with her signature on them. You may also discover that there is a fairly short distance between a technical truth and the door you are being shown out of.

Choice is sensitive to the actual alternatives and the cost of refusing each of them. If refusing a shift means getting your hours cut next month, that’s one kind of choice. If it means spending Saturday morning in bed with a novel, that’s another. A person can be resourceful and even proud of how they meet a constraint they did nothing to choose. Ask them which bits they would change if they could. It’s usually a much more interesting conversation than the one in which you explain how grateful they must be for the opportunity to grow.

And spare a thought for the other people in the proud account of difficulty. Somebody runs a business for twelve years without taking a day off. What admirable dedication. Who covered the parents’ hospital visits? Who could never be ill on the days the shop opened early? Who made it possible for that particular family member to have only one form of suffering on the books? The answer may be that the couple discussed it, both valued the enterprise and one of them positively enjoys an opportunity to eat dinner in peace. Then we’re dealing with a shared undertaking. It may also be that the person who gave motivational talks about choosing your hard had a spouse at home making a packed lunch for his hard.

There’s a nice check available. Do the other people involved get to change their minds? Commitment would be an empty idea if any passing disappointment automatically annulled it. You don’t accept the care of somebody’s three-year-old and decide after ten minutes to pursue the personally more fulfilling experience of watching a film across town. Dependants make real claims on you. People who rely on a promise are entitled to the chance to make other arrangements. An athlete consenting to spend one morning running in the cold hasn’t consented to every possible injury, nor handed a coach permanent custody of what their body is for. A sensible agreement can specify what happens if continuing starts to cost more than anybody thought it would.

Dan hadn’t done this in words. His wife had put a bus number in a book. Between them they apparently constitute a functioning though somewhat unevenly distributed executive intelligence.

“You sure you wouldn’t mind staying with her?” he asks Sam.

“I’d like to see this duck.”

“It’s a wader,” says his niece. “You could call the shelduck a duck.”

“I’m glad to hear we’re allowed one.”

Dan can walk the rest at his own pace. Sam and the girl can check out here and take the bus when they’ve had enough. There’s a friendly exchange confirming whose phone is charged, when their mother expects a message and which adult is responsible for delivering a particular child to a particular house. It takes rather longer than the calculation about walking pace and is of considerably more practical use in dealing with an adult who is to be parted from his daughter halfway along a coast. Dan signs the short-route sheet as a witness for her and adjusts a strap on her binoculars. She rearranges it as soon as he’s gone.

Sam wonders if he has been too keen to solve things. Offering to stay may have made it easier for his brother to avoid deciding what that promise to stop actually meant. He also wonders whether there’s a café at the visitors’ centre. An adult is capable of harbouring both a sophisticated concern about the moral development of a sibling and an intense private interest in chips.

Inside the hide there’s a bench, a long narrow opening and a whiteboard recording things people have seen. An avocet was here yesterday morning. The pencil has been worn down to the size and colour of something the last bird left as a contribution. Dan’s daughter sits with the glass nearly out of the opening. Sam takes the piece of bench next to her. Their knees clear the underside by markedly different amounts.

For ten minutes nothing happens. Then something happens which she identifies as a small plastic bag. Then the plastic bag moves under its own power and is promoted to the still fairly broad category of something alive. Sam has begun to understand the regard in which the birdwatching community holds a person who can identify an arse.

The story your feet tell later

Up on the ridge Dan is enjoying approximately half of his walk. This is a very respectable return for an experience of this type. The wind has gone round to the side. Every so often a break in the cloud drops a piece of sunlight onto a patch of water and he sees one part of the distant grey surface suddenly shine as though there’s a lamp underneath. He finds a speed he can keep. For several minutes his awareness of being a man with things he needs to achieve contracts to one rather enjoyable pair of legs.

Then there’s a long, fenced diversion around a soft bit of cliff path. No view. No interest. Nothing apparently being tested except how a man in his fiftieth year feels about wire mesh in sustained quantities. When he tells the story later he will skip this bit. He is still obliged to walk it.

There’s some good reason to distrust the conversion rate between a difficult experience as it happens and the story about it you give later. In a study by Daniel Kahneman and colleagues, people had two uncomfortable cold-water experiences. The longer episode included the same first stretch as the shorter one, followed by a somewhat less uncomfortable ending. When asked which trial they would rather repeat, many chose the longer one. The extra time hadn’t undone the earlier discomfort. It had changed how the episode ended, and that affected some participants’ retrospective choice. These were brief controlled episodes. Nobody measured whether having a reasonably cheerful pudding at the end justifies spending twenty years in a miserable marriage. [3]

It matters to Dan because he’s already pictured the photograph at the finish. Windburn, number at a jaunty angle, his youngest next to him holding a tin medal with the annoyed civility of children required to participate in family history. He would probably value that picture for years. For his daughter to appear in it with him, she would have had to walk those ten miles. The fact that both of them might enjoy telling the story eighteen months later doesn’t make the hours in between fictional. Neither do sore feet on their own prove the experience ought to have been avoided. A story you’ll cherish is one good thing among several you can want. On a cold day the body keeping the appointment is one of the people with a vote.

There are hardships it makes sense to choose because their value only emerges slowly. Learning to read music can involve a considerable period of being insulted by some dots. A child practising a piece, an adult returning to study and a journalist coming back to a difficult question all have days when there’s no pleasure in it. If they judge the undertaking by the worst five minutes, nearly anything that requires practice will look like a swindle. If they judge by a successful person’s recollection at an awards ceremony, they may end up believing every unpaid internship and humiliating remark from the boss has been personally sanctioned by destiny. The people who left halfway through have generally not been invited to give the acceptance speech.

What evidence could change your mind while there’s still some road left? With the journalist, perhaps six months of work have started turning up the same expertly documented answer. More suffering won’t turn it into the answer the editor wanted. With the student, perhaps after trying a different teacher they begin to enjoy an idea they thought was closed to them. Something has altered. With the child, perhaps the reason they hate the practice session is that their feet don’t reach the floor and the stool makes their back hurt. Good luck teaching character by denying them a box.

You can revise how you do something while remaining faithful to what you wanted it for. You can also discover, with some embarrassment, that what you wanted was to be admired for wanting it. Some exceptionally serviceable lives have begun after an ambitious young person discovered that they didn’t wish to be a surgeon nearly as much as they wished to be introduced as one at dinner. If that person resigns before somebody goes under anaesthetic, a number of quite separate values have been served.

By mile sixteen Dan is getting overtaken by people he passed in the morning. It doesn’t bother him as much as he thought it would. His knees have opinions. His right foot is proposing a more definite review of the sock policy. He still wants to walk the last two. There are days when it’s quite moving to discover that what you want after sixteen miles of evidence is still the thing you thought you wanted at the beginning. There are other days when your main responsibility to the enterprise is to know where the next sensible exit is.

He comes into the second car park about twenty minutes before his bus. The marshal writes down his number, gives him a tin medal and offers him a biscuit. He eats it standing up. There’s a place set up with a bright paper frame where people can take the finish photograph. He asks the woman behind him if she’ll do the honours. She takes two, one facing the correct way round, which seems a fair rate of return. He gets his extra ten pounds for the pool. His daughter hasn’t been required to get them for him. He feels that in the particular circumstances he’s entitled to enjoy the biscuit.

She and Sam are already home when he rings. They stayed in the hide for forty-seven minutes. They saw a shelduck and a thin brown bird whose identity is under appeal. No avocet. Did she mind? Of course she bloody minded. She’d gone to some trouble to see an avocet. Sensible adults have a habit of wanting to alchemise every disappointment a child receives into some much more nourishing alternative outcome. Sometimes you go to see a bird, the bird is off somewhere about its business and the day contains one small legitimate sadness about a bird.

Sam says she also wanted to wait after he’d suggested the bus a second time. She was cold and he offered her his gloves. She accepted the gloves. She declined the bus. It was her forty-seventh minute to spend. Dad, looking at the photograph the woman in the queue has taken, finds that’s a fact about the walk he’s rather pleased to have earned the right to know.

“Will you go back with me?” she asks him.

“To the hide?”

“Yes.”

“What if it’s not there again?”

“Dad. I know how birds work.”

His first idea is to arrange the trip around the tide, the bus and a later walk which would take in a promising bit of coast he missed on the diversion. There’s nothing wrong with his first idea that couldn’t be improved by letting somebody else have the second. He asks how long she wants to go for.

“I don’t know. Have you got a morning?”

He has a look. On the following Saturday he has a whole morning. There’s a shop where you can get food if you need it and, so far as he knows, no system in the United Kingdom for awarding medals to people who sit on a bench eating crisps. He writes the date down. For once he leaves the space at the far end of the appointment empty.
