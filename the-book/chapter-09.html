Chapter 09The Comparison Olympics

The carrot is the detail that gets Sam. In tonight’s photograph from his brother, a loaf of bread cools on the counter of a household in which a twelve-year-old can be trusted with a grater. Dan is forty-nine. He runs the service department at a car dealership and owns a house with a yard across town, where he lives with his wife, Carla, and their two daughters. Sam is forty-six and rents. Half an hour ago he and Jo nearly ended a serviceable relationship over the unloading of a dishwasher. He enlarges the photograph. His niece Nora has her tongue between her teeth. Somebody has taught her to keep the ends of her fingers out of the salad.

Sam types Looks great. It does look great. He loves those kids. He likes the little furrow in his brother’s brow when he tries to do something delicate with his hands. All of that is true, and still the next words out of Sam’s mouth are “How the hell does he have time to make bread?”

Jo looks over his shoulder. She’s spent her day holding other people’s dogs still. “He gets up early.”

“That wasn’t really my question.”

His real question is on the screen. He’s left the bread and the child and moved to the window over the sink, down in the corner where the frame meets the sill, and he has it at four hundred percent.

“What are you doing?”

“I’m happy for my brother,” Sam says. “I only zoomed in to check for mold.”

He won’t say what he’s hoping for, so I will. He wants his brother’s kitchen to have mold. Nothing the girls would breathe. A gray bloom in the caulk would do, a patch the size of a quarter, enough to show that the house with the yard is rotting at the usual rate. Four hundred percent is four times as wide and sixteen times the area, so each dot Dan’s phone recorded now takes up sixteen times as much of Sam’s screen. Enlarging can’t add a speck the camera didn’t catch. It can turn a shadow into almost anything you need. I’d enjoy reporting this more if I hadn’t already thought of the ceiling above the stove, which he’s missed.

Sam is holding a picture of warm bread and a niece with all her fingers. He would cross town at three in the morning if either girl needed him. What he finds harder is wishing their father well at eleven-thirty on a quiet night when nothing is wrong. The kitchen he’s standing in is exactly as clean as it was thirty seconds ago. It has acquired a place in the standings.

Look at how little the photograph tells him. Somebody baked a loaf. Four carrots are involved. It can’t say who usually clears the table, how often the girls help, or what Dan and Carla last said to each other in the car. He needs none of that. His brother has built a good life, and Sam has somehow been around the back of the building looking for parking.

You might be waiting for the camera to pull back and reveal that Dan is secretly miserable. It would make Sam feel better for about a minute. It would also be a hell of a condition to put on a man you love. Must there be an eviction notice under the breadboard before you can enjoy somebody’s bread?

Let the picture be true. Let the caulk be spotless. Let the girls have had a good afternoon, let both parents be getting enough sleep, let the bastard own a sharp knife and know where he put it. Now we have a more interesting problem. What exactly has Dan’s good afternoon taken away from Sam’s?

A family event with no published rules

Of the two boys in their childhood kitchen, Dan was the first to reach the top shelf. For months this meant one person could reach the good cookies and be persuaded to keep his mouth shut. A difference in height made an ordinary Tuesday better for everybody.

Then somebody marked their heights on the doorframe. The marks had dates, and Sam could put his hand against the place his brother had been at his age. Same family, same pencil, same strip of wood. He remembers standing as straight as he could, trying to earn half an inch by force of character. At least the doorframe was measuring a thing that had inches.

That mark has survived the sale of the house. A stranger on television runs a restaurant, speaks three languages and takes his children skiing, and Sam finishes his dinner in good spirits. His brother buys a house across town and Sam spends a month mentally defending his lease. They shared a bedroom. Their father taught them to drive in the same car and shouted at both of them at the same intersection. Surely the difference between where they ended up must say something exact about the two boys.

It says something. Dan learned to run a service department, which is work people pay well for, and bought where a yard was within reach. Sam writes the instructions that come with furniture. He stayed in a second-floor rental because Jo had found work she loved, and for a few years the rent was reasonable because a friend knew the landlord. Some of those differences are their choices, some are the choices of people who never met them, and a few could change with one phone call. Sharing a father doesn’t make two brothers a controlled experiment.

Put the two accounts on a table anyway. Dan has the higher pay. Sam gets home in time to eat with Jo. To produce a single score you’d have to put a price on dinner, and Sam seems prepared to let whichever stranger wrote the mortgage calculator set it.

A salary figure has an honest use. A landlord will even accept payment in the units. Try offering him three excellent friendships and a resting heart rate of fifty-four. The figure turns dishonest around the point where Sam expects it to tell him which of two men their father should have been prouder of. That’s the number he’s really after. Where on a pay stub would they print it?

The view from second place

An Olympic final at least says what it’s measuring. Somebody explains the rules, maintains the equipment and makes sure everybody covers the same distance. People still argue about the result. Imagine the savings if they simply showed a committee eight photographs of people’s kitchens.

Even there, knowing where somebody placed tells you little about how the place felt. In a study published in 1995, Victoria Medvec, Scott Madey and Thomas Gilovich examined footage from the Barcelona Summer Olympics. Observers rated the reactions of silver and bronze medalists. In the footage they rated, the bronze medalists, on average, appeared happier. Second place came with a better medal and, to the people watching, a slightly worse afternoon.[1]

Think of the arithmetic the camera misses. For a silver medalist, a few hundredths of a second one way might have meant gold. For a bronze medalist, a few hundredths the other way might have meant shaking hands and going home with nothing. The authors argued that the alternative each athlete could most easily imagine helped explain the faces. A. Peter McGraw, Barbara Mellers and Philip Tetlock later offered a plainer reading: silver medalists may simply have expected more going in.[2] A stranger with a rating sheet can score a face. Nobody installed a little window in the back of the athletes’ heads.[1]

Either reading fits the man with the phone. Measured against most of the planet, Sam is on the podium. He’s employed, housed and loved by a woman who’s still speaking to him after the dishwasher. The one alternative he can picture is three years older and got to the top shelf first. Silver doesn’t look down at fourth place. Silver looks at gold.

By breakfast he’s built a stronger opponent than his brother. Dan supplies the loaf and the house. A friend from high school supplies a half-marathon time. A former colleague supplies a title with director in it. A cousin supplies children who write thank-you notes. Put them in one body. At a starting line somewhere a man is crouched over a loose shoelace with a loaf under one arm, a set of house keys digging into his thigh and a child on his back composing a thank-you note. The official with the flag is waiting for him to tie the shoe. Sam has worse news for the official. After the race this man is expected to host Thanksgiving for fourteen.

Do the sum on the assembled man. Say, purely for the arithmetic, that one person in ten clears each of four bars and that clearing one tells you nothing about the others. Out of ten thousand people, a thousand clear the first, a hundred the second, ten the third, and one the fourth. He’s the one with the loaf under his arm. Ask who baked. Ask who ran. You seldom get the same address.

Then there’s the mold, the cheap consolation Sam still has his thumb on. Be careful about giving somebody the job of being worse off than you. What happens to Sam’s peace of mind the day Dan discovers bleach? He’d be a man who loves his brother and, on some small secret level, needs the bleach to fail.

The arrangement also makes Dan hard to see. As long as the photograph is a score, the things Sam likes about his brother are unavailable to him: the furrow, the big careful hands, the fact that a man who spends ten hours a day telling people what’s wrong with their transmissions comes home and makes bread. Sam looked at all of that for perhaps four seconds before he went to the window frame.

Sometimes there really is only one chair

It would be pleasant to conclude that somebody else’s success never costs you a thing. Charles Darwin had a week that says otherwise, and he left it in writing.

By his own account it began on June 18, 1858, when the mail brought a package from Alfred Russel Wallace, a younger naturalist collecting specimens in the islands that are now eastern Indonesia. Inside was a short essay on how species change. Wallace asked Darwin to pass it along to the geologist Charles Lyell.[3]

The theory in the essay was Darwin’s own. He had written out a sketch of it in 1842, sixteen years earlier, published none of it and gone on collecting facts. Lyell had warned him that somebody else would get there. Darwin forwarded the essay the same day, as asked, with a letter. “Your words have come true with a vengeance,” he told Lyell. If Wallace had been handed the 1842 sketch, “he could not have made a better short abstract!” Then the line a person only writes to a friend: “So all my originality, whatever it may amount to, will be smashed.”[3]

Science keeps one chair for whoever gets there first, and a man on the far side of the world had just mailed Darwin his claim to it. A week later Darwin wrote to Lyell again. He wanted to publish a short account of his own now, he couldn’t see how to do it decently, and he would “far rather burn my whole book” than have Wallace or anybody think he had “behaved in a paltry spirit.”[4] That’s a man saying the ugly motive out loud. He wanted the chair. He’d wanted it for sixteen years, and he was ashamed of how much. Scarlet fever was in the house that week. On June 28 his baby son Charles died of it.

Lyell and the botanist Joseph Hooker settled the matter without either author in the room. On July 1, extracts from Darwin’s unpublished writing and Wallace’s essay were read out together at a meeting of the Linnean Society in London. Darwin was at home, three days after the death. Wallace was still in the islands, months away by mail, and learned what had been done with his essay from a letter. That October he wrote to Hooker that he considered himself “a favoured party.”[5], [6] Second billing, arranged in his absence by the other man’s friends, and he said thank you. Either Wallace was a better man than most of us or he knew how much say a specimen collector had in the matter. I can’t tell you which.

And the room where it was announced? The following May the society’s president, Thomas Bell, summed up the year and reported that it had not been marked by “any of those striking discoveries” that remake a science.[5]

The story fits Sam up to a point, and the point is the chair. Darwin and Wallace wanted one thing that could be awarded once, under a rule everybody knew. It would have been obnoxious to put an arm around Darwin that week and explain that another man’s success took nothing from him. He was entitled to feel like hell, and did. What he didn’t do was decide that Wallace had wronged him. Dan’s loaf took no bread off Sam’s counter. Sam is mourning a prize that was never offered.

A comparison can also be the only way to find out you’re being shortchanged. Two years ago, at the animal hospital, Jo found a pay stub somebody had left on the break-room printer. A technician hired after her, working the same machines and the same holidays, was making $2.40 an hour more. At forty hours a week that’s $96, or $4,992 a year. She did the sum before her coffee was cool. Until that morning she had believed the practice manager when he said the pay band had no room in it. Imagine telling her to protect her peace by keeping her eyes on her own journey. That advice protects somebody’s peace, and she knew which office he sat in.

In a field experiment at the University of California, David Card and his colleagues randomly told some employees about a website where they could look up what their coworkers were paid. Then they surveyed people. Among those paid below the median for their unit and occupation, the ones who’d been told about the site reported lower job satisfaction than the ones who hadn’t. People above the median showed no matching lift. Nobody’s salary had been cut. All that had changed was what people knew.[7]

A median can’t settle whether two people are doing the same work on different terms. Seniority, hours, favoritism and the size of the budget the week somebody signed are all worth a look. Sending the lower-paid person to a seminar on gratitude is the cheap alternative, and employers have noticed. Jo asked her colleague what his job description said, printed the hospital’s posted ranges and took both to a meeting. Room was found in the pay band. It turned out to be $1.75 wide. She’s still sixty-five cents short and knows whose number that is. The manager sets what the hospital pays for her time. She has never let him set what she’s worth, and he already gets plenty for his money.

This is why I’m suspicious of any philosophy that promises to make you immune to comparison. Immunity would have left Jo $1.75 an hour poorer. A letter from the islands tells you who else got there. A pay stub gives you grounds to ask how the rates were set. A photograph tells you your brother had a decent afternoon. It would be extraordinary if all three answered the question Am I enough?

What would you like to keep?

Sam has another look at the picture over breakfast. Jo takes the phone from his hand and enlarges the loaf until it dissolves into beige squares. “Want me to get you a bread book?”

It’s a good offer, and the research is on her side as far as it goes. Psychologists Penelope Lockwood and Ziva Kunda studied how people responded to outstanding performers in fields that mattered to them. The effect of a star depended in part on whether the observer could imagine getting there. The same example lifted one person and left another feeling smaller.[8] A finished loaf in a photograph teaches about as much as learning to swim from a picture of a dolphin. A book, a bag of flour and a brother willing to explain crust would be another matter.

Sam considers the practicalities. He has little interest in tending a bubbling substance in a jar. One already lives at the back of his fridge. He could take up running. He’s tried running and knows a great deal about how he feels about running. As for a house in Dan’s neighborhood, he’d spend a large part of each day trying to get back to his own. He would like, someday, not to be asked to leave because a landlord wants to sell. That want is real and has nothing to do with Dan. It goes on a different list.

What he wants from the photograph turns out to be oddly specific. He wants a morning when somebody knows he’s coming and sets out an extra cup. He wants Nora to carry on telling him a story from last week without spending the first ten minutes working out what he knows about her life. He can’t recall the last time he went to his brother’s house without an occasion, a bottle and a departure time agreed in the car. Twenty minutes apart, two brothers have slowly become guests.

Visits used to have no ceremony. One of them would be in the bathroom and the other would shout something through the door and help himself to a cookie. Now each would describe the other as welcome any time, and they’ve honored that so carefully they hardly see each other.

Asking carries a risk. Dan could want those mornings to himself. Sam has spent plenty of his own wanting an empty apartment, no pants and nobody watching. Another month of studying the caulk would remove the risk and most of the point.

He sends Dan a message asking if they need another pair of hands next Sunday. Three minutes later his brother sends back a shopping list. It contains milk, coffee and a demand for one hundred and twelve chocolate chips. June, who is seven, has evidently obtained access to the negotiations. Sam is beginning to remember why the adults kept the cookies on the top shelf.

Not every longing resolves into a shopping list. Somebody who wanted children and ran out of options can read the photograph exactly right and still have to put the phone face down.

Sam is luckier. He doesn’t want Dan’s life. He wants one morning of it, and it’s for sale. The price is sleeping late on Sunday. He reserves the right to reconsider when the alarm goes off.

A place at the table

Sunday brings rain, children and a bag of chocolate chips that loses several of its members at a red light under circumstances Sam declines to discuss. Dan opens the door with flour on one forearm. June counts the survivors. Ninety-seven. She looks at his mouth. The house smells wonderful. It’s irritating how good the bread is. Briefly. Well, through the second slice.

On his way to hang up his coat Sam passes the window over the sink. He looks. He isn’t proud of it. The caulk is as white as a tooth.

Nora is grating carrots for lunch. June has been put in charge of butter, a position of almost unlimited destructive scope. Carla, who teaches middle-school science, is at the end of the table grading lab reports with a red pen and visible enjoyment. Dan makes coffee. For a while Sam has nothing to do except tear a corner off the loaf while it’s still warm enough to hurt his fingers. He asks how Dan gets the crust so crisp and gets an answer several minutes longer than he was prepared for. It involves a lidded pot heated empty for half an hour, which traps the steam coming off the loaf. The steam keeps the outside soft until the bread has finished rising, then leaves the crust thinner, shinier and crisper.[9] Dan uses the word gelatinize twice. He’s missed this too, a man he’s known all his life becoming a bore in an entirely new direction.

Later Nora looks up from her phone. “Do the duck with the broken leg. June’s never seen it.” He used to do it when Nora was little. He hadn’t realized she remembered. It’s an appalling piece of physical comedy. The feet go first, then the left wing slowly loses confidence in the whole enterprise. His knees are a good deal older than the last time he did it. The duck has never had so much to work with.

For once he’s in the photograph. The left knee of his jeans has worked its way sideways and he’s got one arm folded up like a cheap umbrella. June is on the floor in front of him, helpless. Dan offers to delete it, which is a kind thing to offer a grown man. Sam asks for a copy.

He’d still like to win a few contests. His brother has still done things he wishes he’d done too. Over lunch Dan mentions a trip they’re planning, and Sam feels the familiar little catch before he asks where they’re going. A person can learn a fair amount and still be the same petty mammal by lunchtime. This time he sticks around long enough to hear the answer.

Fran from next door comes over for a folding chair and stays for coffee. By now Sam has grated a carrot, contributed to a minor butter emergency, listened to his brother and given an affecting performance as an injured waterfowl. Fran sits down and asks the question adults ask each other on meeting.

“So, Sam. What do you do?”

Of all the things he’s just spent the morning doing, he gives her the one that comes with a pay stub.
