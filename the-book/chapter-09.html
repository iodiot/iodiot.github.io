Chapter 09The Comparison Olympics

The carrot is the detail that gets Sam. His brother has two children, a loaf of bread cooling on the counter, and apparently the kind of household in which a small person can be trusted with a grater. Half an hour ago Sam and Jo nearly ended a perfectly serviceable relationship over the unloading of a dishwasher. He enlarges the photograph. His niece has her tongue between her teeth. Somebody has taught her to keep the ends of her fingers out of the salad.

Sam types Looks great. It does look great. He loves those kids. He likes the little furrow in his brother’s brow when he tries to do something delicate with his hands. All of that is true, and still the next words out of Sam’s mouth are “How the hell does he have time to make bread?”

Jo looks over his shoulder. “He gets up early.”

“That wasn’t really my question.”

What Sam meant was something closer to How did we turn out like this? A moment ago he had a clean kitchen and a truce. One photograph later he has the same kitchen, the same truce, and an unfavorable assessment from the international governing body for adulthood. There is some bad news about this body. It meets in his head. It has never once failed to reach a quorum.

Look at the photograph with him. You can establish that somebody baked a loaf. You can establish the approximate number of carrots. You can’t establish who usually clears the table, how often the kids help, or what the two adults in that kitchen last said to each other in the car. The remarkable thing is how little of that information Sam requires before issuing the verdict. His brother has built a good life. Sam has somehow been round the back of the building looking for parking.

You might be waiting for the camera to pull back and reveal that his brother is secretly miserable. It would make Sam feel better for about a minute. It would also be a hell of a condition to put on a man you love. Must there be an eviction notice under the breadboard before you can enjoy somebody’s bread?

Let the picture be true. Let the children have had a good afternoon, let both parents be getting enough sleep, let the bastard own a sharp knife and know where he put it. Now we have a much more interesting problem. What exactly has his good afternoon taken away from yours?

A family event with no published rules

Of the two boys in Sam’s childhood kitchen, his brother was the first to reach the top shelf. For months this meant there was precisely one person who could reach the good biscuits and be persuaded to keep his mouth shut. They both understood the value of the arrangement. A difference in height made an ordinary Tuesday better for everybody.

Then somebody marked their heights on the doorframe. There were dates, and Sam could put his hand against the place his brother had been at his age. Same family, same pencil, same strip of wood. He remembers standing as straight as he could, trying to earn half an inch by force of character. At least the doorframe was measuring a thing that actually had inches.

It’s easy to see how that mark survives the sale of the house. A stranger on television runs a restaurant, speaks three languages and takes his children skiing. Sam manages to finish his dinner in tolerably good spirits. His brother buys a house ten minutes away and Sam spends a month mentally defending his lease. Familiarity gives the resemblance a kind of legal standing. They shared a bedroom. Their father taught them to drive in the same car and shouted at both of them at roughly the same junction. Surely the difference between where they ended up must say something exact about the two boys.

It says something. The younger one stayed in town when Jo found work she loved. The older one moved to a place where a house with a garden was within reach. One has put years into the sort of work people will pay a good deal for. One got a stretch of reasonably priced rent because a friend knew a landlord. Some of those differences reflect their choices, some reflect the choices of people who never met them, and a few could change with one phone call. Sharing a father doesn’t make a pair of brothers a controlled experiment.

There’s a quieter error underneath the problem of incomplete information. Suppose we could obtain both brothers’ bank statements, medical records, work histories and an itemized account of every Saturday since puberty. We could answer many more questions. We’d still have to decide what counts as doing well. On that question the bank statement has no expertise whatsoever. It will, however, come printed to two decimal places, which is often enough to get it appointed chair.

Measures slip into positions of authority this way. A salary can tell you what your employer pays for your time. It can also help a bank decide how much to lend you. Give it five minutes unsupervised at a family dinner and it’s telling your mother which child to worry about. Nothing mysterious has happened. A number was available at the precise moment somebody wanted a judgment. Few of us would stand up at a funeral and announce that the deceased was in the top quartile for his postcode. Quite a few of us are prepared to spend the working week trying to qualify for that eulogy.

The view from second place

There are places where people take the trouble to say exactly what they’re measuring. An Olympic final is one. Somebody explains the rules, maintains the equipment and sees to it that the competitors cover the same distance. People still argue about the result. Imagine the efficiency savings if they simply showed a committee eight photographs of people’s kitchens.

Even under all those carefully specified conditions, knowing where somebody placed tells you surprisingly little about how that place felt. In a study published in 1995, Victoria Medvec, Scott Madey and Thomas Gilovich examined footage from the Barcelona Summer Olympics. Observers rated the reactions of silver and bronze medalists. In the footage they rated, the bronze medalists, on average, appeared happier. Second place came with a better medal and, to the people watching, a slightly worse afternoon. [1]

The researchers offered an explanation involving the alternatives that come readily to mind. From second place you can look up one step and imagine winning. From third you can look down one and imagine going home with nothing around your neck except your accreditation. The person silver lost to and the person bronze beat can be equally vivid company on the podium. An expression on camera gives us limited access to an athlete’s thoughts, and no result like this tells you how any particular medalist felt. It does give us a reason to examine the extra person we have mentally invited to stand beside the winner.

You can meet that extra person without qualifying for anything. Imagine you’ve written a short story. Someone you admire reads it and later, over dinner, accurately describes a moment you spent three nights trying to get right. You know what a rare pleasure that is. A sentence traveled all the way from your desk into somebody else’s head and arrived with the furniture intact.

On the bus home you learn that an old classmate has sold a collection of stories. The dinner recedes with startling speed. For the rest of the ride you’re keeping company with the version of you who would have a book by now if you hadn’t spent all that time doing whatever it is you now propose to call your twenties. Your classmate has achieved one real thing. You have supplied the imaginary person who converts it into a defeat.

There are useful questions to ask of this pang. Do you want to finish a collection? Have you been protecting the stories on your laptop from the possibility that somebody will turn them down? Those could be hard questions with good answers. But on the bus something cruder usually happens first. You treat the pang itself as evidence that the dinner was worth less than you thought. Try to locate the moment the reader stopped understanding your story. It never happened. Your attention went somewhere else.

The fastest cellist in the property market

By breakfast Sam has assembled a much stronger opponent than his brother. Dan supplies the loaf. A friend from school supplies the time from a half marathon. A former colleague supplies the house keys. A woman he has never seen in person contributes a performance on the cello so beautiful that Sam watches it three times, then experiences a pang of regret about his musical career. His musical career consisted of nine months on the recorder and an incident involving chewing gum. It is generous of him to call this regret.

Put all those people in the same room and the absurdity becomes visible. At a starting line somewhere there is a runner crouched over a loose shoelace, a cello case on his back, a loaf under his arm and a set of house keys digging into his thigh. The official with the flag is patiently waiting for him to get his shit together. Sam has somewhat worse news for the official. After the race this man is expected to be a patient father and to host Christmas for fourteen.

Each of the people whose achievements he borrowed had an ordinary Tuesday on which to pursue one of them. Sam has given his composite rival four Tuesdays a week and will shortly be meeting with himself to discuss his disappointing use of time. You can do this with a phone. An enterprising grandmother could do it with three cousins and a church newsletter. The equipment has improved. The arithmetic was lousy to begin with.

Sometimes the arithmetic runs the other way. You mention that you’re exhausted and a well-meaning relative can instantly name six people who have it worse. Naturally one of them has a sick horse. The relative expects six instances of greater distress, plus the horse, to cancel out your need for sleep. Here again a comparison has wandered well outside its area of competence. Knowing what another person carries may help you notice an obligation you’d overlooked. It does surprisingly little to get you through a late shift on four hours’ sleep.

There is an uglier consolation within easy reach. You could look up the classmate who never sold a story, or the cousin whose kitchen genuinely is a biohazard, and climb into bed feeling a little better placed. I understand the attraction. I also think you should be very careful about giving somebody else the job of being worse off than you. What happens to your peace of mind the day they sell a book or discover bleach? Imagine being the person who loves them and, on some small secret level, needs the bleach to fail.

That arrangement makes other people hard to see. You begin to miss precisely what you once liked about them. The cellist, for example. For perhaps twelve seconds you heard a human being draw a sound out of wood and wire that made you want to sit down. Then her left hand became evidence for the prosecution and the music was over, although there were still three minutes left to play. A fair amount of the life comparison steals from us is stolen on that scale. Three minutes here. Half a bus ride there. An afternoon with a brother you’ve quietly made responsible for explaining your shortcomings.

When envy has something to teach you

A beginning cellist can watch exactly the same performance and get something entirely different out of it. Suppose she’s been struggling with a shift in a piece. Every time she moves her hand up the fingerboard she overshoots the note. In the recording she notices how early the other player releases her thumb. She takes the instrument out, slows the passage down and tries again. For the first time all week the sound that emerges from her living room has no obvious application in pest control.

That pleasure depends on a difference in ability. It also depends on her willingness to look at somebody who can do something she can’t. The broad instruction to stop comparing yourself to others would make her a considerably worse cellist. It would also take some of the fun out of being a beginner, when the world is full of people who know a trick worth asking about.

The trouble comes when the question slips from How does she do that? to What kind of person would still be struggling with this at my age? Notice what happens to the thumb. Under the first question you can see it move. Under the second it disappears. The question has become much more painful and much harder to answer, so we assume it must also have become more profound. Often it has just become a worse question.

Psychologists Penelope Lockwood and Ziva Kunda studied how people responded to outstanding performers in fields that mattered to them. In their studies, the impact of a role model depended in part on whether the observer could imagine attaining a similar success. An example that inspired one person could leave another feeling diminished. [2] That finding fits a familiar part of learning. There are days when being told to look at the very best person in the world feels about as useful as being taught to swim by being handed a photograph of a dolphin.

A good teacher can change the size of the comparison. She plays the passage, puts a pencil mark where your hand needs to land, and asks you to listen to the two notes on either side. Now a difference that was big enough to blot out the sun is small enough to hear. There’s nothing particularly uplifting about the fact that you missed the note. There is something immensely encouraging about being able to tell which note you missed.

The same consideration applies outside the practice room. You might genuinely want your friend’s knack for getting people round a table, rather than the kitchen she gets them round. Ask her how she does it. She may tell you to invite people a month in advance and buy the soup. You had pictured acquiring sufficiently beautiful bowls that people would come to you of their own accord, like woodland creatures. Her answer saves you two years and a small fortune in ceramics.

Envy is often embarrassing because it delivers a piece of private correspondence in a public place. There you are at somebody’s housewarming, drinking adequately cold wine, when a thought arrives about the home you once expected to have by now. You have to keep holding the glass and listening while opening this envelope with your teeth. The host hasn’t done anything to you. What the thought brings to your attention may nevertheless be real. You are tired of being asked to leave each time a landlord wants to sell. You would like to paint a bedroom and expect to be there when it needs painting again. Wanting that degree of security deserves a hearing. It needn’t go on trial as a minor defect of character merely because you discovered it in a friend’s hallway.

Sometimes there really is only one chair

Now suppose two musicians audition for the same paid seat in an orchestra. They’ve practiced together for months. When one gets the call, the other loses a chance to pay her rent by doing something she loves. It would be breathtakingly obnoxious to put an arm around her and explain that someone else’s success can take nothing away from her. There was a chair. They have put someone in it. She’s allowed to sit on the curb for a minute and feel like hell.

The friendship may oblige her to congratulate her friend. It doesn’t oblige her to manufacture delight at the exact moment the call comes through. She can put one honest sentence in a message now and go for a walk before attempting a second. Later, when she can listen to an answer, it may be worth asking what the panel heard in her audition. In the meantime there is a large difference between losing a job and concluding that the person who got it has been certified as a better occupant of the earth. The orchestra had one question to settle. It has no jurisdiction over the second.

A similar distinction matters when there are no auditions and the rules are hard to find. Think of two technicians who work the same equipment, train new hires and take the same number of weekends on call. One learns by accident that the other earns considerably more. Until yesterday she believed her manager’s assurance that there was no room in the pay band. Today she has a question to take back to that office. Imagine telling her to protect her peace by keeping her eyes on her own journey. There is somebody whose peace this advice would protect, and you can probably guess whose office he sits in.

In a field experiment at the University of California, David Card and his colleagues randomly informed some employees about a website where they could look up pay information. The researchers subsequently surveyed employees. Among people paid below the median for their occupation and pay unit, those given information about the site reported lower satisfaction than their counterparts in the comparison group. Above-median workers showed no corresponding increase in satisfaction. Nobody’s salary had been cut as part of the experiment. What had changed was access to information about other people’s pay. [3]

It’s worth resisting two easy morals here. The experiment didn’t establish that the lower-paid employees ought to cultivate gratitude, any more than it established that every difference they discovered was unjust. Seniority, duties and hours can matter. So can favoritism, secrecy and the sheer dumb luck of what the budget looked like the week you signed. If two people may be doing equivalent work for different pay, it takes an actual comparison to find out. A loss of satisfaction can be the first sensible reaction to learning something you needed to know.

Our technician might ask her colleague what the job description says and when the rate last went up. She could bring her own duties and the published ranges to a meeting. If several people have been given incompatible explanations, they might decide to go together. The inquiry might uncover a significant difference in the work, or something rotten about the way the company sets pay. In either case the question now has somewhere to go. She can investigate the terms of an agreement without giving her manager the privilege of setting a price on her as a human being. He already gets quite enough for what he pays.

This is why I’m suspicious of any philosophy that promises to make you immune to comparison. Immunity would leave you with little defense against a bad bargain. The more interesting capacity is to follow a comparison as far as the evidence and your purposes take it, and then find the brakes. One audition tells you who got the chair. A pay discrepancy gives you grounds to ask how the rates were set. A photograph tells you your brother had a decent afternoon. Three different pieces of information. It would be extraordinary if they all answered the question Am I enough?

What would you like to keep?

Sam has another look at the picture over breakfast. Jo takes the phone from his hand and enlarges the loaf until it dissolves into beige squares. “Want me to get you a bread book?”

It’s a good offer. Sam considers the practicalities. He has little interest in tending to a bubbling substance in a jar. There is already one at the back of his fridge, and if he ever gets the nerve to open the lid he intends to have a priest present. He could take up running. He has tried running, and knows a great deal about how he feels about running. As for a house in his brother’s neighborhood, he would spend a large part of each day trying to get back to the neighborhood where he lives now.

What he wants from that photograph turns out to be oddly specific. He wants a morning when somebody knows he’s coming and sets out an extra cup. He wants his niece to carry on telling him a story from last week without spending the first ten minutes trying to remember what he knows about her life. He can’t recall the last time he went to his brother’s house without an occasion, a bottle and a departure time agreed in the car. Across a distance he could cover by bus, two people have slowly become guests.

There are real reasons for this. Dan had children. Sam and Jo have had years of mismatched schedules. A couple of invitations came at inconvenient times, and after the second one each brother waited politely for the other to suggest a third. Families can lose a decade like that without anybody doing anything more sinister than checking a calendar.

Now there’s something in the kitchen that Sam can act on. He sends Dan a message asking if they need another pair of hands next Sunday. Three minutes later his brother sends back a shopping list. It contains milk, coffee and what appears to be a demand for one hundred and twelve chocolate buttons. His niece has evidently obtained access to the negotiations. Sam is beginning to remember why the adults used to keep the biscuits on the top shelf.

Something could easily have gone differently in that exchange. Imagine Dan had written back that the mornings were one of the few times he had alone with the kids. It would have hurt. It would also have been an honest answer from a person who gets to have a say in the life Sam envies. The point of recognizing what you want is that it gives you the chance to ask for it. There is no accompanying clause requiring another person to say yes.

Nor is every longing going to resolve itself into a shopping list. A person who wanted children can be perfectly clear about what hurts in a friend’s family photograph. They may have pursued it at considerable cost and reached the end of the choices available to them. There’s no cleverer interpretation of the picture that will give them the years they wanted. They can love the child, be glad the child is here, and occasionally put the phone face down because they have had enough for one afternoon. Turning that grief into an assignment to improve yourself is an especially nasty way of giving a grieving person extra work.

We speak of wanting somebody else’s life as though the order went through in full. Usually we want a particular freedom, a skill, a little recognition, the chance to love somebody in a certain way. Finding the particular thing matters even when it’s out of reach. There is a dignity in knowing what you have lost. And where a choice remains, you can decide what you’re willing to give up for it. Sam will give up sleeping late on Sunday. It’s a small price. He gives himself full permission to reconsider his appraisal when the alarm goes off.

A place at the table

Sunday brings actual weather, actual children and a bag of chocolate buttons that loses several of its members on the bus under circumstances Sam declines to discuss. His brother opens the door with flour on one forearm. The house smells wonderful. It is briefly irritating how good the bread really is.

The older child is grating carrots for lunch. The younger one has been put in charge of butter, a position of almost unlimited destructive scope. Dan makes coffee. For a little while there’s nothing for Sam to do except tear off a corner of the loaf while it’s still warm enough to hurt his fingers. His brother has spent years learning to bake this well. Sam asks him how he gets the crust so crisp and gets an answer several minutes longer than he was prepared for. He has missed this too, a man he’s known all his life becoming a bore in an entirely new direction.

Later his niece looks up from the floor. “Can you do the duck with the broken leg?” He used to do it for her when she was little. He hadn’t realized she remembered. It’s an appalling piece of physical comedy. The feet go first, then the left wing slowly loses confidence in the whole enterprise. His knees are considerably older than the last time he did it. The duck has never had so much to work with.

For once he’s in the photograph. He looks ridiculous. Dan shows it to him and Sam asks for a copy. Nothing about that morning would have shown up if, a week ago, you’d asked which of the brothers was ahead. The answer required by the question has room for only one of them. What Sam wanted required both.

There are still contests he’d like to win. There are still things his brother has done that he wishes he’d done too. Over lunch Dan mentions an upcoming trip, and Sam feels the familiar little catch before he asks where they’re going. A person can learn quite a bit and still be the same petty mammal by lunchtime. This time he sticks around long enough to hear the answer.

A neighbor comes to the door for a folding chair and stays for coffee. By now Sam has grated a carrot, contributed to a minor butter emergency, listened to his brother and given a reasonably affecting performance as an injured waterfowl. The neighbor pulls out the chair and asks the ordinary question people ask upon meeting another adult for the first time.

“So, Sam. What do you do?”

Of all the things he’s just spent the morning doing, he gives her the one that comes with a payslip.
