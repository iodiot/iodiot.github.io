Chapter 21Take Responsibility Without Hating Yourself

Fran’s father watches himself get out of a chair three times before he asks who else has seen it.

The first time he gets up, his knees let him about halfway into the operation and return him gently to his point of departure. On the second attempt he obtains the assistance of a kitchen worktop. Then he straightens up, gives the oven glove on his left hand a small sinister wave and proceeds with the invasion of Earth. The bit with the glove lasts four seconds. It’s what Fran meant people to see.

On her father’s phone the film starts again automatically. There he is, getting out of the chair.

“How do you stop me doing that?”

She puts her hand over the screen. “Tap it here.”

“I know how to stop the phone, love.”

Six other people help organise the neighbourhood film night. After the film with the colander spaceship, somebody asked how they did the alien. Fran sent the group a few seconds from one of their rehearsals. Her dad had watched a version beginning with the glove rising into view from behind the worktop. He liked the shot. He also liked the impression it gave that the actor playing the alien enjoyed a degree of physical independence from his daughter. The clip she actually sent started about twelve seconds earlier.

She could tell you exactly how it happened. Two nearly identical rectangles in her phone gallery. Same kitchen, same actor, same civilisational threat from the drawer where they kept the tea towels. One had been trimmed. One hadn’t. It was close to bedtime. Look at the thumbnails side by side and see how well you do. There’s an excellent defence available here, and you can hear the chairs being set out for it in her head.

The difficulty is what happened next morning. Somebody sent back a laughing face. Fran watched what she’d posted. She saw the extra seconds and thought about taking it down. There was a nice message from the projectionist about the little wave, and another from a woman whose son had apparently gone to bed conducting a similar operation with a sock. She left it up. The morning decision took less than a breath. She was enjoying something and preferred, for the moment, to go on enjoying it.

“Six people in the group,” she says. “I can ask if anybody’s passed it on.”

“Yes. Find that out.”

He goes into the kitchen. She hears the tap, the spoon in the sugar, the radio coming on. She stays in the front room looking at the small, light device in her hand. An hour ago it contained an explanation fit to carry into court. Now it mostly contains the numbers of six people she needs to call.

A closed door and a different man

A few weeks before this, Fran left the last date off a notice for evening music practice. A trombone player took two buses to a locked hall. She admitted the mistake, offered him his fare and put a contact number on the corrected notice. This was a pretty instructive experience, right up to the moment she considered drawing from it the general conclusion that she had now become a person who takes responsibility. That might look rather well on a personal website. It does very little to get twelve seconds of your father off somebody else’s telephone.

The particulars have changed. The trombonist lost an evening and the cost of a journey. He could say what he wanted back. Her dad has lost some say over who gets to watch a hard bit of his day. There’s nothing useful she can transfer from her banking app. If she offers to buy him a new chair he may find a use for the old one that neither of them will enjoy.

She’d like a rule. Own it, say sorry, make amends, go forth a moderately improved member of the species. What do you do if the person you’ve hurt has an errand to run and no current interest in presiding over the ceremony of your moral improvement? Her father comes back in for his coat. He’s going to the shop. Does she want anything?

“No,” she says. Then, because it feels unbearable to let him leave while they’re like this, “Dad, I feel absolutely rotten.”

“I expect you do. Is there any milk?”

She checks. There’s enough for breakfast. He takes his bag and goes. For forty minutes she is going to have to manage her own wickedness without professional assistance from the man whose morning she spoiled.

There are good reasons people come to think self-hatred is a form of accountability. Most of us have watched an objectionable bastard sleep wonderfully after a day spent distributing the consequences of his behaviour among other households. It isn’t an attractive spectacle. If those are the alternatives, a bad night sounds like the least you ought to provide. And if you love the person you’ve hurt, of course some of the hurt reaches you. It would be peculiar to insist on cheerfulness as a duty immediately following the discovery that you’ve been a shit.

The question is what moral work the bad night gets done. If the objective was to have one additional person feel dreadful before breakfast, the enterprise has succeeded beyond its investors’ wildest hopes. If the objective was to find out how far a film has travelled, the first forty minutes have passed without a telephone call. Nor is the father necessarily comforted by his daughter’s poor opinion of herself. He was rather fond of her beforehand. Now he has to learn that he raised a monster on top of everything else he’s got on his list for the supermarket.

Fran types a message to the group. I am absolutely mortified and disgusted with myself. She reads this twice. It’s accurate. It’s also breaking news about Fran, at a moment when the six people reading it need some fairly straightforward instructions about a video. One of the less flattering questions you can ask of your most anguished sentence is who gets the leading part.

She deletes it. Tries again. I sent the rehearsal clip that starts with Dad getting out of the chair. He agreed to share a shorter version. Please delete this one and let me know privately if you sent a copy to anyone. I’m taking it down here too. Fran.

For a second her thumb hovers over the button. This is worse than the mortified version. The mortified version informs the committee that Fran has extremely high ethical standards, against which she has, on this isolated occasion, fallen short. This version allows the six people to have whatever opinion of her they happen to form when they read what she did. She presses send.

Who was at the wheel

In an essay on moral luck, Bernard Williams imagines a lorry driver who, through no fault of his own, runs over a child. Williams is interested in a distinction you could easily miss if you treated moral philosophy as a method of allocating points on a driving licence. A spectator might be appalled and profoundly sorry it happened. There is something particular the driver can feel about having been the person at the wheel. Williams calls it agent-regret. You can grant him that feeling and still mean every word when you say he wasn’t to blame. [1]

There is a considerable distance between a child being killed and the wrong file going into a group chat. Keep it. The thought experiment does one precise job here. It prises apart questions that often arrive in one large indigestible parcel. Did something happen through me? Was I in a position to do better? Is there anything I can do for the people who have to live with it? You can answer yes to the first without answering yes to the second. And sometimes the third has an answer even where the second does not.

Picture the vastly more ordinary business of backing into a neighbour’s bicycle. It has fallen flat behind the car in a place you could not reasonably have seen. You feel something under the wheel. Even if the bike’s owner accepts that you did everything a careful person would do, it’s quite reasonable for you to get out, help pick it up and take an interest in how they’re going to get home. Now change one fact. Their child knocked on your window to tell you the bike was there, and you drove on because you thought the knocking was some idiotic game. Same bent wheel, different answer to the question of blame. One fact changes again if you can mend wheels and your neighbour needs to be at work in an hour. You have a practical opportunity to help that a faultless witness with a manicure and a positive attitude may lack entirely.

These distinctions become especially important when the thing that happened has several causes. Fran can point to an interface that makes one file look very like another. Good. Change the name. Put the approved file somewhere separate. The interface is an exceptionally unpromising candidate for lunch with her father, however, and has no views on what the projectionist thought of the glove. It didn’t see the clip the next morning and decide to let a lovely compliment stand. That part of the transaction has a name on it.

You can take responsibility for a part without volunteering for the lot. Somebody else may have copied the film. That person is better placed to tell you where it went. Fran can ask. She can follow up. She can’t reach back through the eyes of six adults, wipe the memory and put the little doors down again. There’s a temptation to promise exactly that kind of thing when a person you love looks at you with disappointment. You’d promise to adjust the orbit of Venus for the privilege of seeing their usual expression return. An offer to do the obtainable work by five this afternoon sounds so much less impressive, especially to the woman making the offer.

The first reply comes from the projectionist. He’s deleted his copy. Hadn’t noticed the chair business. Thought the glove was a hoot. She spends a moment savouring this witness statement for the defence. Then she rereads what her father asked. Where did it go? It went as far as the projectionist and he didn’t send it on. That’s what she writes down. How much he noticed is his affair. Which parts of himself her father wanted shown is her father’s.

A second person replies that she showed it to her son on her own screen. There’s no copy on his phone. Now there are two columns in Fran’s notebook. Has seen and has a copy. This already contains more useful moral information than an hour of asking herself whether she’s an awful daughter. It also tells her something she would rather not have found out. Seven people saw the clip. The son doesn’t even belong to the group. Getting curious about consequences can be a rather poor strategy for lightening your sentence.

By the time Dad gets back three people have confirmed deletion. One is sorry. One wonders if they might still show the short version at the next film night. The third sends a little drawing of a heart, which can be filed under heartfelt sentiments with absolutely zero forensic value. Fran has a beginning to bring to her father. He puts the shopping on the table and takes out two pints of milk.

“You said we had enough.”

“We do.”

He shuts the fridge. “Tremendous. At least the cows have had a productive morning.”

An apology you could turn down

“What did it look like to you?” she asks.

There’s an honest danger in asking this question. Dad might say it looked like nothing in particular, a slightly undignified few seconds that everybody can forget. He might also tell her something she didn’t know about getting out of a chair, which will mean adding to her account of the harm at a time when she’d sooner be knocking a few items off.

He tells her he practises getting up on his own. He has a good chair in the front room for the purpose, high enough that he can do it if he takes a moment and gets his feet in the right place. In the film he’s in a lower chair because it gave him the right angle on the spaceship. He knows he’s going to need the worktop. He knew it when he allowed her to set up the camera. He had reckoned on choosing the bit that other people would see.

“When I made the film,” he says, “I meant to be the fellow operating a glove. I don’t want to be the fellow that people feel nice about because he can still operate a glove.”

There’s half a sentence of reassurance in her mouth. Nobody thought that. How on earth would she know? She’s just spent part of the morning complimenting herself on how thoroughly she understands other people on the strength of a few words on a screen.

“And I’m not embarrassed about needing a hand,” he says. “I get embarrassed by the way everybody suddenly looks very interested in the ceiling when I need one.”

It’s a different complaint from the one she would have apologised for if he’d let her get on with the speech she prepared. In that speech she’s stolen his dignity. He’d be quite offended to learn that his dignity was so poorly attached a woman could remove it from him with an index finger. What she took, on a small and particular occasion, was a choice.

“I’m sorry I chose for you,” she says. “I saw it next morning. I could have taken it down then.”

“Why didn’t you?”

“I liked that they liked it.”

He nods. She would have preferred him to look a little more astonished by this revelation. It’s entirely possible the villain who brought shame on the family by wanting people to like her film is a woman whose character he’s been acquainted with for some time.

“Leave the short one with me,” he says. “I’ll think about what to do with it.”

There have been actual experiments on what makes an apology convincing. Roy Lewicki and his colleagues gave people scenarios involving a breach of trust and varied what appeared in the apology. Acknowledging responsibility and offering to put things right carried substantial weight in participants’ ratings. Asking to be forgiven counted for considerably less. These were people evaluating written scenarios. Nobody in the study was standing next to this fridge with entirely too much milk in it. [2]

Still, the result points to a confusion worth noticing. Will you forgive me? is a request for the other person to do something. That can be a perfectly reasonable question after you’ve heard what happened to them and taken whatever action is available to you. At the start it can operate rather like a waiter setting the bill down before anybody’s seen the food. Here’s your portion of the work. If you would kindly absolve me, I have further appointments.

There are situations in which a person has excellent reason to decline. Imagine somebody who borrowed your van three times, returned it three times with an empty tank, and on each occasion stood in your kitchen displaying an increasingly sophisticated understanding of their own faults. By round four you might welcome a person with slightly less insight and forty quid’s worth of diesel. You can accept the apology, care about their continuing moral development and put your keys somewhere else. Forgiveness, trust and future access to the van are decisions a reasonable adult can make on three different dates.

This is awkward for the person trying to improve. They really may have changed this time. In the privacy of their own mind they have access to the sincerity of the new intention, the physical sensation of the awful sinking feeling, the little burst of determination as they promise to do better. Out in the drive a person looking at the gauge has access to rather different instruments. It’s perfectly rational for evidence to accumulate at different speeds in these two locations. If you want to close the gap, there are a number of filling stations on the road to almost everywhere.

Fran’s dad hasn’t asked her to stop filming. He’s asked for the short film and time to think. She’s tempted to offer a lifetime ban on herself for the handling of electronic equipment in the vicinity of any living relative. In the theatre of her mind it looks splendid. Out here it would remove his choice a second time. Maybe he’d like to make another film with his daughter. It would be nice if one of them got to ask him about it.

The price of a clean conscience

A small practical objection remains. If you stop calling yourself a piece of shit, aren’t you rather likely to do the shitty thing again? At least while you loathe yourself there’s a security guard on duty. You may not enjoy his methods, but you have serious doubts about the character of the person he’s guarding.

Juliana Breines and Serena Chen investigated a version of this question. In one experiment participants recalled something wrong they’d done. People guided to respond to themselves with compassion reported greater motivation to make amends and avoid repeating it than people in the comparison conditions. In another experiment, after an initial test failure, participants in the self-compassion condition actually spent longer studying for another test. The first result concerns what people said they wanted to do about a past wrong. Extra minutes spent studying in a different experiment do not establish that a single neighbour got their diesel money. They do make it harder to maintain that there’s something inherently reformative about treating yourself like vermin. [3]

Perhaps compassion sounds to you like someone in a loose cardigan promising that deep down you always meant well. Fran didn’t always mean well. For a few seconds she chose a small personal pleasure over her father’s preferences. A more generous account of herself can include the unlovely bit without dilution. She’s able to like applause, love her father and make a selfish decision on a Sunday morning. Give her that amount of moral equipment and you have a person complicated enough to be responsible for something. Reduce her to a species of ambulatory sewage and it becomes quite mysterious on what theory anybody has entrusted her with a camera, let alone asked her to make a better choice.

There’s a selfish attraction in a sweeping verdict. I ruin everything allows you to avoid the tedious business of finding out exactly what you ruined. If you really do ruin everything, your prospects for Tuesday are settled. There’s very little point learning the names of the people who have copies. Call yourself somebody who sent the wrong file and then liked the attention, and suddenly the day is full of jobs. A particular file can be labelled. A person can be rung. An uncomfortable desire can be noticed next time it opens its little mouth. You’ve gone from starring in a grand tragedy to doing paperwork in a problem you could conceivably solve.

Self-forgiveness is sometimes presented as the last item on a list, a ceremony to be held once restitution is complete. What about a fault whose consequences you cannot put right? Someone crashes the car and a passenger lives with pain thereafter. You can give lifts, pay what you owe, listen when the other person wants to speak and keep your promises. The passenger can go on hurting. An account that requires you to undo all the harm before you are allowed to be a recognisable human being will put the rest of both lives in permanent receivership. Your pain doesn’t travel through some morally governed heat exchanger to relieve theirs. If such machines existed, families would have one in the kitchen next to the toaster.

There’s also a boundary to what another person can ask. Doing wrong doesn’t sign away your future consent. Somebody you once let down can reasonably want an apology, a repair, some distance or an arrangement which prevents a recurrence. If they know that one reference to that incident will get you to do anything at all, indefinitely, you may at some point need to refuse a demand even while accepting the original fault. This is uncomfortable territory. A great many people would be delighted to hear that refusing to pay back money they actually owe is an act of self-care. You can generally get a clearer picture by stating what happened, what remains owing and what new thing is being asked. The nouns help. Loan. Repayment. Weekend spent repainting a conservatory. Somewhere in there the subject changed.

It’s evening before the last person replies. One copy went on to a sister in Brighton. She has deleted it too. One person can confirm taking it off his phone but can’t be certain about automatic backups. Fran resists the extremely British impulse to send a polite message to Brighton asking whether somebody might nip into their cloud and have a bit of a look round. She tells her father exactly what she’s been able to establish. The clip is down. All six have replied. One forwarded copy was reported and removed. She cannot promise there are no others.

“That’ll do,” he says.

It takes a moment for her to understand that this is an instruction about how much more investigation he wants. It isn’t a declaration about the ultimate state of her soul. He’s picked up his tablet and is going through the shorter film. Frame by frame, his glove comes up from behind the worktop. For four seconds of cinema he has quite a lot of suggestions.

Take two

Three weeks later he asks her to come round. He wants a shot of the spaceship descending into the sink. There’s an old tin of peaches in juice which, combined with the action of the draining board, he believes can be made to suggest a planet in the grip of a serious geological disturbance. She’s welcome to sacrifice a tea towel to the advancement of human knowledge. He has bought a little remote button that will start and stop the camera from his chair.

When she gets there he hands it to her. It needs pairing with the phone. This is so far beyond the simple rules of reward and punishment she would have proposed three weeks ago that she has to laugh. Did he spend good money on a way to get her finger off the button only to discover it would require her finger on the button?

“You get it working,” he says. “I’ll do the damage.”

They prop the phone on two cookbooks. In the view on the screen you can see the sink and one edge of the curtain. She was going to frame it tighter to keep the curtain out. He likes the curtain. It makes the possibility of an entire unsuspecting suburban population more credible. Apparently the alien civilisation has got a distribution deal and is now in a position to issue notes.

He gets up. One hand takes the worktop. She waits while he gets his balance. This time he has the button, and he leaves the camera running. Fran glances over in surprise.

“I may want that bit,” he says.

The same event can be experienced quite differently by the person who gets to decide where the picture begins. If you cared only about the eventual number of people who saw a pair of old knees, you could label the morning absurd and the whole previous business an overreaction. He might show the new footage to the exact same six people. There’s a human distinction here that a view count cannot pick up. He gets to make a joke about his own arse taking two goes to reach escape velocity. He might rather enjoy it. It’s his arse.

She asks if he wants a hand with the peaches. He does. When the spaceship hits the surface, a piece of fruit goes into the washing-up liquid and conducts the first credible search for intelligent life under their taps. Dad says a word which will considerably limit the film’s chances of getting into the schools programme. They have the fit of giggles you get when there’s a mess to clean up and the person who normally minds is laughing harder than you are.

Afterwards they sit down to look. For a bit Fran watches him instead of the little screen. She’s spent three weeks trying to make a fair estimate of what sort of person she is. It comes as a relief to find him thinking about something else. He’s got to the picture of himself getting up. He takes it backwards and forwards with his thumb. Starts here. No, there. She can see the exact instant he’s looking for. One hand steady on the worktop, the other just beginning to rise. He presses the button. Into the kitchen comes the glove.
