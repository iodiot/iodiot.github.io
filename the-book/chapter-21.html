Chapter 21Take Responsibility Without Hating Yourself

Fran’s father watches himself fail to get out of a chair three times before he asks who else has seen it.

“Three thousand four hundred,” she says. “About.”

In the clip Walt’s knees take him halfway up and set him back down where he started. On the second try he gets a hand on the kitchen counter and the counter does its share. He’s standing. On his left hand is a rubber kitchen glove. That’s where it ends. Twelve seconds. Across the bottom somebody has typed him vs the chair, round 2.

Fran is holding the phone up between them. It starts over by itself. There he goes, getting out of the chair.

He raises a hand at the screen, the way he’d stop a car backing toward him. “How do you stop me from doing that?”

“Tap it here.”

“I know how to stop the phone, sweetheart.”

He looks at the frozen picture. “Where’s the wave?”

“He cut it.”

“Who did?”

“A boy named Caleb. He’s fifteen.”

Walt considers this. “He took out the only part with any acting in it.”

Here’s how twelve seconds of an old man got from a kitchen to three thousand four hundred phones. Fran is one of the three people who run film night, and collecting the entries was her job, so the film-night group chat is hers. Six other people are in it, the projectionist and the people behind the five other films. On Thursday night, months after the picture with the colander spaceship was shown, one of them asked how they’d done the alien. In the finished film Fran worked the glove and her father held the camera. In the kitchen rehearsals he’d worn it himself. He’d approved a four-second version for other people’s eyes, in which the glove rises from behind the counter and gives a small sinister wave. He liked the shot. He also liked the impression it gave that the actor playing the alien enjoyed some physical independence from his daughter. Fran sent the other file, the one that starts twelve seconds earlier.

She can tell you how it happened. Two nearly identical rectangles in her phone’s gallery. Same kitchen, same actor, same threat to civilization from the drawer where they keep the dish towels. One had been trimmed and one hadn’t. It was bedtime. Look at the thumbnails side by side and see how well you do.

It’s a good excuse, and it covers Thursday. On Friday morning somebody sent a laughing face. Fran watched what she’d posted, saw the extra seconds and thought about taking it down. The projectionist had written something nice about the wave. Somebody wanted to book the alien for a kid’s birthday. She left it up. The decision took less than a breath. She was enjoying something and preferred to go on enjoying it.

On Saturday one of the six, a woman who’d entered a film of her own, showed it to her son at breakfast, because of the glove. He asked her to send it to him, and she did. He did some trimming of his own. He kept the chair, cut the wave, typed a caption across the bottom and put it on a video app. His mother’s film ran three careful minutes, and the neighborhood watched it politely from folding chairs. Her son’s cut runs twelve seconds, puts his words on another man’s body, and has been seen by more people than everything shown at film night put together.

On Sunday morning his mother sent Fran the link. Don’t be mad. I’ve told him to take it down. For what it’s worth, people love your dad. Under the clip, strangers had written protect this man and why the glove tho and me every morning. A company that sells lift recliners had left a discount code.

Fran thought for about a minute about not telling him. Most of those strangers go to high school with Caleb, and some of them bag groceries at the store where her father buys his milk. So she’s shown him herself. She’d like some credit for that. Nobody’s offering any.

“Can you get it back?” he asks.

“I can get the boy to take his down. I can’t get it back.”

“Do that, then.”

He goes into the kitchen. She hears the faucet, the spoon in the sugar, the radio coming on. An hour ago she had an explanation involving two thumbnails. It’s still true. Nobody’s left to give it to.

What a bad night buys

Last fall Fran left a date off a notice, and in January Leon took two buses with a trombone to a locked building. She admitted it, paid his fares and put a phone number on the corrected notice. It went well enough that she was tempted to conclude she’d become a person who takes responsibility. That would look good on a poster. She’s had some recent experience with posters. It does nothing to get her father off three thousand phones.

Leon lost an evening and some bus fare, and he could say what he wanted back. Her dad has lost his say over who watches a hard part of his day, and nothing in her banking app transfers that. If she offers to buy him a higher chair, he may find a use for the old one that neither of them will enjoy.

She’d like a rule. Own it, apologize, make amends, go forth a moderately improved member of the species. The rule assumes the person you hurt has the morning free to supervise. Her father comes back in for his coat. He’s going to the store. Does she want anything?

“No,” she says. Then, because she can’t stand to let him leave like this, “Dad, I feel sick about it.”

“I’m sure you do. Do we have milk?”

She checks. They have enough for breakfast. He takes his bag and goes. For the next forty minutes she’ll have to manage her own wickedness without help from the man it happened to.

People come honestly by the idea that hating yourself is a form of accountability. Most of us have watched some bastard sleep like a baby after a day spent distributing the consequences of his behavior among other households. If that’s the alternative, a bad night seems like the least you can offer. And if you love the person you hurt, some of the hurt reaches you. It would be strange to demand cheerfulness from a woman who’s just watched what she did to her father play on a loop.

The question is what the bad night gets done. If the goal was one more person feeling awful before lunch, it’s a triumph. If the goal was getting a video taken down, twenty of the forty minutes are gone and the number is 3,480. Her father isn’t comforted by her poor opinion of herself, either. He liked her fine on Thursday. Now, on top of the milk, he’s supposed to absorb the news that he raised a monster.

She types a message to the group. I am mortified and disgusted with myself. She reads it twice. It’s accurate. It’s also breaking news about Fran, at a moment when six people need instructions about a video. A useful thing to ask your most anguished sentence is who has the lead in it.

She deletes it and tries again. I sent the rehearsal clip that starts with Dad getting out of the chair. He’d agreed to a shorter one. A copy is now public. Please delete yours and tell me privately if you sent it to anyone. Fran.

Her thumb hovers. This is worse than the mortified version. That one told the group that Fran has very high standards from which, this once, she has slipped. This one lets six people think whatever they think when they read what she did. She sends it. Then she writes to Caleb’s mother. Please have him take it down today. And ask him who else has it.

Who was at the wheel

In an essay on moral luck, the philosopher Bernard Williams imagines a truck driver who, through no fault of his own, runs over a child. A bystander would be appalled and sorry. Williams thinks the driver is entitled to something more, a feeling about having been the one at the wheel, and he calls it agent-regret. You can grant the driver that feeling and still mean every word when you tell him he wasn’t to blame.[1]

A long distance separates a dead child from a video of a man in a chair. Keep it. The thought experiment does one job here. It separates questions that usually arrive as a single lump. Whether it happened through me. Whether I could have done better. Whether there’s anything I can do now for the people who have to live with it.

Fran’s answers change with the day of the week. On Thursday it happened through her, and a careful person at bedtime with two identical thumbnails might have done the same. On Friday she could have done better and knew it. Saturday belonged to a fifteen-year-old she’s never met, and it still had to pass through her thumb to reach him. That’s three yeses to the first question and one to the second. The third is still open, and it’s the only one her father asked.

You’ve met this weather before, in the chapter with the film night, where the note Eisenhower wrote that day got one sentence. Here’s the rest. On June 5, 1944, with the forecast in and the order given, Dwight Eisenhower took a pencil and wrote out what he would announce if the landings in Normandy failed. It runs four sentences. The first says the landings have failed to gain a foothold “and I have withdrawn the troops.” His first draft said the troops “have been withdrawn.” He changed it so the sentence had a person in it. He started the next one with “This particular operation,” crossed that out and wrote “My decision to attack.” The decision, he says, was based on the best information available. The troops, the air and the Navy did everything bravery and devotion could do. The last sentence reads, “If any blame or fault attaches to the attempt it is mine alone.” He dated it July 5. It was June. He put it in his wallet, and five weeks later he showed it to his naval aide, Harry Butcher, and said he’d written one like it before every amphibious operation of the war and torn the others up. Butcher asked to keep this one. It’s in the Eisenhower Presidential Library in Abilene, Kansas.[2]

Look at what’s missing. Not one word about what kind of man he is. He doesn’t say he’s sickened. He says what happened, what he decided, what he knew and whose it is. One sentence defends the decision and another takes the blame for it, and he saw no reason to choose between them.

Nobody had to live under that note. The landings held and it stayed in his wallet. And “mine alone” was accurate for him. He was the Supreme Commander, and those two words kept the blame off everybody below him. Fran commands nobody. If she tells the group this is entirely my fault, she’s wrong about the facts and she’s taken the lead again. Her part is the file and the Friday. Caleb’s part is Caleb’s. He’s fifteen, and somebody ought to see that he gets it.

So she takes her part and does the work that goes with it. The projectionist answers first. He’s deleted his copy. Hadn’t noticed the chair. Thought the glove was a hoot. She enjoys this for a second, then rereads what her father asked for. He didn’t ask what people thought. She starts two columns in a notebook, has seen and has a copy.

Caleb takes his post down at 3,612 views. His mother writes, Done. He says sorry. He says tell your dad he’s a legend. Fran searches for his caption and finds the twelve seconds on two accounts she’s never heard of. One has a cartoon frog for a face. The other collects clips of old people being sweet and has given this one a new title, He never gave up. She reports both. The form asks whether she is the person in the video. She isn’t. The person in the video is at the store.

Getting curious about consequences is a poor way to feel better. When she drew the second column it had six names to chase. Now it has no bottom.

Walt comes home and takes two half gallons of milk out of his bag.

“You said we had enough.”

“We do.”

He shuts the fridge. “Then the cows had a good morning.”

An apology you could turn down

By midafternoon she has something to tell him. The boy’s post is down. Five of the six in the group have deleted theirs. One of them is sorry. Someone wonders if they could still show the short version at the next film night. Someone sent a heart, which is nice and tells her nothing. Two copies are out where she can’t reach them, each with somebody else’s words on it.

“What did it look like to you?” she asks.

It’s a risky question. He might say it looked like nothing much. He might tell her something she didn’t know, and then the list gets longer just when she was hoping to cross something off.

He tells her he practices getting up. The good chair in the living room is high enough that he can do it alone if he takes a second and sets his feet. For the rehearsal he sat in the low kitchen chair, because it gave him the right angle on the spaceship. He knew he’d need the counter. He knew it when he let her set up the camera. He’d counted on choosing which part people saw.

Clinics time the same exercise. In the CDC’s fall-risk kit you sit in a chair seventeen inches high, arms crossed at the wrists, and count how many times you can stand in thirty seconds. For a man of eighty to eighty-four, fewer than ten is below average. Put a hand on anything and the test stops with a score of zero.[3] A few thousand strangers had watched a zero and typed never give up.

“When I said send the short one,” he says, “I meant to be the guy working the glove. Now I’m the guy three thousand people feel good about because he got out of a chair.”

She has half a reassurance in her mouth. Nobody thinks that. She’s read the comments. It’s exactly what they think.

“And I’m not embarrassed about needing a hand,” he says. “I get embarrassed by the way everybody suddenly looks very interested in the ceiling when I need one.” He takes off his glasses and rubs his eyes. “These people didn’t look at the ceiling. I’ll give them that. They looked right at me and said aww.”

It’s a different complaint from the one she’d prepared to apologize for. In her speech she’s stolen his dignity. He’d be offended to hear that his dignity was attached so loosely a woman could remove it with one finger. What she took was a choice. Then a boy and a few thousand strangers took what was left of it and wrote on it.

“I’m sorry I chose for you,” she says. “I saw it Friday morning. If I’d taken it down then, the boy never gets it.”

“Why didn’t you?”

“I liked that they liked it.”

He nods. She’d have preferred a little more astonishment. Apparently the woman who brought shame on the family by wanting people to like her film is someone he’s known for a while.

“Leave the short one with me,” he says. “I’ll think about what to do with it.”

Roy Lewicki and his colleagues have tested what makes an apology work. They gave people scenarios involving a breach of trust and varied what the apology contained. Acknowledging responsibility carried the most weight in the ratings, and an offer to repair the damage came next. Asking to be forgiven counted least. These were written scenarios, rated by people the breach hadn’t happened to.[4]

Still, the ranking points at a confusion. Will you forgive me? asks the other person to do something. Later, once you’ve heard what happened to them and done what you can, it may be a fair thing to ask. Asked first, it’s a waiter putting the check down before anybody’s seen the food.

And some people have good reason to say no. Imagine somebody who borrowed your van three times, brought it back three times on empty, and each time stood in your kitchen showing a deeper understanding of their own faults. By round four you’d trade the insight for forty bucks of gas. Forgiving them, trusting them and lending them the van are three decisions, and they don’t have to fall on the same day.

Walt hasn’t taken Fran’s keys. He’s asked for the short film and some time. She’s tempted to offer a lifetime ban on herself from operating a phone near any living relative. In her head it’s magnificent. In this kitchen it would be choosing for him a second time. He might want to make another film with his daughter. It would be nice if one of them asked him.

The price of a clean conscience

One objection is still standing. If Fran stops calling herself a piece of shit, isn’t she more likely to do it again? At least while she loathes herself, a guard’s on duty. His methods are ugly, but look who he’s guarding.

Juliana Breines and Serena Chen tested a version of this. In one experiment, people recalled something they’d done wrong. Those guided to respond to themselves with compassion reported more motivation to make amends and not repeat it than people in the comparison conditions. In another, after failing a test, the self-compassion group spent longer studying for the next one. The first result is what people said they wanted to do, and the second is minutes at a desk. Neither shows that anybody’s amends got made. They do make it hard to argue that treating yourself like vermin is what does the reforming.[5]

Compassion here doesn’t mean a person in a soft cardigan telling Fran she always meant well. She didn’t. For a few seconds on Friday she put a small pleasure ahead of her father’s wishes. A fair account of her includes that at full strength. She likes applause, she loves her father and she made a selfish choice before breakfast. That’s a person complicated enough to be responsible for something. Reduce her to walking sewage and it gets hard to say why anyone would hand her a camera, or ask her to do better with it.

I ruin everything has a selfish appeal of its own. It spares you finding out exactly what you ruined. If Fran ruins everything, Tuesday is settled and she doesn’t have to learn which accounts have copies. Call her a woman who sent the wrong file and then liked the attention, and the day fills up with jobs. A file to rename. A boy’s mother to call. A wish to be liked that she can watch for the next time it opens its little mouth.

The jobs run out before the harm does. The app reviews her reports and finds nothing wrong with either video. He never gave up stays up. For a week Fran checks it last thing at night. 1,180. 1,240. It takes her four nights to work out that the app is counting her too. Every time she goes to look at what she did, she does a little more of it. In 2003 Barbra Streisand sued a photographer over an aerial picture of her house, one frame in a survey of the whole California coast. Before the suit it had been downloaded six times, twice by her own lawyers.[6] After it, hundreds of thousands of people went to look. Fran is running the small version. She’s the lawyers. None of what it costs her reaches him as relief.

On the second Saturday he comes home from the store without the milk. A boy bagging groceries had recognized him. “Chair guy,” the boy said, delighted, and asked him to do it.

“Do what?” says Fran.

“Get up. I was standing right in front of him.” He hangs up his coat. For now, she buys the milk.

That evening she tells him everything she’s established. The boy’s post is gone. All six in the group have answered. Two copies are still up, the app won’t remove them, and she can’t promise those are the only ones.

“That’ll do,” he says.

It takes her a moment to understand that this is an instruction about how much more digging he wants. It says nothing about the state of her soul. He has the four-second version open on his tablet and is going through it frame by frame. For four seconds of cinema he has a lot of notes.

Take two

Ten days later he calls her into the kitchen. He wants a shot of the spaceship coming down into the sink. He has an old can of peaches in juice which, with the help of the dish rack, he believes will suggest a planet in serious geological trouble. She’s welcome to sacrifice a dish towel to science. And he’s bought a little remote button that starts and stops the camera from across the room.

He hands it to her. It needs pairing with the phone. She laughs before she can stop herself. He’s spent good money to get her finger off the button, and the first thing the button needs is her finger.

“You get it working,” he says. “I’ll do the damage.”

They prop the phone on two cookbooks. The frame holds the sink and one edge of the curtain. She’d have cropped the curtain out. He likes it. It suggests a whole unsuspecting neighborhood on the other side. He settles into the low chair, the one from the rehearsal, with the remote in his hand. Fran stands at the sink holding the colander up by its thread. He raises a finger. Not yet. A cloud has to finish with the window.

When the spaceship hits the surface, a slice of peach goes into the dish soap and conducts the first credible search for intelligent life under their faucet. Walt says a word that will keep the picture out of the schools program. They get the kind of giggles you get when you’ve made a mess and the person who usually minds is laughing harder than you are.

Then he asks for the glove. This time he’s playing the alien himself. He pulls it on, sets his feet and starts to get up. One hand takes the counter. She waits while he finds his balance. He has the button, and he’s left the camera running. Fran looks over at him.

“I might want that part,” he says.

If the only thing that mattered were how many people watch an old man’s knees give out, the last two weeks would be an overreaction. He might show this footage to the same six people. He might put it up himself, where the boy at the store can find it. A view count can’t tell that apart from what happened to him. This time he gets to make the joke about his own ass needing two tries to reach escape velocity. He might even enjoy it. It’s his ass.

Afterward they sit down to look. For a while Fran watches him instead of the screen. She’s spent two weeks estimating what kind of person she is, and it’s a relief to find him thinking about something else. On a page she can’t reach, the other twelve seconds are still going around under a title he didn’t write. He’s reached the part where he gets up. He runs it back and forth with his thumb. Starts here. No, there. She can see the frame he’s looking for, one hand steady on the counter, the other just starting to rise. He presses play. Into the kitchen comes the glove.
