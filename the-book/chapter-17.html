Chapter 17Black Holes, Death, and the Art of Letting Go

“Put it on its side and take your thumb away,” says Tess. “See? That’s what the flat bit is for.”

Ellen turns the wooden mushroom over. The patch on the side of its cap is roughly the shape of a thumbnail.

“Did Grandad show you that?”

“I made the bloody thing.”

There’s a pleasure peculiar to the younger sibling in being able to produce, after an interval of thirty-odd years, documentary evidence that the older sibling was paying no attention whatsoever. Tess intends to enjoy hers. She made the mushroom in woodwork. Somebody else in the class made a bowl with a hole right through the bottom. The teacher displayed both. Their mother used the mushroom for years and at no point in this long and fruitful association did the mushroom once piss soup all over a tablecloth.

“Do you want it back?” says Ellen.

“Yes. And bring some change. I need a pound coin.”

“What for?”

“Something very exciting involving a pocket.”

Their mother has been dead for three years. During that time Ellen has disposed of a fridge freezer, cancelled a telephone line, given a tin of assorted foreign coins to a charity shop and received four separate letters addressed to The Late Mrs followed by offers to renew her breakdown cover. The insurer appeared to have a better understanding of her mother’s present circumstances than of its own limitations as a service provider.

There are objects Ellen could get rid of because everybody agreed they were objects. There are others that have acquired the status of witnesses. Under the cap of the darning mushroom is a dent the shape of a grain of rice. She has owned it for three years without taking a scrap of interest in that dent. Now, for the price of a short phone call, she knows who made the mushroom and how it got the flat side, and suddenly she would quite like to know what made the dent. By lunchtime she and Tess have set the thing up on a pair of cotton reels in Tess’s kitchen and are examining it with the little magnifying glass their mother used for threading needles. All it needs to be a proper museum exhibit is a label and a wealthy man who stole it from somewhere else.

Tess remembers the sanding. She remembers their mother making an appreciative noise and immediately producing a sock so disgusting that the eleven-year-old Tess briefly reconsidered her position on homemade presents. She has no idea about the dent. Maybe their mother dropped it. Maybe one of them used it to hit something. There were long periods of their childhood when the availability of a wooden weapon and the proximity of a sister would have been quite sufficient to explain a dent.

“You should have asked her,” Tess says.

It’s a perfectly ordinary remark. It takes Ellen all the way back to her own kitchen three years ago, a tea towel folded in quarters on the draining board and her sister’s name lighting up her phone. She had been on the evening visits at the hospital all week. On Friday she and Tess had had a row about whose turn it was to deal with a bag of wet clothes. There had been forceful statements involving a calendar and an allocation of labour. Their mother had been asleep for the best of it and would have been sorry to miss two daughters prepared to come to blows over the right not to do the laundry. When Tess called on Saturday, Ellen saw the name, thought You can do one thing without me, and carried on folding the tea towel.

Their mother died that night. For three years Ellen has been fairly certain she missed the last occasion on which her mother wanted to speak to her. The call lasted long enough for the towel to go into the drawer. Its little red record sat on her phone for weeks. She can picture it now. There’s a question she has never asked Tess, a question a good deal nearer to hand than the one about the dent.

Where a message can come from

A certain kind of person is going to try to explain the death of your mother to you using a black hole. On this occasion the person has put it in the title, so there was at least adequate warning. We might reasonably require him to explain what a black hole actually does, where the comparison runs out, and whether he can put the kettle on before he starts.

Consider an astronaut falling freely towards a very large black hole. Give her a watch, a radio and the right to file a serious complaint about the terms of her employment. Far away, a colleague waits for news. While she is outside the event horizon she can in principle send an outward signal that reaches him. After she crosses, she can carry on talking into the microphone. Her colleague, however good his aerial and however long his grant is renewed, cannot receive a new message she sends from inside. In the classical picture of an isolated black hole, the event horizon is the boundary that marks precisely that distinction. From inside, even a light signal aimed outwards cannot get away to a distant observer. [1]

This isn’t an obstacle like a steel wall. A sufficiently determined civil servant can run a cable through a steel wall eventually, provided you have completed the form requesting a consultation concerning the proposed hole. An event horizon changes what destinations a signal can have. Light still travels locally at the speed of light. The astronaut can shine a torch across her cabin. It is the route from that event in the cabin to the distant colleague that no longer exists. Turning up the brightness would be a beautifully expensive way of misunderstanding the problem.

A black hole needn’t even be a particularly large target for a given mass. For an ideal black hole with no spin or electric charge, the horizon’s radius is twice the gravitational constant times the mass, divided by the speed of light squared. At one solar mass the answer is roughly three kilometres. At ten solar masses it’s roughly thirty. That’s the radius, so a drawing that gives the ten-solar-mass example a sixty-kilometre diameter has read the brief correctly. Scale the mass and you scale that radius by the same factor. Nothing in this arithmetic tells you where to place a neat little black disc over a dying star’s photograph. The radius belongs to a particular model, and real astronomical black holes can spin.

The astronaut’s own watch can reach the crossing in a finite time. What her colleague receives outside is another matter. Signals emitted as she approaches the horizon reach him increasingly delayed, shifted to lower frequencies and fainter. You may have heard that you would see her frozen on the brink for ever. Don’t buy a season ticket. A signal dwindling beyond detection won’t provide you with a permanent, brightly lit last frame. Nor does the final bit of radio your equipment can pick up come with a chime announcing that the astronaut has crossed. Those are different events. That difference is what interested me in borrowing a very expensive piece of astrophysics for a question about a tea towel.

The last message you receive and the last thing that happened are frequently different things. In daily life the difference is easy to miss because you can usually settle it. Did the builder leave at four, or is four simply the time he sent the photograph of your extraordinarily large hole? Ring the builder. Ask the neighbour. Look at the mud in the hall. You can establish something that wasn’t in the original message. A material change in what you can know begins when the person you need can no longer answer. It does not follow that you become infallible about what their final silence means.

Consider the photographs people keep on their phones. A woman in a yellow cardigan is halfway through telling somebody off for taking her picture. It happens to be the last picture anybody took of her. After she dies, people searching for words for the service describe that expression as her characteristic wicked smile. In fact she had a mouthful of meringue and had specifically asked to be given a minute. There’s dignity in letting her be mildly annoyed about a meringue. Becoming the subject of a funeral shouldn’t retrospectively oblige a person to have been delivering a message about how to live.

Ellen knows more about the moment she didn’t answer than she has ever told her sister. She knows exactly what shabby little piece of revenge she enjoyed when she let it ring. It lasted only a few seconds, but time served is time served. Her private bargain has been to plead guilty to absolutely everything about that night, including the parts she has never established. In exchange she need never again stand face to face with a living witness and say what those few seconds were for.

A picture of the neighbourhood

In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration released its image of the compact object at the centre of the galaxy M87. There’s a bright, uneven ring around a dark centre. People sitting at their kitchen tables could see the result of radio observatories on different parts of the Earth working together to resolve a patch of sky much too small for any one of them to distinguish on its own. One of the better uses for a planet, frankly. [2]

The bright part is radiation from hot material around the black hole, mapped from radio observations into an image we can see. Gravity bends the paths of light. Some paths end in the hole. With suitable emitting material around it, this produces a central region of diminished brightness, the black hole’s shadow. Its apparent size is influenced by the bending of light. That inner darkness is therefore a poor place to stick a ruler and declare that you’ve directly measured the diameter of the horizon. The team compared the data with models of the emitting material and the geometry of spacetime. That’s how the interpretation does its work. Nobody persuaded a photon to pop back out and say which way up the furniture was.

What is striking here is a kind of intellectual good manners. The instruments have a limited view. The researchers still establish a great deal about what is there. Their achievement depends in part on being exact about which questions their evidence can answer. A boundary that prevents one kind of message leaves an astonishing amount to find out on the accessible side.

There’s a cheaper experiment available in Tess’s kitchen. Set the mushroom on a slight incline and it rolls until the flat side comes round. Repeated trials will tell you approximately which slope it can cope with before it tumbles anyway. You could write a small, somewhat lonely paper about it. The tests will tell you nothing about whether their mother privately preferred the wonky wooden bowl the other child made. You could, until quite recently in the history of the universe, have asked their mother. You can still ask the woman who made the flat side. You can tip the table. Three quite different questions, each requiring its own idea of where an answer might come from.

This is also where the astronomy must give up its visitor’s badge. A woman dying in a hospital is no astronaut crossing an event horizon. There is no radius in metres on either side of which a human conversation changes its status according to a law of gravity. The capacities of particular people alter, sometimes return, sometimes stop, sometimes much earlier than their relatives were prepared for. Some people leave letters. A witness can speak years later. There is no useful calculation in which you insert your mother’s mass and obtain the last Saturday on which you ought to have gone round. Anybody selling you that equation can pay for the kettle.

Death closes off the possibility of a new answer from that person. It doesn’t establish what the missed answer would have been. It certainly doesn’t compose the answer on their behalf. When somebody brightly tells you that your mother would have wanted you to be happy, you are entitled to remember that the last time the matter came up she wanted you to take a particular route to the airport and remained extraordinarily interested in which roundabout you used. You can love her. You can be happy. You can take the fucking bypass. None of this requires the dead to sign off on the itinerary.

Tess has put her cardigan over the back of a chair. It belonged to their mother. Ellen can remember her wearing it in the garden, tugging the sleeves down over her hands to pull dead nettles. It’s a tough, grey thing, halfway between an item of clothing and something you might issue a worker doing maintenance on a lighthouse. One pocket sags. Tess sticks her hand in the top and puts a fingertip out through the bottom.

“I’ve lost two pound coins and a front door key,” she says. “Still, I know which bit of the house I lost them in. The downstairs bit.”

She wants the mushroom so she can darn the pocket. She’s asked Ellen for a coin so they can check whether the repair holds. Fair enough, except Ellen can’t see why they need to do it now, why the mushroom has to come back today or indeed why Tess seems quite so comfortable wearing a dead woman’s cardigan. She has an urge to ask whether Tess is afraid she’s stretching the elbows. It strikes her in time that this is an odd welfare concern to express on behalf of a woman who has been cremated.

Who was on the other end

Tess threads the needle. Ellen turns the cardigan inside out. There’s a long pale seam where somebody repaired the same pocket before, and about half an inch at the end where the new tear has come away from the old stitching. The hole looked bigger with Tess’s finger coming through it. Most things do when you put a finger through them instead of finding a ruler.

“Did Mum want me to ring her, that Saturday?”

Tess looks up. “Which Saturday?”

The first injury to the version Ellen has rehearsed is that Tess apparently remembers several Saturdays.

“At the hospital. The last one. I saw you ringing.”

“Did you?”

“Was she asking for me?”

“She was asleep, El.”

“Did she wake up, though?”

“When I rang, she was asleep. I don’t know what happened every minute. I had to go and have a piss.”

Tess is pulling white cotton through the eye of a fairly thick needle. This seems to be taking an offensive amount of her attention.

“I thought she’d asked for me.”

“Why would you think that?”

Ellen describes the towel, the call and how long she could see the name on the screen before it went dark. She hears herself put particular emphasis on the length of time it rang, as if somewhere between six rings and eight rings was a reliable indicator of maternal involvement. Her sister listens with the needle held between her lips now, which is why she takes a while to answer.

“I rang because I wanted to hear your voice,” she says. “I’d just eaten a cheese sandwich off my knee. There was mustard inside the packet and I’d got it all down my skirt. I was sitting there trying to get the stain out with some of the water in Mum’s jug. I thought if I told you what I was doing, we might laugh.”

“Jesus, Tess.”

“I washed it. It was fine.”

They both know Ellen has no particular interest in the subsequent career of the skirt. She has a feeling close to relief and then an unpleasant suspicion that she’s taking the wrong relief. Her mother had been asleep. Three years of an unanswered last request begin, in a single instant, to lose their grip. Sitting in their place is a woman in a stained skirt who could have done with a laugh and did not get one.

There are people who would quite like the story to end here with a hug. There’s a decent case for it. One sister has been carrying an invented injury for three years, the other has just been able to lift it off her, and a hug costs both parties considerably less than professional upholstery cleaning. But there’s an actual injury left on the table. It belonged to Tess at the time and she hasn’t yet said what she makes of it.

“I saw it was you and I let it ring,” Ellen says. “I was still pissed off about the washing. I wanted you to have to deal with something yourself.”

“I was dealing with it myself.”

“Yes. I’m sorry.”

Tess considers this. “I was pissed off about the washing too. You said I always left things for you to do. I took her teeth home in a sandwich box that week. You try getting a bus with your mother’s teeth on your lap.”

Ellen remembers seeing the teeth on Tess’s draining board and is briefly visited by the possibility that the entire system of recording who did what, which she has carried around in her head at some expense, may require an external audit.

“I didn’t know what some of that was like for you,” Ellen says.

“You didn’t ask.”

There is a way to accept responsibility that is almost indistinguishable from trying to seize the microphone. Ellen could make a long speech about the kind of daughter she was, what other duties she performed, whether she has it in her to be a good sister and just how many hours of sleep and digestion she has already sacrificed in the interests of atonement. By the end of it Tess would be comforting her, which would put Ellen right back where she wanted to be that Saturday, letting somebody else do the difficult bit.

So she asks. “What was it like, sitting there?”

For one thing, says Tess, their mother snored. Ellen laughs in surprise. She has so successfully converted that hospital room into the solemn final scene of a life that it comes as a shock to remember the old girl could snore a teaspoon across a saucer. For another, there was no decent light to read by. Tess had a very good detective novel in her bag and every time a person came under suspicion she had to take him out into the corridor to get a proper look at him. Then there were the intervals between somebody coming round and somebody coming round again. “I’d do anything to get some of that time back now,” Tess says. “That evening I kept looking at the clock. I wanted it over. Both things are true.”

Ellen lets the woman who was in the room tell her what happened in the room. No astronomical discovery has been required. A substantial piece of what Ellen had treated as unknowable was available two bus stops away, and had been quietly leaking pound coins.

The difference between making good and making it unhappen

If somebody has died, the things you didn’t do assume a dreadful equality in hindsight. You didn’t visit on Tuesday. You didn’t ask about the man in the naval uniform. You didn’t sit up all night. You didn’t petition Parliament to repeal the underlying arrangement by which everything alive has to die. All four can feel like derelictions of quite similar severity around four in the morning. It helps, sometimes, to notice which of them were ever within the job description.

There are limits even to that exercise. Perhaps you did do something dreadful. You sent the letter. You took the money. You told somebody you were ashamed of them, and the chance to sit opposite that particular face and take those words back has gone. There’s a popular consolation to the effect that we all did the best we could with what we knew. Some of us, some of the time, did a comprehensively mediocre job with information quite sufficient for better. Ellen knew the number on her phone. Giving yourself a flattering performance review is an odd condition to place on your willingness to get up again the following morning.

What can responsibility mean when you can no longer make restitution to the person you hurt? It can mean giving somebody what belongs to them. If you took the money and the person who should have had it is dead, there may be an estate, a child who was short of something, an account you can put right. It can mean telling the truth to the other people who have had to work around your version of events. It can mean letting a niece know that the photograph of her father she grew up hearing a terrible story about was taken on a day when you were angry and got the story wrong. These acts change what happens to living people. Some of them are inconvenient, unflattering and expensive, which can be one way of telling them apart from a fascinating afternoon spent privately deploring yourself.

Sometimes there is no appropriate recipient. Marching round to tell a bereaved husband exactly how badly you once behaved towards his wife may largely solve the problem of you having to carry that information alone. He’s entitled to wonder who invited him to carry the other end. An apology can be an offer that somebody is free to refuse. A confession requiring a distressed person to listen until you feel clean is closer to a hostage situation with tasteful biscuits.

Ellen can’t compensate Tess for that hour in the hospital. Sunday afternoon in the kitchen doesn’t purchase it back. It matters that she can hear this story now because Tess is still here, and because something has changed between them in the last fifteen minutes. It would be a shame to miss that exchange because they were both busy pretending it was a refund.

Consider a rather different piece of news that arrived after the events responsible for it. On 14 September 2015 two LIGO detectors in the United States recorded a passing gravitational-wave signal. Its frequency and amplitude rose, peaked and died away. Comparing the signal with relativistic models, the collaboration found it consistent with two black holes spiralling together and merging into a larger one. The event took place long before the two American instruments, their designers or their respective canteens existed. What became available in 2015 was evidence of the event. [3]

The signal was a disturbance in spacetime associated with the changing gravitational field of the system. It wasn’t a transmission from an interior observer who had finally badgered technical support into fixing the radio. Here are two claims you can hold comfortably at the same time. No new light signal can come out from inside a classical event horizon to an observer far away. The exterior dynamics of black holes can leave measurable evidence at an enormous distance. One real limit leaves plenty of room for one real discovery. In fact, had everybody treated the first fact as grounds to go home, we’d have missed quite a noise.

Why take another detour into physics in the middle of two women mending a cardigan? Because there’s a particular abuse of the word impossible that a physicist would lose a week’s sleep over and the rest of us slip into before breakfast. It’s impossible to change Saturday night. Therefore it’s impossible to find out anything about Saturday night. Therefore it’s impossible to change what Saturday night does to the people who lived through it. Three sentences. Only the first one follows from the fact the evening is over. The others require separate investigations, one of which has just taken place in front of a perfectly ordinary sewing tin.

The distinction can run the other way. A real signal can be too weak for your instrument. A willing witness may genuinely have forgotten. Tess can recall the mustard because she spent a surprising amount of time trying to remove the stuff. She can’t remember the last complete sentence their mother spoke. Ellen wants to ask again, with a different wording perhaps, as if Tess had access to an advanced search function she was reluctant to deploy for family. “Was it something about going home?” she starts to say. Then she stops. Put a good enough candidate in the question and you may be pleased with the answer for all the wrong reasons.

They can still speak about their mother. They can still fall out about their mother. If they agree in every detail about their mother it will be the first occasion the woman has ever managed to bring that about and a pity she won’t be here to see it. The dead have an inconvenient right to some untidiness. Somebody you live with for forty years has enough time to love you, let you down, get much better at loving you, and still, on occasion, scrape burnt toast straight into the butter.

An appropriate use for a dead woman’s cardigan

When does letting go start? Ellen would have assumed it involved a wardrobe. You selected an item of clothing, pressed it to your face for a suitable interval, then closed the charity bag to the sound of tasteful strings. She knows a woman who cleared her dad’s shirts the day after his funeral because she needed the space for a lodger. She knows another who works in her husband’s shed fifteen years after his death and can put her hand on any tool by reaching to where he put it. Neither woman can tell Ellen what to do with a cardigan. Asking them to justify the interval is a little like asking two cooks how old their saucepan is before you decide whether the soup tastes good.

Tess has put on the cardigan because she gets cold. She likes where the cuffs come on her wrists. The wool at the elbows is shiny and if they go through she intends to put leather patches on them, something their mother considered suitable only for men who went to folk clubs. Ellen says Mum would have made a remark about it. Tess agrees. She misses the remark. It would never have occurred to her, while their mother was alive, to keep the remarks and lose the veto.

What you do with somebody’s things can have practical consequences. Check whether your brother wants the suitcase before you give it away. Find out whose name is on the car. There may be a neighbour who has spent the better part of a decade waiting, with admirable restraint, for the return of a decent stepladder. Once you’ve done the obtainable work, you remain free to wear the cardigan, give it away, or put it in the drawer and revisit the question in spring. It will readily bear all three interpretations of your love. It is less tolerant of a hot wash.

Tess slips the mushroom under the damaged patch. Ellen takes the first few stitches. It’s an ordinary task with an advantage some of the grander efforts at repair lack. The woman whose pocket it is can tell her, in real time, to ease off a bit because she’s sewing the two sides together. One can just about imagine a self-help book in which nobody ever says anything quite that useful.

“Do you want to know why I never rang you back that night?” Ellen asks.

“I think you’ve told me.”

“I have more of an explanation.”

“Have you got a better one?”

“No.”

“Then you can hold the wool.”

They sit like that, Ellen letting out a length of yarn as her sister needs it. In the long run this is unlikely to qualify as a turning point in anybody’s official biography. Tess has to unpick a few of Ellen’s stitches. Ellen spots a thin place near the top and does a decent job of catching it before it goes. Neither sister acquires a new purpose. Nothing in the sky rearranges itself to indicate approval. Years ago somebody taught an eleven-year-old how to turn down a piece of wood on a lathe. Her mother kept the result. Two people who used to fight about whose turn it was have found themselves holding opposite ends of the same bit of wool.

Ellen takes out the pound coin. Tess puts on the cardigan, slips the coin into the pocket and stands on her toes. Her sister’s listening before she realises she’s listening. For the first time all afternoon she has a question that can be asked of exactly the person available to answer it, with a simple test and a reasonable expectation of results.

Tess jumps. Nothing hits the floor. She jumps again, harder.
