Chapter 17Black Holes, Death, and the Art of Letting Go

“Put it on its side and take your thumb away,” says Tess. “See? That’s what the flat spot is for.”

Ellen turns the wooden mushroom over. The patch on the side of its cap is roughly the shape of a thumbnail.

“Did Grandpa show you that?”

“I made the damn thing.”

The younger sibling has a pleasure all her own in being able to produce, after thirty-two years, documentary evidence that the older sibling was paying no attention whatsoever. Tess is forty-three, does payroll for a roofing company and intends to enjoy hers. She made the mushroom in shop class when she was eleven. Somebody else in the class made a bowl with a hole right through the bottom. The teacher displayed both. Their mother used the mushroom for years and at no point in this long and fruitful association did the mushroom once piss soup all over a tablecloth.

“Do you want it back?” says Ellen.

“Yes. And bring some change. I need a quarter.”

“What for?”

“Something very exciting involving a pocket.”

Their mother has been dead for three years. During that time Ellen has disposed of a refrigerator, canceled a phone line, given a coffee can of foreign coins to a thrift store and received four letters addressed to The Estate of, each offering to renew her roadside assistance. The insurer appears to have a better understanding of her mother’s present circumstances than of its own limitations as a service provider.

Some objects Ellen could get rid of because everybody agreed they were objects. Others have acquired the status of witnesses. Under the cap of the darning mushroom is a dent the shape of a grain of rice. She has owned it for three years without taking a scrap of interest in that dent. Now, for the price of a short phone call, she knows who made the mushroom and how it got the flat side, and suddenly she’d like to know what made the dent. By lunchtime they’ve set the thing up on a pair of thread spools in Tess’s kitchen. Tess is examining it with the little magnifying glass their mother used for threading needles. Ellen has written it a label.

Tess remembers the sanding. She remembers their mother making an appreciative noise and immediately producing a sock so disgusting that the eleven-year-old Tess briefly reconsidered her position on homemade presents. She has no idea about the dent. Maybe their mother dropped it. Maybe one of them used it to hit something. There were long periods of their childhood when the availability of a wooden weapon and the proximity of a sister would have been enough to explain a dent.

“You should have asked her,” Tess says.

It’s an ordinary remark. It takes Ellen all the way back to her own kitchen three years ago, a dish towel folded in quarters on the dish rack and her sister’s name lighting up her phone. She had been on the evening visits at the hospital all week. On Friday she and Tess had had a fight about whose turn it was to deal with a bag of wet clothes. There had been forceful statements involving a calendar and an allocation of labor. Their mother had slept through the best of it and would have been sorry to miss two daughters prepared to come to blows over the right not to do the laundry. When Tess called on Saturday, Ellen saw the name, thought You can do one thing without me, and carried on folding the dish towel.

Their mother died that night. For three years Ellen has been fairly certain she missed the last occasion on which her mother wanted to speak to her. The call lasted long enough for the towel to go into the drawer. Its little red record sat on her phone for weeks. There’s a question she has never asked Tess, a question much closer to hand than the one about the dent.

Where a message can come from

Before she asks it, consider a place no answer comes back from.

An astronaut is falling freely toward a very large black hole. The size matters. Near a small one, the difference between the pull on her feet and the pull on her head would stretch her like spaghetti before she reached the horizon. At the horizon of a very large one the stretch is gentle enough for her to keep talking. Give her a watch, a radio and a legitimate grievance about the terms of her employment. Far away, a colleague waits for news. While she’s outside the event horizon she can in principle send an outward signal that reaches him. After she crosses, she can carry on talking into the microphone. Her colleague, however good his antenna and however long his grant is renewed, can’t receive a new message she sends from inside. In the classical picture of an isolated black hole, the event horizon is the boundary that marks that distinction. From inside, even a light signal aimed outward can’t get away to a distant observer.[1]

This isn’t an obstacle like a steel wall. Somebody can run a cable through a steel wall eventually, provided you’ve filled out the form requesting a consultation about the proposed hole. An event horizon changes what destinations a signal can have. Light still travels locally at the speed of light. The astronaut can shine a flashlight across her cabin. It’s the route from that event in the cabin to the distant colleague that no longer exists. Turning up the brightness would be a beautifully expensive way of misunderstanding the problem.

For its mass, a black hole is a surprisingly small target. For an ideal black hole with no spin or electric charge, the horizon’s radius is twice the gravitational constant times the mass, divided by the speed of light squared. At one solar mass the answer is roughly three kilometers. Scale the mass and you scale that radius by the same factor, so ten solar masses gives roughly thirty kilometers, a horizon about sixty kilometers across. That figure belongs to a particular model. Real astronomical black holes can spin, and spin shrinks the horizon: at the theoretical maximum, its radius is half the figure above.[2]

The astronaut’s own watch can reach the crossing in a finite time. Signals emitted as she approaches the horizon reach her colleague increasingly delayed, shifted to lower frequencies and fainter. You may have heard that you would see her frozen on the brink forever. Don’t buy a season ticket. A signal dwindling beyond detection won’t provide you with a permanent, brightly lit last frame. Nor does the last scrap of radio your equipment can pick up come with a chime announcing that the astronaut has crossed. Those are different events. That difference is what interested me in borrowing a very expensive piece of astrophysics for a question about a dish towel. The last message you receive and the last thing that happened are frequently different things.

Consider the photographs people keep on their phones. A woman in a yellow cardigan is halfway through chewing somebody out for taking her picture. It happens to be the last picture anybody took of her. After she dies, people searching for words for the service describe that expression as her characteristic wicked smile. In fact she had a mouthful of meringue and had specifically asked to be given a minute. There’s dignity in letting her be mildly annoyed about a meringue. Becoming the subject of a funeral shouldn’t retrospectively oblige a person to have been delivering a message about how to live.

Ellen knows more about the moment she didn’t answer than she has ever told her sister. She knows exactly what shabby little piece of revenge she enjoyed when she let it ring. It lasted only a few seconds, but guilt doesn’t come pro rata. Her private bargain has been to plead guilty to everything about that night, including the parts she has never established. In exchange she never again has to stand face to face with a living witness and say what those few seconds were for.

A picture of the neighborhood

In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration released its image of the object at the center of the galaxy M87, a bright, uneven ring around a dark center. The dark part is a shadow shaped by bent light, and the team was exact about what the picture could support, which was a comparison with models and not a ruler laid across the horizon.[3]

Tess’s kitchen offers a cheaper experiment. Set the mushroom on a slight incline and it rolls until the flat side comes around. You could write a small, somewhat lonely paper about it. The paper will tell you nothing about whether their mother privately preferred the lopsided wooden bowl the other child made. You could, until quite recently in the history of the universe, have asked their mother. You can still ask the woman who made the flat side. You can tip the table. Three different questions, each requiring its own idea of where an answer might come from.

A woman dying in a hospital is no astronaut crossing an event horizon. No radius in meters divides a human conversation into before and after according to a law of gravity. The capacities of particular people alter, sometimes return, sometimes stop, sometimes much earlier than their relatives were prepared for. Some people leave letters. A witness can speak years later. No useful calculation lets you insert your mother’s mass and obtain the last Saturday on which you ought to have gone over.

Death closes off the possibility of a new answer from that person. It doesn’t establish what the missed answer would have been. It certainly doesn’t compose the answer on their behalf. When somebody brightly tells you that your mother would have wanted you to be happy, you’re entitled to remember that the last time the matter came up she wanted you to take a particular route to the airport and remained extraordinarily interested in which exit you took. You can love her. You can be happy. You can take the toll road. None of this requires the dead to sign off on the itinerary.

Tess has put her cardigan over the back of a chair. It belonged to their mother. Ellen can remember her wearing it in the backyard, tugging the sleeves down over her hands to pull thistles. It’s a tough, gray thing, halfway between an item of clothing and something you might issue a worker doing maintenance on a lighthouse. One pocket sags. Tess sticks her hand in the top and puts a fingertip out through the bottom.

“I’ve lost two quarters and a house key,” she says. “Still, I’ve narrowed down where I lost them. Below the pocket.”

She wants the mushroom so she can darn the pocket. She’s asked Ellen for a coin so they can check whether the repair holds. Fair enough, except Ellen can’t see why they need to do it now, why the mushroom has to come back today or why Tess seems so comfortable wearing a dead woman’s cardigan. She has an urge to ask whether Tess is afraid she’s stretching the elbows. It strikes her in time that this is an odd welfare concern to express on behalf of a woman who has been cremated.

Who was on the other end

Tess threads the needle. Ellen turns the cardigan inside out. There’s a long pale seam where somebody repaired the same pocket before, and about half an inch at the end where the new tear has come away from the old stitching. The hole looked bigger with Tess’s finger coming through it. Most things do when you put a finger through them instead of finding a ruler.

“Did Mom want me to call her, that Saturday?”

Tess looks up. “Which Saturday?”

The first injury to the version Ellen has rehearsed is that Tess apparently remembers several Saturdays.

“At the hospital. The last one. I saw you calling.”

“Did you?”

“Was she asking for me?”

“She was asleep, El.”

“Did she wake up, though?”

“When I called, she was asleep. I don’t know what happened every minute. I had to go take a piss.”

Tess is pulling gray yarn through the eye of a fairly thick needle. This seems to be taking an offensive amount of her attention.

“I thought she’d asked for me.”

“Why would you think that?”

Ellen describes the towel, the call and how long she could see the name on the screen before it went dark. She hears herself put particular emphasis on the length of time it rang, as if somewhere between six rings and eight rings was a reliable indicator of maternal involvement. Her sister listens with the needle held between her lips now, which is why she takes a while to answer.

“I called because I wanted to hear your voice,” she says. “I’d just eaten a cheese sandwich off my lap. There was mustard inside the wrapper and I’d gotten it all down my skirt. I was sitting there trying to get the stain out with some of the water in Mom’s pitcher. I thought if I told you what I was doing, we might laugh.”

“Jesus, Tess.”

“I washed it. It was fine.”

They both know Ellen has no particular interest in the subsequent career of the skirt. She has a feeling close to relief and then an unpleasant suspicion that she’s taking the wrong relief. Her mother had been asleep. Three years of an unanswered last request begin to lose their grip. Sitting in their place is a woman in a stained skirt who could have used a laugh and didn’t get one.

The story could end here with a hug. One sister has carried an invented injury for three years and the other has just lifted it off her. But an actual injury is left on the table. It belonged to Tess at the time and she hasn’t yet said what she makes of it.

“I saw it was you and I let it ring,” Ellen says. “I was still pissed off about the laundry. I wanted you to have to deal with something yourself.”

“I was dealing with it myself.”

“Yes. I’m sorry.”

Tess considers this. “I was pissed off about the laundry too. You said I always left things for you to do. My car was in the shop that week and I took her teeth home in a lunchbox. You try taking a bus with your mother’s teeth on your lap.”

Ellen remembers seeing the teeth on Tess’s dish rack and is briefly visited by the possibility that the entire system of recording who did what, which she has carried around in her head at some expense, may require an external audit.

“I didn’t know what some of that was like for you,” Ellen says.

“You didn’t ask.”

One way of accepting responsibility is almost indistinguishable from trying to seize the microphone. Ellen could make a long speech about the kind of daughter she was, what other duties she performed, whether she has it in her to be a good sister and just how many hours of sleep and digestion she has already sacrificed in the interests of atonement. By the end of it Tess would be comforting her, which would put Ellen right back where she wanted to be that Saturday, letting somebody else do the hard part.

So she asks. “What was it like, sitting there?”

For one thing, says Tess, their mother snored. Ellen laughs in surprise. She has so successfully converted that hospital room into the solemn final scene of a life that it comes as a shock to remember the woman could snore a teaspoon across a saucer. For another, there was no decent light to read by. Tess had a very good detective novel in her bag and every time a person came under suspicion she had to take him out into the hallway to get a good look at him. Then there were the intervals between somebody coming by and somebody coming by again. “I’d do anything to get some of that time back now,” Tess says. “That evening I kept looking at the clock. I wanted it over. Both things are true.”

Ellen lets the woman who was in the room tell her what happened in the room. No astronomical discovery has been required. A substantial piece of what Ellen had treated as unknowable was available two bus stops away, and had been quietly leaking quarters.

The difference between making good and making it unhappen

If somebody has died, the things you didn’t do assume a terrible equality in hindsight. You didn’t visit on Tuesday. You didn’t ask about the man in the Navy uniform. You didn’t sit up all night. You didn’t petition Congress to repeal the underlying arrangement by which everything alive has to die. All four can feel like derelictions of similar severity around four in the morning. It helps, sometimes, to notice which of them were ever within the job description.

A popular consolation says we all did the best we could with what we knew. Some of us, some of the time, did a thoroughly mediocre job with all the information we needed to do better. Ellen knew the number on her phone. Giving yourself a flattering performance review is an odd condition to place on your willingness to get up again the following morning.

When the person you hurt is past repaying, responsibility can still mean giving the living what belongs to them, and telling the truth to the people who have had to work around your version of events. Those acts tend to be inconvenient, unflattering and expensive, which is one way of telling them apart from a fascinating afternoon spent privately deploring yourself.

Sometimes there’s no appropriate recipient. Marching over to tell a bereaved husband exactly how badly you once behaved toward his wife may largely solve the problem of you having to carry that information alone. He’s entitled to wonder who invited him to carry the other end. An apology can be an offer that somebody is free to refuse. A confession requiring a distressed person to listen until you feel clean is closer to a hostage situation with tasteful cookies.

Ellen can’t compensate Tess for that hour in the hospital. Sunday afternoon in the kitchen doesn’t purchase it back. It matters that she can hear this story now because Tess is still here, and because something has changed between them in the last fifteen minutes. It would be a shame to miss that exchange because they were both busy pretending it was a refund.

Some news arrives long after the events responsible for it. On September 14, 2015, the two LIGO detectors recorded a gravitational wave that rose in pitch, peaked and died away. The collaboration found it consistent with two black holes spiraling together and merging, long before the detectors, their designers or their cafeterias existed.[4] Nothing had come out from inside a horizon. What the two holes did to the space around them left evidence at an enormous distance all the same. Had everybody treated the first fact as grounds to go home, we’d have missed quite a noise.

A physicist would lose a week’s sleep over a particular abuse of the word impossible that the rest of us slip into before breakfast. It’s impossible to change Saturday night. Therefore it’s impossible to find out anything about Saturday night. Therefore it’s impossible to change what Saturday night does to the people who lived through it. Three sentences. Only the first one follows from the fact the evening is over. The others require separate investigations, one of which has just taken place in front of a cookie tin full of thread.

The distinction can run the other way. A real signal can be too weak for your instrument. A willing witness may genuinely have forgotten. Tess can recall the mustard because she spent a surprising amount of time trying to remove the stuff. She can’t remember the last complete sentence their mother spoke. Ellen wants to ask again, as if Tess had access to an advanced search function she was reluctant to deploy for family. “Was it something about going home?” she starts to say. Then she stops. Put a good enough candidate in the question and you may be pleased with the answer for all the wrong reasons.

They can still speak about their mother. They can still fight about their mother. If they agree in every detail about their mother it will be the first occasion the woman has ever managed to bring that about and a pity she won’t be here to see it. The dead have an inconvenient right to some untidiness. Somebody you live with for forty years has enough time to love you, let you down, get much better at loving you, and still, on occasion, scrape burnt toast straight into the butter.

An appropriate use for a dead woman’s cardigan

When does letting go start? Ellen would have assumed it involved a closet. You selected an item of clothing, pressed it to your face for a suitable interval, then closed the donation bag to the sound of tasteful strings. She knows a woman who cleared out her dad’s shirts the day after his funeral because she needed the space for a tenant. She knows another who works in her husband’s workshop fifteen years after his death and can put her hand on any tool by reaching to where he put it. Neither woman can tell Ellen what to do with a cardigan.

Tess wears the cardigan because she gets cold. She likes where the cuffs come on her wrists. The wool at the elbows is shiny and if they go through she intends to put leather patches on them, something their mother considered suitable only for men who owned a banjo. Ellen says Mom would have made a remark about it. Tess agrees. She misses the remark. It would never have occurred to her, while their mother was alive, to keep the remarks and lose the veto. The cardigan will bear being worn, given away or put in a drawer until spring. It’s less tolerant of the hot cycle.

Tess slips the mushroom under the damaged patch. Ellen takes the first few stitches. It’s an ordinary task with an advantage some of the grander efforts at repair lack. The woman whose pocket it is can tell her, in real time, to ease off a little because she’s sewing the two sides together. It’s hard to imagine a self-help book that says anything that useful.

“Do you want to know why I never called you back that night?” Ellen asks.

“I think you’ve told me.”

“I have more of an explanation.”

“Do you have a better one?”

“No.”

“Then you can hold the yarn.”

They sit like that, Ellen letting out a length of yarn as her sister needs it. In the long run this is unlikely to qualify as a turning point in anybody’s official biography. Tess has to pick out a few of Ellen’s stitches. Ellen spots a thin place near the top and does a decent job of catching it before it goes. Neither sister acquires a new purpose. Nothing in the sky rearranges itself to indicate approval. Years ago somebody taught an eleven-year-old how to turn down a piece of wood on a lathe. Her mother kept the result. Two people who used to fight about whose turn it was have found themselves holding opposite ends of the same piece of yarn.

Ellen takes out the quarter. Tess puts on the cardigan, slips the coin into the pocket and stands on her toes. Ellen’s listening before she realizes she’s listening. For the first time all afternoon she has a question that can be asked of exactly the person available to answer it, with a simple test and a reasonable expectation of results.

Tess jumps. Nothing hits the floor. She jumps again, harder.
