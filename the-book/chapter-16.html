Chapter 16The Opportunity Cost of Being Everyone

On Thursday morning Ellen offered a woman forty quid to move Saturday.

The woman ran a small letterpress shop. She was retiring. A buyer was collecting the press on Monday, and Saturday would be the last afternoon anybody could come in, set a line of type and print it. The woman listened with the great patience of somebody who had spent forty years spelling people’s surnames back to them. For forty quid, Ellen could have a drawer to keep things in. Saturday was going to stay where it was.

Ellen thanked her and hung up. She had wanted to go with her friend Liv, who first took her there at nineteen. Liv had already picked out a line to print. She wouldn’t tell Ellen what it was.

At precisely the same time on precisely that Saturday, a museum would like to pay Ellen to draw a potato masher. This was a considerably better offer than it sounds. Ellen made technical drawings for a living. On most days her great professional achievement was that a stranger could identify the underside of a valve without ever having to ask who drew it. She had proposed a family afternoon where a child could stick a hand inside a bag, feel some mysterious domestic object and describe it to an illustrator who couldn’t see it either. The illustrator had to draw exactly what the child described. Then everybody got to see what an absolute bastard language is.

The museum had spent eleven months considering this proposal. For most of those months, Ellen would happily have done it on any Saturday in the English calendar, including Christmas if somebody could rustle up a child. Now a cancellation had freed up a room. They had a budget. They even had a potato masher. Could she let them know by noon?

At eleven she had two maps open on her kitchen table. At eleven fifteen she had discovered a bus that could take her from the museum to the print shop in twenty-six minutes. All she had to do was leave a roomful of children with their hands in bags, abandon Liv halfway through setting the type, and catch the same bus backwards in time. She put a shoe on each map to stop the edges curling and made some tea.

If you’ve ever gone looking for a timetable to solve what is fundamentally a problem with how many bodies you have, come in. There’s tea.

The man who didn’t sell a pair of shoes

In 1850 Frédéric Bastiat published a little scene about a broken window. A boy breaks a pane. His father has to spend six francs on a glazier. The onlookers find a consolation. The glazier gets work. Those six francs will circulate. Perhaps a child with a brick and an adequate supply of windows could get the whole French economy back on its feet.

Bastiat invites one more person into the scene. Before his boy intervened in economic policy, the father might have spent those six francs on shoes, or perhaps a book. The glazier standing at the house is perfectly visible. The shoemaker who never gets the order has no reason to come round and stand in the street looking disappointed. After the repair the father has a window. Had it stayed intact he might have had a window and shoes. The injury has provided a job. Describing the job tells us remarkably little about whether the injury was good news. [1]

I like the shoemaker because he doesn’t know he’s in the story. He gets up, opens his shutters, gets on with his day and never hears about the six francs. It takes an effort of imagination to make him visible. It takes a different effort to stop at one shoemaker. Once you start picturing what else those francs could have bought, France contains a hell of a lot of shops.

That stopping point matters. The opportunity cost of using a resource one way is the value of the best feasible alternative you give up. If you buy the shoes, you may be giving up the book you would otherwise have bought. You haven’t also been deprived of six francs’ worth of sausages, six francs’ worth of candles and a thrilling evening with six francs’ worth of brandy. Your capacity to picture all three is a fine thing. The shopkeepers would still prefer that you pay them.

Money has the useful property that the till can tell you when you’re trying this shit. Time is much more polite. Sit on a train looking at somebody else’s wedding pictures and in the space of four stops you can feel you have forfeited a marriage in Tuscany, a life alone in a clean flat, three children who speak fluent Italian and the glorious sexual freedom of never having had children. That’s an awful lot to lose on a single fare. Ask which one of those lives you could actually have chosen on the day in question and the carriage gets quieter.

Ellen, for instance, can reasonably picture the print shop. Liv has a place for her. It is possible to get there, possible to pay for it, possible to spend the afternoon standing at the case with a composing stick in her hand. Ellen, aged nineteen, starting a printing apprenticeship and discovering she has a gift that makes her the subject of a small but much loved documentary on public television, will have to make her own travel arrangements. No one offered that woman a place on Saturday.

It’s worth keeping the real offer in view for a minute. Liv moved away several years ago. She and Ellen still send each other pictures of hideous lettering. To their friendship, a café advertising FRESHLY GROUN DCOFFEE is as good as a bunch of roses. Getting four hours together at a bench, with no obligation to make the four hours interesting, happens maybe once a year. They remember going to the print shop as girls and finding their names in the visitors’ book afterwards. Somebody had put an ornamental flourish under Liv. Ellen, who’d just been dumped, wrote her name as if she intended to press charges. The book might still be there. They could look for it. This is the life Ellen stands to miss, and it has a smell, a person and an address.

A hundred and ninety pounds

The museum fee is £160. Take out £10 for the return journey and doing the session leaves Ellen £150 up. The workshop charges £35 for paper, ink and the afternoon at the press. The return bus fare is £5. That afternoon leaves her £40 down. Choose the print shop and by bedtime her bank balance is £190 lower than if she’d done the museum. A hundred and fifty she could have earned plus forty she would have spent. Both trips take the same chunk of Saturday. Assume for the moment there are no other costs and we’re looking at cash before tax.

That’s quite a different question from “Would you pay thirty-five pounds to print a page with Liv?” At thirty-five, yes, easily. At a hundred and ninety she would at least like to know what the bloody page says.

The extra money isn’t another charge from the printer. She doesn’t owe a business £150 for the privilege of staying out of it. It’s the difference between two available afternoons. Price the visit against a free day at home and you get £40. Price it against the museum and you get £190. On a Sunday with no booking the figure may change back to forty. The quality of the friend has held up remarkably well through these fluctuations.

In a study published in 2009, Shane Frederick and colleagues asked people to imagine coming across a video they wanted for $14.99. Some could choose to buy it or decline. Others saw the same choice with a few more words explaining that declining would leave the $14.99 available for other purchases. Fewer people chose the video with that wording. There was no additional money in the second version. There wasn’t even an actual video. A small change in what was made visible affected how people answered a hypothetical shopping question. [2]

Try a version that goes the other way. A friend asks you for an hour to help carry a wardrobe upstairs. You look at an otherwise blank evening and agree. Later you discover the cost of that hour was the only uninterrupted bath you were likely to get that week. It’s perfectly possible you would still have carried the wardrobe. It’s also perfectly possible you spend the following forty minutes resenting a person who never had the opportunity to compete with the bath, because you yourself only found out about it while wedged under the bastard wardrobe on the turn of the stairs.

One reason to name the alternative out loud is that it can change the arrangement. “I need the evening, but I can give you half an hour at six if you can get your neighbour to take the other end” is a functional offer. “No problem, whatever you need” followed by an evening of carrying oak furniture with the expression of a recently deposed king is a surprisingly common basis for friendship.

Another reason is that sometimes the number decides it. Suppose Ellen needs £120 by Monday to keep the electricity on. Under those conditions a comparison of artistic fulfillment may be of limited interest to Ellen and of no interest whatever to the meter. It is easy to give another person a stirring speech about what they will remember on their deathbed when you don’t have to live in their flat on the intervening Tuesday. A shortage of money can settle an argument between two genuine desires without settling which desire the person holds more dear. If Ellen takes the work, the transaction records what she did. Be careful what kind of biography you write from her bank statement.

As it happens, this month she can afford either answer. She can replace the cash over a few weeks. Liv has offered to pay for her if things are tight. Ellen turns that down because it wouldn’t fix the problem. The museum has asked her for something she wants to do. It would be almost a relief if the director were a prick. She’s spoken to him twice in eleven months and both times he has been thoughtful, keen and irritatingly specific about paying her.

At half past eleven Liv rings.

“Do you remember what she used to clean the press with?” Liv says.

“No.”

“Neither do I. I’m sitting here trying to remember a smell. Are you coming?”

Ellen starts to tell her how difficult it is to earn a living drawing things for people. This is a thoroughly researched subject. She has an audience of one, and unfortunately the audience has already offered to buy the ticket.

“I think I want to do the museum,” she says eventually.

“Oh,” says Liv. There’s a little space before she goes on. “Then I’ll ask my brother. He can drive. We can bring home a drawer.”

“Are you cross?”

“I wanted you to come, El. Those are different questions.”

After they hang up Ellen keeps her phone in her hand. If Liv had been cross she could have spent the rest of the morning taking a principled stand against emotional blackmail. Now she has to sit in her kitchen with the rather less bracing news that somebody would have liked her company. It’s a lovely thing to be wanted. There is no rule that says it will feel lovely on the day.

The Thursday you can still do something about

At eleven forty Ellen calls the museum. Before she accepts, she asks whether they have any other dates. This sounds like the kind of boring practical detail you might leave out of a serious philosophical investigation. Leave it out and you can spend the rest of your life bravely accepting the inevitability of a scheduling conflict that Janet in bookings could have moved to October.

There is no other date. The room belongs to the ceramics people the following weekend. After that the building work begins and they’re going to take the roof off. Ellen says yes to Saturday and asks them to put her name on the poster. Within ten minutes Liv’s brother has the workshop place. Half an hour later the museum emails a poster on which Ellen is a local illusionist. She considers leaving it. There may be a better living in making a potato masher disappear.

There are occasions when you should wait before choosing. The distinction is almost insultingly practical. What might you learn in the time you buy? If the museum can hold the booking for twenty minutes while the printer checks for a cancellation on Sunday, twenty minutes buys a chance of getting both. If Ellen waits twenty minutes after both people have told her the dates are fixed, the only likely new information is that twenty minutes have gone by. Meanwhile Liv’s brother is waiting to find out whether he should put petrol in the car.

Flexibility has value, and somebody generally pays to provide it. A returnable train ticket costs more because you can do something with it that you can’t do with the cheap one. The friend who keeps their diary clear in case you decide you’re free has granted you a similar option. The fact that your friend hasn’t invoiced you is among the better features of the friendship. It doesn’t make their evening a public utility. Keep saying “Let’s see how we feel” and eventually you may discover that what they feel like doing is asking somebody else.

Even a free option can change the experience of what you choose. In an experiment by Daniel Gilbert and Jane Ebert, photography students made prints of their own photographs and had to select one to keep. Some could subsequently exchange their print. Others had to live with their first choice. When the researchers asked later how much the students liked the pictures, the group who could change their minds liked the one they’d kept less than the group whose choice was final. Students hadn’t expected the exchange arrangement to make this difference. [3]

That was a study involving photographs. Please resist the urge to apply it straight to divorce law. A bad landlord would love to hear that your inability to get out of your lease is an exciting opportunity to appreciate the damp. A return clause can protect you from real defects that only emerge later. But there’s an insight here for a person who has actually chosen a picture. For as long as you can take it back, looking at it can remain a form of shopping. At some point you might like to find out what it’s like to hang the thing on the wall.

On Friday Ellen gets the bags ready. She has a tea strainer, a potato masher, a pastry brush and a wooden darning mushroom that belonged to her mother. It’s a squat little knob on a stick. You put it inside a sock so you can pull the hole flat enough to mend. Held the wrong way it looks like a judge’s gavel from a country that takes a fairly relaxed attitude to crime.

She nearly leaves the mushroom at home. Everything else she can replace. Then she remembers her mother’s considered opinion of people who kept the good towels for visitors, which was that there must be some absolutely fucking filthy visitors out there if they needed that many towels. She slips the mushroom into a bag and ties the cord loosely around the top.

The museum of what might have been

At one on Saturday a small boy puts his hand in the tea-strainer bag. He describes a bowl with lots of little holes where somebody forgot to put the bottom in. He feels the handle. There’s a tail coming out of its head, he tells Ellen. She asks him how big the head is. Big enough for a mouse, he says. The audience watches a helmet take shape. On a point of personal privilege, Ellen draws in the mouse.

The boy takes the strainer out. He is faintly disappointed. An adult would now say something gracious about what an interesting mistake we all made together. The boy asks whether they have anything else in there that could kill a knight.

At half past one she looks up to find three empty seats. She ought to be looking for a bigger sheet of paper. Instead she sees Liv at the type case, tilting the little blocks of metal to read them upside down. When Ellen was nineteen she got ink on the side of her face and went the rest of the day waiting for some mysterious new sexual confidence to explain why all the men at the bus stop were looking at her. She gets to enjoy that memory for several seconds before a man in front of her asks if she’s allowed to draw a meat tenderiser in a public building.

This is where a tidy version of the story gives Ellen a sign. A child tells her she wants to become an artist. A newspaper photographs the mouse. The museum director approaches carrying a permanent job and a potato masher engraved To the only woman who chose correctly. Nobody does. A table leg wobbles. A father tries to get his daughter to say perforated although holey has done perfectly well all afternoon. Ellen loses the point off her best pencil. Over in the print shop, at least in her imagination, the pencils are terrific.

There’s a dirty trick built into the comparison. She has access to the entire museum afternoon, including its plumbing and the bit where she eats a squashed sandwich behind a fire door. Her visit to the print shop is being programmed by a woman with great affection for the subject and a running time of about ninety seconds. This is how an imaginary life gets a reputation for containing no boring bits. You can run that little film the other way round. Put Ellen at the press sorting a tray of letters somebody has dropped, show her one image of a delighted child looking at a drawing of a mouse, and the museum will acquire an almost unbearable lustre. The missing life has been relieved of the duty to fill the other three hours and fifty-eight minutes.

It would be just as dishonest to protect this Saturday by inventing a bad afternoon for Liv. The woman who runs the shop might be a wonderful teacher. There might be sausages. Liv might laugh so hard she has to put the ink roller down. If Ellen needs her friend to have had a slightly shit time in order to enjoy how she spent her own, something funny has happened to the original desire to make a print with somebody she loves.

One way to catch the trick is to finish the scene you envy. You took the job in Lisbon. Good. You get the light through the shutters, fresh bread, the odd thrill of conducting an entire transaction in Portuguese. Who’s helping your dad take his fridge down the stairs on Wednesday? What did you do with the dog? What happens after the exact morning you’re currently imagining has happened sixty times? Maybe you find a neighbour for your dad, the dog loves the heat and on the sixty-first morning you still walk down the hill giddy with what you’ve done. The move may be worth making. Give the person who moved there a whole ordinary Wednesday and see if you still want to be them.

Conversely, give the person who stayed something better than a supporting role in an advertisement for Lisbon. Maybe she can lend a hand with the fridge. Maybe she didn’t want to, and being the sibling within driving distance has robbed her of a decade of free Wednesdays. There’s information in that resentment. She might ask her brothers to pay for a man with a van. She might move. There is a great deal of mileage between congratulating yourself on the life you chose and quietly accepting every single one of its current arrangements as part of an indivisible package.

A girl in a coat two sizes too big gets the mushroom. She turns it over inside the bag for a long time. “It’s for somebody who’s got one little round foot,” she says. Her dad opens his mouth to tell her what it is. She puts the other hand over his mouth without once taking her eyes off Ellen’s pencil. Ellen draws a little person with one ordinary foot and one shaped like a brioche. When the object comes out the girl wants to know if anybody has a sock with a hole in it. Her father’s moment has come.

By four there’s a drawing on the wall of his big toe seen through a grey sock. He’s signed a release permitting the museum to display the drawing. The release has a box for the signature of a parent or guardian if the subject is under eighteen. He says individual bits of him still feel quite young. His daughter insists that the toe is definitely old enough.

It’s a good afternoon. Some of its goodness could only possibly arrive after Ellen said yes. Nobody evaluating two calendar entries at eleven on Thursday could know how much she would enjoy drawing that toe. Nor could she know the name of the girl, what it felt like to watch the father surrender the floor, or how good the wood of the darning mushroom would feel in her palm when the room had emptied. If you wait for the evidence that would make you perfectly certain of a choice, some of the evidence will have to wait with you. It lives on the other side.

The thing Liv printed

On Sunday Liv comes round with an envelope. She has managed to get ink on the back of her ear. They have a brief and satisfactory conversation concerning the relative merits of soap, cooking oil and learning to live with a small permanent ear tattoo.

“I got you something,” Liv says.

The line is printed in a rather grand serif on a thick piece of cream paper. It says PLEASE LEAVE ROOM FOR THE PERSON CARRYING THE COFFIN.

Ellen laughs before she can ask why. The shop used to be next door to an undertaker. People coming to collect stationery kept blocking the alley. The printer had set the sign for the shared gate years ago. Liv, who remembered it from the first time they went there, asked to set it again. At the bottom in tiny red capitals she has added THANK YOU. It’s the thank you that does it. You could get a considerable amount of British civic life onto that one piece of paper.

“We found the visitors’ book,” Liv says. “Want to see?”

She does. There are their names. On the facing page some boy has written that he intends to marry Liv. This proposal has taken twenty-odd years to reach its intended recipient, who reasonably feels he could have followed up.

Was it a lovely afternoon? Yes. Is Ellen sorry she missed it? Yes. Did she make a mistake? For once these questions are allowed to sit in the same room without a fight breaking out. Ellen told a woman she would do some work, and she did it. Her friend stood where the two of them once stood and found their names. It was a thing worth wanting. She’s allowed to want it afterwards. Regret can testify that something was worth having. It’s a poor witness on the question of what would have happened if you’d gone.

There is one more piece of evidence to admit. Liv enjoyed going with her brother. He was fourteen the first time she tried to take him there and told her he would rather have his legs waxed. He spent Saturday printing little cards to go inside the bird boxes he makes. Ellen’s imaginary version of the workshop had Liv waiting at the case all afternoon in case her best friend materialised. The actual woman found somebody else to put ink on. In a curious way that makes Ellen both happier and sadder. Missing somebody does not confer ownership of their Saturday.

Liv looks round at the drawings spread across the table. She likes the mouse. She likes the toe better. She picks up the wooden mushroom and asks who made it.

“Grandad, I think,” says Ellen. She can picture him making things in a shed. The picture is vivid enough to make her suspicious. What she actually knows is that there was a shed, that he had hands and that the mushroom exists. On these standards of evidence he also built their local railway station.

She takes a photograph and sends it to her younger sister, Tess. Do you know where this came from? Used it at the museum yesterday. Thought you might like it back.

Liv is looking at the print and the drawings side by side. “Ever considered printing that toe?”

Now that might be possible. An old press has gone to a new owner. Liv has the woman’s number. Nobody’s pretending they can have last Saturday again. But a desire stripped of the demand to undo a previous choice is sometimes quite useful. They can see each other more than once a year. They can make something awful enough to frame. They can put an actual date in their phones and see which Tuesday the new owner could do. Ellen hangs the coffin sign in the narrow hall outside the downstairs loo, where passing traffic has been a cause for concern for some time.

As Liv is leaving, a message comes through from Tess. Yes I know where it came from. That flat bit on the side still there?

Ellen sets the mushroom on the table. It rolls half a turn and stops. There’s a whole afternoon she has never pictured, in which somebody took a file to this piece of wood and gave it a place to stop. For once the person who knows what happened is still available for questions. Ellen taps her sister’s name.
