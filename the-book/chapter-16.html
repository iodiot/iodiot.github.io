Chapter 16The Opportunity Cost of Being Everyone

On Thursday morning Ellen offers a woman forty bucks she can’t spare to move Saturday.

The woman runs a small letterpress shop. She’s retiring. A buyer is picking up the press on Monday, and Saturday is the last afternoon anybody can come in, set a line of type and print it. The woman listens with the patience of somebody who has spent forty years spelling people’s last names back to them. For forty bucks, Ellen can have a drawer to keep things in. Saturday is going to stay where it is.

Ellen thanks her and hangs up. She wants to go with her friend Liv, who first took her there at nineteen. Liv has already picked out a line to print. She won’t tell Ellen what it is.

At the same time on the same Saturday, a museum would like to pay Ellen to draw a potato masher. This is a better offer than it sounds. Ellen makes technical drawings for a living. On most days her great professional achievement is that a stranger can identify the underside of a valve without ever having to ask who drew it. Since the furniture company let her go she’s been freelance. It’s the same work, with a longer wait to find out whether anybody meant to pay for it. The furniture company is one of the clients. It pays her by the job to check the drawings made by the program that replaced her, which is a bit like being hired to proofread your own obituary.

Back when she had a salary she proposed a family afternoon to the museum. A child sticks a hand inside a bag, feels some mysterious domestic object and describes it to an illustrator who can’t see it either. The illustrator has to draw exactly what the child describes. Then everybody gets to see what an absolute bastard language is.

The museum has spent eleven months considering this proposal. For most of those months Ellen would have done it on any Saturday on the calendar, including Christmas if somebody could rustle up a child. Now a cancellation has freed up a room. They have a budget. They even have a potato masher. The fee is $160. Can she let them know by noon?

The notice from the electric company is stuck to the refrigerator, which has the most to lose. It has a red stripe across the top and would like $120 by Monday. Ellen has $120. It’s also the grocery money.

At eleven she has two bus maps open on the kitchen floor. Her car needs an alternator and is going to go on needing one. At eleven-fifteen she has discovered a bus that can take her from the museum to the print shop in twenty-six minutes. All she has to do is leave a roomful of children with their hands in bags, abandon Liv halfway through setting the type, and catch the same bus backward in time. She puts a shoe on each map to keep the edges from curling and stands between them barefoot.

If you’ve ever gone looking for a bus schedule to solve what is fundamentally a problem with how many bodies you have, come in. There’s coffee, at least until Monday.

The man who didn’t sell a pair of shoes

In 1850 Frédéric Bastiat published a little scene about a broken window. A boy breaks a pane. His father has to spend six francs on a glazier. The onlookers find a consolation. The glazier gets work. Those six francs will circulate. Perhaps a child with a brick and an adequate supply of windows could get the whole French economy back on its feet.

Bastiat invites one more person into the scene. Before his boy intervened in economic policy, the father might have spent those six francs on shoes, or perhaps a book. The glazier standing at the house is easy to see. The shoemaker who never gets the order has no reason to come over and stand in the street looking disappointed. After the repair the father has a window. Had it stayed intact he might have had a window and shoes. The injury has provided a job. Describing the job tells us very little about whether the injury was good news.[1]

I like the shoemaker because he doesn’t know he’s in the story. He gets up, opens his shutters, gets on with his day and never hears about the six francs. It takes an effort of imagination to make him visible. It takes a different effort to stop at one shoemaker. Once you start picturing what else those francs could have bought, France contains a hell of a lot of shops.

That stopping point matters. The opportunity cost of using a resource one way is the value of the best feasible alternative you give up. The phrase is usually traced to an 1894 journal article by the economist David I. Green, titled “Pain-Cost and Opportunity-Cost.”[2] Ellen, on her kitchen floor, could have told him they’re frequently the same item. If the father buys the shoes, he may be giving up the book he would otherwise have bought. He hasn’t also been deprived of six francs’ worth of sausages, six francs’ worth of candles and a thrilling evening with six francs’ worth of brandy. His capacity to picture all three is a fine thing. The shopkeepers would still prefer that he pay them.

Money has the useful property that the register can tell you when you’re trying this. Time is more polite. Nobody in the kitchen stops Ellen from losing, between eleven and eleven-fifteen, an afternoon with Liv, a printing apprenticeship at nineteen, a gift for the work that surprised everybody and the small but much loved public-television documentary about the gift. That’s a lot to lose standing on one spot. Ask which of those she could actually choose this Saturday and the kitchen gets quieter.

Only one of them has made her an offer. Liv has a place for her. It’s possible to get there, possible, barely, to pay for it, possible to spend the afternoon standing at the case with a composing stick in her hand. The nineteen-year-old with the apprenticeship will have to make her own travel arrangements.

Liv moved away several years ago. She and Ellen still send each other pictures of hideous lettering. To their friendship, a café advertising FRESHLY GROUN DCOFFEE is as good as a bunch of roses. Getting four hours together at a bench, with no obligation to make the four hours interesting, happens maybe once a year. They remember going to the print shop at nineteen and finding their names in the guest book afterward. Somebody had put an ornamental flourish under Liv. Ellen, who’d just been dumped, wrote her name as if she intended to press charges. The book might still be there. They could look for it. This is the life Ellen stands to miss, and it has a smell, a person and an address.

A hundred and ninety dollars

The museum fee is $160. Take out $10 for the round-trip bus fare and doing the session leaves Ellen $150 up. The print shop charges $35 for paper, ink and the afternoon at the press. The round-trip fare is $5. That afternoon leaves her $40 down. Choose the print shop and by bedtime her bank balance is $190 lower than if she’d done the museum. A hundred and fifty she could have earned plus forty she would have spent. Both trips take the same chunk of Saturday. Assume for the moment there are no other costs and we’re looking at cash before taxes.

That’s a different question from “Would you pay thirty-five dollars to print a page with Liv?” At thirty-five, yes. At a hundred and ninety she would at least like to know what the damn page says.

The extra money isn’t another charge from the printer. Ellen doesn’t owe a business $150 for the privilege of staying out of it. It’s the difference between two available afternoons. Price the visit against a free day at home and you get $40. Price it against the museum and you get $190. The friend is worth the same at every price.

In a study published in 2009, Shane Frederick and colleagues asked people to imagine coming across a video they wanted for $14.99. Some could choose to buy it or decline. Others saw the same choice with a few more words explaining that declining would leave the $14.99 available for other purchases. Without the reminder, 56 of 75 students said they’d buy the video. With it, 41 of 75 did. There was no additional money in the second version. There wasn’t even an actual video. A small change in what was made visible affected how people answered a hypothetical shopping question.[3]

One reason to say the alternative out loud is that sometimes the number decides it. Ellen’s number is on the refrigerator. She has $120 in her account, which is what the electric company wants by Monday. Spend $40 of it on Saturday and she has $80 and a notice asking for $120. Do the museum and she pays the notice and has $150 to eat on. Under those conditions a comparison of artistic fulfillment is of limited interest to Ellen and of no interest whatever to the meter.

It’s easy to give another person a stirring speech about what they’ll remember on their deathbed when you don’t have to live in their apartment on the intervening Tuesday. A shortage of money can settle an argument between two genuine desires without settling which desire the person holds more dear. Be careful what kind of biography you write from Ellen’s bank statement.

Liv, who knows about the job and not about the refrigerator, has offered to cover the afternoon, fare included. That takes forty dollars off the problem and leaves a hundred and fifty. Ellen would have the lights on and nothing to cook under them. And the money isn’t the whole of it, which is the awkward part. The museum has asked her for something she wants to do. It would be almost a relief if the director were a prick. She’s spoken to him twice in eleven months and both times he has been thoughtful, eager and irritatingly specific about paying her. A check, the same day.

At eleven-thirty Liv calls.

“Do you remember what she used to clean the press with?” Liv says.

“No.”

“Neither do I. I’m sitting here trying to remember a smell. Are you coming?”

Ellen starts to tell her how difficult it is to earn a living drawing things for people. This is a thoroughly researched subject. She has an audience of one, and unfortunately the audience has already offered to buy the ticket.

The notice would do as an answer. It’s real, it’s six feet away and nobody argues with a meter. It would also let her leave out the part where she’d like to draw the potato masher.

“I need the money,” she says eventually. “And I think I want to do the museum.”

“Oh,” says Liv. There’s a little space before she goes on. “Then I’ll ask my brother. He can drive. We can bring home a drawer.”

“Are you mad?”

“I wanted you to come, El. Those are different questions.”

After they hang up Ellen keeps her phone in her hand. If Liv had been mad she could have spent the rest of the morning taking a principled stand against emotional blackmail. Now she has to sit in her kitchen with the less bracing news that somebody would have liked her company. It’s good to be wanted. Nothing says it’ll feel good on the day.

The Thursday you can still do something about

At eleven-forty Ellen calls the museum. Before she accepts, she asks whether they have any other dates. This sounds like the kind of boring practical detail you might leave out of a serious philosophical investigation. Leave it out and you can spend the rest of your life bravely accepting the inevitability of a scheduling conflict that Janet in events could have moved to October.

Whether to wait has an almost insultingly practical test. What might she learn in the time she buys? If the museum can hold the booking for twenty minutes while the printer checks for a later slot on Saturday, twenty minutes buys a chance of getting both. If Ellen waits twenty minutes after both people have told her the dates are fixed, the only likely new information is that twenty minutes have gone by. Meanwhile Liv’s brother is waiting to find out whether he should put gas in the car.

They have no other date. The room belongs to the ceramics people the following weekend. After that the construction begins and they’re going to take the roof off. Ellen says yes to Saturday and asks them to put her name on the poster. Within ten minutes Liv’s brother has the place at the print shop. Half an hour later the museum emails a poster on which Ellen is a local illusionist. She considers leaving it. There may be a better living in making a potato masher disappear.

Somebody generally pays for another person’s open options. Liv has held a place for Ellen all week and hasn’t sent an invoice, which is among the better features of the friendship. It doesn’t make her Saturday a public utility. Keep saying “Let’s see how we feel” and eventually what the friend feels like doing is asking somebody else.

Even a free option can change the experience of what you choose. In an experiment by Daniel Gilbert and Jane Ebert, photography students made prints of their own photographs and had to select one to keep. Some could subsequently exchange their print. Others had to live with their first choice. When the researchers asked later how much the students liked the pictures, the group who could change their minds liked the one they’d kept less than the group whose choice was final. Students hadn’t expected the exchange arrangement to make this difference.[4]

That was a study involving photographs. It doesn’t transfer to divorce law, and a bad landlord would love to hear that your inability to get out of your lease is an exciting opportunity to appreciate the mold. A return clause can protect you from real defects that only show up later. But the study has something for a person who has actually chosen. For as long as the bus maps stay open on the floor, Ellen’s Saturday is still a form of shopping. She picks up her shoes and folds the maps.

On Friday she gets the bags ready. She has a tea strainer, a potato masher, a pastry brush and a wooden darning mushroom that belonged to her mother. It’s a squat little knob on a stick. You put it inside a sock so you can pull the hole flat enough to mend. Held the wrong way it looks like a mushroom drawn by somebody who has only had mushrooms described to them.

She nearly leaves the mushroom at home. Everything else she can replace. Then she remembers her mother’s considered opinion of people who kept the good towels for company, which was that their company must be filthy if it needed that many towels. She slips the mushroom into a bag and ties the cord loosely around the top.

The museum of what might have been

At one on Saturday a small boy puts his hand in the tea-strainer bag. He describes a bowl with lots of little holes where somebody forgot to put the bottom in. He feels the handle. There’s a tail coming out of its head, he tells Ellen. She asks him how big the head is. Big enough for a mouse, he says. The audience watches a helmet take shape. On her own authority, Ellen draws in the mouse.

The boy takes the strainer out. He’s faintly disappointed. An adult would now say something gracious about what an interesting mistake we all made together. The boy asks whether they have anything else in there that could kill a knight.

At one-thirty she looks up to find three empty seats. She ought to be looking for a bigger sheet of paper. Instead she sees Liv at the type case, tilting the little blocks of metal to read them upside down. Printers used to keep the capitals in a case set above the one for small letters, which is the entire reason anybody says uppercase.[5] Ellen learned that at nineteen and has been waiting twenty-six years for a good moment to bring it up. The same afternoon she got ink on the side of her face and went the rest of the day waiting for some mysterious new sexual confidence to explain why all the men at the bus stop were looking at her. She gets to enjoy that memory for several seconds before a man in front of her asks if she’s allowed to draw a potato masher in a public building.

This is where a tidy version of the story gives Ellen a sign. A child tells her she wants to become an artist. A newspaper photographs the mouse. The museum director approaches carrying a permanent job and a potato masher engraved To the only woman who chose correctly. Nobody does. A table leg wobbles. A father tries to get his daughter to say perforated although holey has done fine all afternoon. Ellen loses the point off her best pencil. Over in the print shop, at least in her imagination, the pencils are terrific.

The comparison has a dirty trick built in. She has access to the entire museum afternoon, including its plumbing and the part where she eats a squashed sandwich behind a fire door. Her visit to the print shop is being programmed by a woman with great affection for the subject and a running time of about ninety seconds. This is how an imaginary life gets a reputation for containing no boring parts. Run the little film the other way around. Put Ellen at the press sorting a tray of letters somebody has dropped, forty dollars lighter and doing the math on Monday, show her one image of a delighted child looking at a drawing of a mouse, and the museum acquires an almost unbearable luster. The missing life has been relieved of the duty to fill the other three hours and fifty-eight minutes.

It would be just as dishonest to protect this Saturday by inventing a bad afternoon for Liv. The woman who runs the shop might be a wonderful teacher. There might be doughnuts. Liv might laugh so hard she has to put the ink roller down. If Ellen needs her friend to have had a slightly lousy time in order to enjoy her own, something funny has happened to the original desire to make a print with somebody she loves.

A girl in a coat two sizes too big gets the mushroom. She turns it over inside the bag for a long time. “It’s for somebody with one little round foot,” she says. Her dad opens his mouth to tell her what it is. She puts the other hand over his mouth without once taking her eyes off Ellen’s pencil. Ellen draws a little person with one ordinary foot and one shaped like a dinner roll. When the object comes out the girl wants to know if anybody has a sock with a hole in it. Her father’s moment has come.

By four there’s a drawing on the wall of his big toe seen through a gray sock. He’s signed a release permitting the museum to display the drawing. The release has a box for the signature of a parent or guardian if the subject is under eighteen. He looks at the toe for a while and leaves the box blank.

It’s a good afternoon, and some of its goodness could only arrive after Ellen said yes. Nobody weighing two calendar entries at eleven on Thursday could know how much she would enjoy drawing that toe, or what it would feel like to watch a father surrender the floor. If she’d waited until she was certain, part of what would have made her certain would have had to wait with her, on the other side.

The director brings the check himself, in an envelope with her name spelled right. Ellen hasn’t thought about money for almost three hours. She counts that as the afternoon’s second achievement, after the toe.

The thing Liv printed

On Sunday morning Liv comes over with an envelope. She has managed to get ink on the back of her ear. They have a brief and satisfactory conversation concerning the relative merits of soap, cooking oil and learning to live with a small permanent ear tattoo.

“I got you something,” Liv says.

The line is printed in a grand serif on a thick piece of cream paper. It says PLEASE LEAVE ROOM FOR THE PERSON CARRYING THE COFFIN.

Ellen laughs before she can ask why. The shop used to be next door to a funeral home. People coming to pick up stationery kept blocking the alley. The printer had set the sign for the shared gate years ago. Liv, who remembered it from the first time they went there, asked to set it again. At the bottom in tiny red capitals she has added THANK YOU. It’s the thank you that does it. A man with a coffin on his shoulder has enough to deal with without a woman collecting her wedding invitations.

“We found the guest book,” Liv says. “Want to see?”

She does. Liv has it on her phone. There are their names. On the facing page some boy has written that he intends to marry Liv. This proposal has taken twenty-six years to reach its intended recipient. Liv feels, reasonably, that he might have followed up.

It was a good afternoon, Ellen is sorry she missed it, and she didn’t make a mistake. For once all three can sit in the same room without a fight breaking out. Ellen told a museum she’d do some work and she did it, and on Monday the lights will stay on. Her friend stood where the two of them once stood and found their names. It was a thing worth wanting. She’s allowed to want it afterward. Regret can tell her the afternoon was worth having. It has no idea what would have happened if she’d gone.

Liv, for her part, enjoyed going with her brother. He was fourteen the first time she tried to take him there and told her he would rather have his legs waxed. He spent Saturday printing little cards to go inside the birdhouses he makes. Ellen’s imaginary version of the afternoon had Liv waiting at the case in case her best friend materialized. The actual woman found somebody else to put ink on. That makes Ellen both happier and sadder. Missing somebody doesn’t confer ownership of their Saturday.

The drawings are spread across the table. The museum’s check is under the tea strainer, already spent. A hundred and twenty of it is keeping the refrigerator conscious. Liv likes the mouse. She likes the toe better. She picks up the wooden mushroom and asks who made it.

“Grandpa, I think,” says Ellen. She can picture him making things in his garage. The picture is vivid enough to make her suspicious. What she actually knows is that there was a garage, that he had hands and that the mushroom exists. By that reasoning he also built the train station.

She takes a photograph and sends it to her younger sister, Tess. Do you know where this came from? Used it at the museum yesterday. Thought you might like it back.

Liv is looking at the print and the drawings side by side. “Ever considered printing that toe?”

Now that might be possible. An old press has gone to a new owner. Liv has the woman’s number. Nobody’s pretending they can have yesterday again. They can put a date in their phones once Ellen knows what the new owner charges, which is the order her questions come in now. Ellen hangs the coffin sign in the narrow hall outside the bathroom, where passing traffic has been a cause for concern for some time.

As Liv is leaving, a message comes through from Tess. Yes I know where it came from. That flat spot on the side still there?

Ellen sets the mushroom on the table. It rolls half a turn and stops. There’s a whole afternoon she has never pictured, in which somebody took sandpaper to this piece of wood and gave it a place to stop. Ellen taps her sister’s name.
