Chapter 05Your Brain Is a Prediction Machine With Terrible Customer Service

The message that says “Sure”

At 9:41 p.m. you send a text. “Are we still on for tomorrow?” At 9:43 your phone lights up. “Sure.”

Four letters, one little dot. Yesterday this person sent you three exclamation points and a penguin. Tonight there’s been a dramatic contraction in the local penguin population. You scroll up to establish the time of death.

The penguin was alive at 4:12 yesterday afternoon. Between the penguin and the period lies exactly one event, Tuesday’s dinner. By a quarter to eleven you’ve located a questionable joke you made at that dinner, reconstructed the seating chart, and compiled enough supporting material to convict yourself. Meanwhile your friend has sent precisely one word.

She lives across the hall. On an ordinary night you’d settle this in your socks, by knocking. Tonight no light shows under her door. You know because you’ve taken out the trash, at nearly eleven at night, with one tea bag in it.

Your roommate Gus is eating cereal over the sink. You hold out the phone. He reads it. “So she said yes,” he says. You scroll up to the penguin and hold the phone out again.

On the other side of the screen somebody moved a thumb a fraction of an inch. On this side a stomach has tightened, a cup of tea has gone untouched and a grown person is checking whether penguins were always this rare in October. The symptoms are all genuine. Most of the plot is still open for casting.

Notice what the investigation has left out. In an hour of scrolling it hasn’t once occurred to you to wonder how her day went. Every exhibit is about you: what you said, what she thinks of you now, what it’s going to cost. Dread is a diligent detective with exactly one suspect. It doesn’t need four letters, either. People have opened this inquiry over a thumbs-up.

One scientific account of perception starts from an everyday difficulty. The signal reaching your senses can fit more than one story. A shape in the doorway might be your coat or somebody who hasn’t learned to knock. In predictive-processing accounts, what you expect helps you interpret what you sense, and a mismatch can change the expectation. Researchers disagree about how much of the mind this framework explains. It gives us a good question to ask about that text. What did you bring to the reading?[1]

The best story I know about what a prediction can do to a pair of eyes has a planet in it.

The planet everybody saw

In 1846 a mathematician in Paris named Urbain Le Verrier did the best trick anybody has done with a pencil. Uranus kept straying from its predicted path. Le Verrier calculated where an unseen planet would have to be to drag it off course and mailed the position to Berlin. His letter reached the astronomer Johann Galle on September 23. Galle found Neptune that night, within one degree of where the letter said to look.[2]

So when Le Verrier turned to Mercury in 1859 and found another misfit, he knew what a misfit meant. The point where Mercury swings closest to the Sun creeps forward a little faster each century than the pull of the known planets can account for. He put the excess at 38 arcseconds a century. An arcsecond is one 3,600th of a degree. The modern figure is 43. That’s the width of a dime seen from about 280 feet, most of a football field, and Mercury needs a hundred years to run up that much error.[3] He was right that it was real. He was also the man who’d found a planet inside a discrepancy like that. He concluded that something more was in there, between Mercury and the Sun, lost in the glare.[4]

That December a letter arrived from Edmond Lescarbault, a country doctor in the village of Orgères-en-Beauce. Back in March, through his own small telescope, he had watched a small black dot cross the face of the Sun. Le Verrier took the train, walked the last twelve miles from the station, showed up unannounced and grilled him. The doctor’s watch had no second hand. He’d counted seconds with a pendulum he’d made from an ivory ball and a piece of string, and, being short of paper, he kept his notes in charcoal on a board. The most exacting astronomer in France went through all of it and came home convinced. By early 1860 the planet had a name, Vulcan, and the doctor had the Legion of Honor.[5], [6]

For the next two decades people kept seeing it. Reports came in of dark dots crossing the Sun, some of them dug out of notebooks years old. Le Verrier worked out orbits and predicted when Vulcan would cross again, and each time the Sun’s face stayed clean. He died on September 23, 1877, thirty-one years to the day after Neptune, still sure of his planet. The following July a total eclipse crossed the American West, and two respected astronomers, James Craig Watson in Wyoming Territory and Lewis Swift near Denver, each reported a new point of light beside the darkened Sun. Their positions didn’t agree with each other, or with Le Verrier’s planet.[4], [7] Astronomers from Lick Observatory photographed the sky around the eclipses of 1901, 1905 and 1908 and found nothing, and Mercury went on being 43 arcseconds wrong.[8]

On November 18, 1915, in Berlin, Albert Einstein presented a calculation to the Prussian Academy. In his new theory of gravity, a planet close to the Sun doesn’t retrace its orbit the way Newton’s laws say it should. For Mercury the difference came to 43 arcseconds a century, with nothing adjusted to make it fit. He told one friend he’d been beside himself with joy for days, and another that the result had given him heart palpitations. A planet with a name, an orbit and a decorated discoverer had been abolished by a man with no telescope.[9]

None of these people were fools or frauds. Dark dots do cross the face of the Sun. Usually they’re sunspots. Points of light do show up beside an eclipsed Sun. Usually they’re stars. The observers had real specks. What they brought to the eyepiece was the planet. And the expectation was a good one. It came from the man who’d been right about Neptune, by the method that had been right about Neptune. In 1859 Newton’s law had never been caught in an error, and a hidden planet had been the right answer the last time. A prediction gets that strong by paying off. That’s what makes it so hard to see around on the night it’s wrong.

A few dozen astronomers aren’t a brain, and an episode from 1859 can’t show that your neurons run on prediction. That case gets made in laboratories, and it’s still being argued. Vulcan shows something plainer. Expectation can put an object in front of trained eyes.

“Sure.” with a period and no penguin is your 38 arcseconds. It’s real. She sent it. Something may well be off. The joke at dinner is your planet.

A hundred evenings

How far should a real discrepancy move you? Try a little bookkeeping. Invent a hundred evenings in this friendship. On twenty of them she’s irritated with you. On half of those she sends a short reply. On the other eighty evenings she sends a short reply only a quarter of the time. A curt message is twice as common when she’s irritated. Your case is looking excellent. Please remain seated for the arithmetic.

Ten of the twenty bad evenings produce a short reply. So do twenty of the eighty decent ones. In this invented history only ten of the thirty short replies mean trouble. You started with one chance in five and now have one in three. That period earned a little suspicion. You appear to have sent flowers to the funeral of a friendship with a two-in-three chance of being fine.

Those numbers came out of my pocket. Nobody gets issued a table like that with a friend. What you can borrow is the question people skip when they see something that fits their theory. How often would I see this very same thing if my theory were wrong? The men who saw Vulcan never asked how often a dot crosses the Sun when no planet is involved. Gus, rinsing his bowl, reads the message aloud the way you’d read a parking sign. “Sure.” The ink hasn’t moved.

One chance in three is still worth something. You’d carry a raincoat on those odds. A raincoat costs almost nothing, and so does going a little gently tomorrow with a friend who might be mad at you. Texting her at midnight that the two of you need to talk is a more expensive kind of raincoat.

The price of checking

Now audit your evening. If three different people told you she sounded upset, you’d have three accounts to weigh, though even then they might all have heard it from the same loudmouth. Rereading the message three times gets you one account and two good close-ups of your thumb. Showing it to Gus added a reader. For tonight’s tally the sample size is one.

So why can’t you put the thing down? Researchers who study anxiety keep coming back to threats whose timing or likelihood is uncertain. Something that might happen at any moment gives you no good moment to stand down.[10] In 2016 a team at University College London had forty-five volunteers turn over rocks in a computer game. Under some rocks was a snake, and a snake meant a mild electric shock to the hand. Stress, by the volunteers’ own account and by their sweat and pupils, peaked when the odds of a snake were fifty-fifty. It bottomed out at zero, and bottomed out again at a hundred percent.[11] A sure shock is easier to sit with than a maybe. A friend who has told you to go to hell is the rock with the snake definitely under it. She’s a problem with edges. You can apologize, or argue, or buy flowers on purpose. A friend who is maybe something is a dark door you can keep walking past with the trash.

Checking promises to settle it. Marcel van den Hout and Merel Kindt had volunteers check a gas stove on a computer screen, over and over. Their memory of the last check stayed as accurate as anybody’s. What fell was how vivid the memory was, and how far they trusted it.[12] Nobody in the lab had a house to burn down. The stove was a drawing, and the doubt arrived anyway, manufactured by the checking that was supposed to remove it. You’ve now read four letters and a period some thirty times. They’ve never looked less like English.

By eleven you could send “Are you mad at me?” and get a little relief. If you knew what you’d done and wanted to apologize for it, that would be a real conversation waiting to happen. Tonight it would be a request for a sedative. She already told you tomorrow was on. That reassurance was dead by 9:44. Consider what a second one would have to do to survive in this household.

You try Gus instead. “Do you think she’s mad at me?”

“I think she said sure.”

What you want from Gus is a guarantee that she isn’t going anywhere, which would call for powers suspiciously absent from the rest of his résumé. He goes to bed. You stay up with the phone.

Two breakfasts

Around eleven the rehearsals begin. Your imaginary friend has now heard six versions of your opening statement. In version seven she finally says the thing you need her to say. This feels like progress until you notice that you’re writing her lines too. She hasn’t received a script and in the morning may have the bad taste to improvise.

Another part of you has skipped breakfast and gone straight to the year after it. No two knocks on your door when her kettle boils. Some other door getting them. Nodding to her at the mailboxes like somebody you once sat next to on a plane. In studies of affective forecasting, people tend to overestimate how hard and how long an event will dominate their feelings, and one reason is the way the mind sets the stage. You imagine the loss and give it the whole year. You leave out the rent coming due, Gus needing the bathroom, your uncle calling about a shelf. An incomplete forecast can exaggerate one feeling without making the eventual hurt any less real.[13] Your stomach, which can’t tell a forecast from a delivery, has started on the year tonight.

You could switch to rehearsing the happy version, in which she’s fine and you’re an idiot. Practicing cheerful mind reading is still practicing mind reading. Try a less glamorous skill. Write two forecasts that can lose, by naming something that would happen in one and surprise you in the other.

In the first breakfast, the one you’ve been catering all night, she’s finished with you. She keeps her coat on and orders only coffee. The joke comes up inside five minutes, because she’s been waiting to bring it up.

In the second she’s fine. She’s late, she eats half your hash browns, and when you mention the text she has to scroll back to find it.

A coat on or off, a joke mentioned or not. By nine-fifteen one of those will be wrong. The thing you’ve been running since 9:43 can’t lose. A warm text would have meant she was hiding it. A cold one means you were right. No text at all would have been worse. Vulcan lasted half a century on those terms. Every dot was the planet and every clean Sun was bad luck. Einstein’s number was a different kind of object. It said 43, and Mercury was free to say something else.

Keep one awkward possibility open all the same. A person might be irritated with you and still show up for coffee because she likes you. A model with two states, fine and finished, makes beautifully decisive predictions about a friendship that exists nowhere outside your own tired head.

And you have one advantage Le Verrier never had. He couldn’t ask Mercury. You’re having eggs with yours at nine.

At 9:43 you couldn’t have ordered that first jolt of dread back down your spine. It was delivered, and no desk exists where you can return it. By midnight you’d made a seating chart for the prosecution and your tea had gone stone cold. Somewhere between those two events you acquired a little discretion over how the evening went. You use what’s left of it to put the phone face down and go to bed. Face down, mostly. It gets turned over twice before one.

“Just parked. Coffee?”

You get to the diner twenty minutes early, in a clean shirt, with an apology for the joke roughed out on a napkin. Third draft. At 8:52 the phone lights up. “Just parked. Coffee?”

You look at the napkin. Shit. You’ve paid for the flowers.

She comes in with her coat half off and her keys in her teeth. She hasn’t been home. She spent the night at her mother’s, across town, which is why no light showed under her door. “I was draining Mom’s pasta when you texted,” she says. She has barely sat down when her phone lights up. A plumber wants to know if he can come Friday instead. You watch the choreography from three feet away this time. Cup in one hand, thumb on the screen, coat sliding off a shoulder. “Sure.” No penguin. The plumber may yet get on with his life.

So the second forecast wins, you’re an idiot, and you’d be glad to leave it there. Then the eggs come, and she puts her fork down.

“Can I say something about Tuesday?”

Here it is. You have a napkin for this.

“I was trying to tell you about Mom’s appointment,” she says, “and inside of a minute we were back on your manager. Lately we’re always back on your manager.”

“I thought it was the joke.”

“What joke?”

You tell her the joke. It does no better the second time.

Three drafts on a napkin for an offense nobody else recorded, while the thing you actually did went past you without a twinge. You were right that something was off. The one chance in three came in. It just had nothing to do with the period, which was typed last night by a woman with a pot in her other hand, and nothing to do with the joke. You spent eleven hours pointing every instrument you own at the wrong patch of sky.

The real trouble is the awkward kind your two forecasts had no room for. She’s irritated with you, and she parked the car and texted Coffee?

Add up what happened to you between 9:43 and now. Four letters. Most of a night’s sleep. One sentence about your manager, which took her six seconds and which you survived sitting down. Everything else you suffered from, you had to build: a joke, a seating chart, a year of empty hallway. The dread went looking for a planet because a planet was what it had found before.

So you ask what you missed on Tuesday. Her mother has waited since July to see a specialist. The appointment was yesterday afternoon. They’re running more tests, and it’ll be two weeks, maybe three, before anybody knows anything. The clinic has given her mother a login for a portal where the results will turn up at whatever hour the system pleases, along with some advice about not checking it too often.

You gave one period most of a night. If pure attention were the same thing as care, you’d be in line for a statue.

She has two or three weeks of not knowing in front of her, the kind with something in it, and she’s spending the first morning of it buying you coffee. You turn the napkin over, put the phone face down on top of it and ask what the doctor said, exactly. Twice in the next ten minutes your job tries to come up. You let it knock.
