Chapter 02The Control Problem

The leak begins at 2:17 in the morning, which is an unnecessarily theatrical hour for water to make an announcement. You wake to a small, steady sound from the kitchen. Drip. Drip. Drip. The ceiling over the counter has developed a brown stain shaped vaguely like a country you once failed to identify on a map. You stand beneath it in your underwear, holding a saucepan, and learn that adulthood is mostly discovering new ways to move water from one container to another.

You slide the saucepan two inches to the left. The next drop hits metal. Under the counter is the cabinet where you keep a box of photographs, on the theory that nothing ever happens there. You carry the box to your bed and stand a pillow in front of it. The pillow protects it from nothing. It feels correct.

What you want to know is whether you can go back to sleep. You set the glass measuring cup in the pan, under the drip, and read the clock on the stove. 2:24.

Then you call the building’s emergency maintenance number, give the person on duty your apartment number, and hear the sentence that divides the rest of the night in half. Someone will call you back.

For the first half there were things your hands could do. Now your hands have found the phone again. You check the volume. Full. You check the call history. Yes, you actually called. The box of photographs needed moving exactly once. The need to know when help will come renews itself about every forty seconds.

At 2:34 the measuring cup reads half a cup. Half a cup in ten minutes. An empty two-quart pan holds eight cups, so at that rate it buys you 160 minutes, or two hours and forty minutes. If the drip turns into a stream, you have a new problem and some very handsome obsolete mathematics. Until it does, you empty the pan, set a timer for two hours and go find your pants.

The phone and the measuring cup make an excellent pair of instruments. One tells you whether somebody has called. The other tells you when you’re going to need a bigger pan. You can spend half the night consulting the first instrument about the second question. At some point the sound hitting the kitchen floor will constitute peer review.

The only competent adult in the building

While you look for pants, your mind goes shopping for somebody to blame, and the nearest candidate is standing in your underwear. You should have noticed the stain. Was it there on Sunday? You’ve stood in this kitchen for four years and can’t swear you ever looked up.

Believing you could have prevented everything takes a peculiar vanity. It means that somewhere in this building, among all the people with pipes and tools and access to the upstairs bathroom, the only competent adult was apparently you. A difficult honor to decline at two in the morning.

I understand the appeal. If it’s your fault, it was preventable, and if it was preventable, the world still runs on rules and you merely broke one. Blame is the cheapest way to buy back a universe that makes sense. The other universe on offer tonight has a pipe in it that let go for reasons nobody will ever tell you, and a security deposit whose future is being decided by a ceiling.

Your roommate Gus appears in the doorway a little after two-thirty, hair pointing at the stove, a roll of duct tape already in his hand. Gus owns two solutions to any problem. The other one is the drawer.

From the drawer, under a takeout menu for a restaurant that closed before you moved in, he produces the building’s original floor plan. It shows where the pipes are supposed to go. A date in the corner is from before either of you was born. Between then and this saucepan, at least one human being has owned a wrench and experienced the thought that’ll do.

According to the drawing, no pipe runs over your counter. The nearest one comes down behind the bathroom wall, a good ten feet from the stain. The drawing is valuable anyway. It tells you whose floor is over your head and where a sensible person would start looking. It records what somebody intended in a year when the building was new. The water hasn’t read it.

Gus tears a strip off a grocery bag and tapes one end beside the wet spot, so the drip runs down the plastic and lands dead center in the pan. It works the first time, which at this hour passes for engineering. “Houston,” he says, “we have a problem.”

Nobody on that flight said it that way, and the true version is better. You have a couple of hours. Pull up a chair.

Square canisters, round holes

On the evening of April 13, 1970, about fifty-six hours after launch and some 200,000 miles from Earth, an oxygen tank exploded in the service module of Apollo 13. “Okay, Houston, we’ve had a problem here,” said Jack Swigert. He, Jim Lovell and Fred Haise had left two days earlier to land in the lunar hills called Fra Mauro. Within about an hour the ship that was supposed to bring them home was dying, and Houston moved them into the lunar module, a lander whose air scrubbers were sized for two men for two days. They were three, and home was four days off.[1], [2], [3]

Most of what mattered was already out of their hands. The tank had been doomed for years. In 1965 its heaters were redesigned for a higher voltage, and every part was upgraded for it except the little thermostat switches, which were overlooked. In 1968 the tank was dropped about two inches. In March 1970, during a countdown rehearsal, it wouldn’t drain, so the oxygen was boiled off with the tank’s own heaters. By the board’s account the switches welded shut and the insulation cooked on the wires inside. NASA had its that’ll do. The review board blamed “an unusual combination of mistakes, coupled with a somewhat deficient and unforgiving design,” a phrase that would also do for your building.[4]

Then the air. Every breath a man lets out adds carbon dioxide to the cabin, and canisters of lithium hydroxide scrub it back out. After a day and a half in the lander a warning light came on. The dead command module next door held plenty of fresh canisters. Those were square. The lander’s sockets were round. Three men were being slowly poisoned by their own breathing, and the cure was sitting a few feet away in the wrong shape.[1], [5]

In Houston an engineer named Ed Smylie ran the Crew Systems Division. His people built an adapter out of things they knew were on the ship: plastic bags from the cooling garments, the cardboard cover of the flight plan, a hose from a spacesuit and a roll of gray duct tape. Smylie said later that once he knew tape was aboard he felt they were home free. “One thing a Southern boy will never say is, ‘I don’t think duct tape will fix it.’” Joe Kerwin, an astronaut who was also a physician, read the instructions up over the radio a step at a time. “Our do-it-yourself lithium hydroxide canister change is complete,” Swigert reported. The thing looked like a mailbox, which is what everybody called it. In about an hour it was working, and the carbon dioxide came down.[5], [6], [7], [8]

Count up what the three of them had. They couldn’t unexplode a tank. They couldn’t land. They couldn’t move the Earth any closer. They had a list of verbs. Tape this. Turn that off. Read the gauge. On April 17 they splashed down in the Pacific, about four miles from the recovery ship. They had gone to land on the Moon. None of the three ever did.[2]

What’s on board

When those three called for help, a building full of engineers picked up and stayed on the line for four days. You have Gus. Their stakes were three lives, and yours are a ceiling, a deposit and a night’s sleep. And we tell their story because it ended in a splashdown. Nothing in a roll of tape promised that. A crew can do every sensible thing and still not come home, and then nobody quotes the engineer. So I can’t offer you Apollo 13 as proof that good work decides how things turn out. It doesn’t prove that about Apollo 13.

What survives the trip to your kitchen is the question Smylie’s people worked on. Nobody in that room could answer will they live. They could answer what’s on board. On board here: a pan, a measuring cup, a clock, a roll of tape, a phone and a roommate. It’ll be dawn before you remember the neighbors.

Also a timer, and setting it meant putting a price on being wrong. Set the alarm early and you lose some sleep. Set it late and your downstairs neighbor learns your name. Two errors of the same number of minutes can have different costs. You’d set it for two hours, which buys forty minutes of margin for forty minutes of sleep. That’s a fair trade. A lifetime guarantee would be slightly more expensive.

What you’d really like is the time of the callback. If it’s two hours off, you can sleep while Gus listens for the phone. If it’s exactly seventeen minutes, you can spend sixteen of them enjoying the sensation that your life is in competent hands. I’d pay a lot for that second service. I’ve occasionally mistaken the size of my proposed payment for proof that the service exists.

At 4:35 the timer goes off and the pan holds six cups, as forecast. Nothing else in your life has performed this reliably all year. You empty it, reset the timer and go back to bed.

A little before six you wake because the sound has stopped. The pan holds a quart. You stand in the gray light from the window over the sink and feel, for about a minute, like a person who has won.

Then you get businesslike. You outline the stain in pencil and write the time beside it. A bigger stain tomorrow would be news. You photograph the ceiling, the pencil line and the pan. You email the landlord a request for repair with the pictures attached, so that the request has a date on it. All of this is sound, and I’d like to be plain about what it’s for. It’s for the deposit. It’s so that when somebody says damage a year from now, you can say 2:17.

At first light you call the neighbor upstairs, whose number you have because of a package in March. He checks his bathroom floor and reports it dry as a bone, which sinks the overflowing bathtub you’ve been privately blaming on him since 2:20.

That leaves a question you were happier without. Half a cup every ten minutes has been arriving in this building since 2:17. It has stopped arriving in your kitchen. Water doesn’t quit. It goes somewhere else.

The apartment underneath

A dry counter is excellent news until you learn where the water went. At twenty to seven you knock on the door below yours. You’d give a lot for her to be out, and you stand there long enough to give her a chance to answer.

She opens the door in a school district windbreaker, holding a mop bucket by the handle the way other people hold a purse. You’ve lived over her head for four years. You know her car. You’ve never needed her name.

“It’s you,” she says. “I wondered who was doing all the walking.”

So that’s who you were last night. While you ran a small space program by the sink, a woman who gets up at five lay under your heels at 2:17 and 2:35 and 4:35 and formed a view.

Her kitchen ceiling started in around six. She heard it before she saw it, and the bucket has been under it since. You tell her what you have. It started upstairs at 2:17. Half a cup every ten minutes. Maintenance says someone will call back. Her face does something at call back that tells you she’s been a tenant here a long time. Twenty-two years, it turns out.

“You heard it at two,” she says, “and you’re down here at twenty to seven.”

“It wasn’t coming through down here at two.”

“You know that?”

You don’t. You know when it stopped upstairs. The drawing in the drawer has no line on it for where water goes when it’s finished with you. She isn’t angry. She says it the way she’d read out a route number, and then she tells you her actual problem, which has nothing to do with whose fault anything is. Her name is Lorraine. She drives a school bus. She has to be at the yard by ten after seven, forty-odd kids have never been late on her account, and she can’t walk away from a bucket without knowing how long a bucket lasts.

This you can do. You go up for the measuring cup. Ten minutes on her counter gets a shade under half a cup. The building is keeping some for itself. Her bucket holds three gallons, which is forty-eight cups. Emptied now, at half a cup every ten minutes, it’s good for 960 minutes. Sixteen hours. Lorraine looks at the number, then at the clock, picks up her keys and goes to work. All night you’d thought of that arithmetic as yours.

Cosmetic

At breakfast Gus asks what happens now. It takes the two of you about a minute, because you make it a game of grammar. Finish the sentence “Before lunch we can” with a verb one of you could perform. Keep the pencil line honest. Send Lorraine the photographs and the times. Write down who’s calling whom. “Get the landlord to care” contains a second person’s verb, which is where the trouble begins.

Gus wants to put cracked pipe upstairs in the email. “The drip stopped at six” is what either of you saw. The pipe is a guess, and if it goes out without a label, by lunchtime three people will be quoting you as the building’s structural engineer.

By the time the eggs are done, the back of the floor plan has three names on it and three phone numbers. The neighbor upstairs. Lorraine. The landlord. Influence is easier to live with when it has a name and a telephone number.

Emergency maintenance calls at ten to eight. Someone has, as promised, called you back. They’d like you to have a reference number.

The landlord comes by a little after one with a clipboard and a printout of the photograph you sent at dawn. He looks at the ceiling for some time. It’s dry. He looks at the pencil line. The stain has stayed politely inside it. “Cosmetic,” he says, and writes it down.

The saucepan is on the counter with the last quart still in it, because some part of you knew this was coming. You pick it up and hand it to him. A pint’s a pound, the old rule goes, near enough. Cosmetic turns out to weigh about two pounds. He holds it like somebody else’s baby and gives it back.

You tell him where the rest went. Directly under his shoes, home between routes, Lorraine is emptying a bucket. He says he’ll take a look when the schedule allows. As of this afternoon, then, your landlord has fixed the leak by changing which tenant has it.

People will tell you, at moments like this, to accept what you can’t control. It’s good advice with one drawback, which is that the man with the clipboard would give it to you too. He’d love for you to be at peace. As far as I can make out, acceptance means you stop arguing with the ceiling about whether it should have leaked. It has nothing to say against a request with a date on it, a reference number, or two tenants on two floors who now have each other’s phone numbers and matching measurements. You can’t make him care. Making it expensive not to care has verbs in it. The landlord can continue to find the ceiling attractive on his own time.

A fitting the size of your thumb

It takes a week. On the fourth day a piece of Lorraine’s ceiling about the size of a placemat lets go while she’s out on the afternoon route. Most of it misses the bucket. She texts you a photograph with no words. You forward it to the landlord with the reference number and the word cosmetic in quotation marks.

On the seventh day a plumber cuts a square hole in your ceiling, reaches into the dark and hands you a corroded fitting about the size of your thumb. It’s been going green up there since before you signed the lease. A bill follows, which for once isn’t yours, then a repair, and eventually a coat of paint. Your ceiling gets painted first. Yours is the one with the paperwork.

Months afterward you open the box you moved that night and find your grandmother, aged twenty, squinting into the sun. You’d forgotten that photograph. Saving it was a decent night’s work, and you can say so without knowing whose fault the pipe was, or whether next year’s landlord will be an entirely different species of bastard.

So add the night up. The fitting was never yours to do anything about. Neither was the callback, or the word on the clipboard, or Lorraine’s ceiling, which was coming down whatever you did with a saucepan. What was yours was smaller, and it still took all night. You carried a box twelve feet. You counted to half a cup. You wrote down a time. You knocked on a door you were hoping nobody would answer and gave a stranger a number she could go to work on.

And one more thing, which had been yours for four years without your knowing it. You take your shoes off at the door now. Lorraine has never asked. She gets up at five, and she’d know your heels anywhere.
