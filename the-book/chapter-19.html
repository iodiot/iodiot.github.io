Chapter 19Feynman Was Curious. You Are Just Anxious.

Sam has got as far as writing probable structural defect before the boy tells him it only happens on D.

They’re in the practice room on Tuesday evening. The piano is one of those big upright jobs that looks as if it was built in the room and the room will one day have to be buried with it. A child has reported a kind of buzz, somewhere behind the keys. Sam can feel it through his fingertips when he rests a hand on the top. There it goes. Brrrp. He has seen piano innards precisely once, in a pub where somebody went through the front of one in a fight. That makes him, of the two adults in the room, the leading specialist.

“Is it dangerous?” asks the boy’s mum.

There are any number of reasonable answers to this. “I don’t know” covers the present state of human knowledge in the immediate vicinity. “Let’s leave it alone until somebody who maintains pianos can listen to it” would be sensible if there were grounds to suspect a real fault. “Is it dangerous to whom?” might start an interesting conversation about the relative prospects of the instrument, the child and the man expected home in thirty minutes with a bag of frozen peas.

Sam says, “Probably something worked loose in transit.”

“It’s been here since before I was born,” says the boy.

It is at this point that the title of this chapter becomes unfair to Sam, to you, and quite possibly to Richard Feynman, a human being who managed on occasion to be anxious. You can be curious and anxious in the same chair. What’s worth noticing is that they ask different things of the next five minutes. Curiosity might like to hear the D again. Anxiety would very much like the boy’s mother to go away believing that a qualified man has the situation under control.

She does go away. She needs to move the car. The boy plays the D again. Sam gets one ear right down against the side of the piano and tries to hear what his original opinion sounds like from nearer the floor.

The most expensive word on the repair slip

The form on the hall noticeboard has three spaces. Fault reported. Action required. Name. Sam has already filled the first with probable structural defect. Look at the value that first word is providing. In the event that there’s a defect he will have found it. In the event that there isn’t, he has at no point guaranteed there would be. A man with a head for business could rent that word out by the hour.

Suppose you are the caretaker coming on shift tomorrow. You get a piece of paper saying Rattle on low D when played firmly. Heard it three times. Couldn't find the source. What can you do with it? You can play the note. You can try it softly. You can get the piano tuner to come on a day when the instrument is free. Now take probable structural defect. What does that tell you to do? Quite possibly telephone an insurer and close a room. Our man has described the bit he can’t locate with words that make it sound as though he’s located it. This can get expensive quite a long way down the line.

There is a private version of this form and most of us are filling it in all the time. Fault reported: nobody said much after I spoke in the meeting. Probable structural defect: my colleagues think I’m a prick. If that’s the explanation, the action required is extensive. Retrain, move city, start again among a people with no historical memory of your contribution to the stationery budget. If the thing you actually observed was a roomful of people looking towards a window, it may be worth checking whether a bloke in a high-visibility jacket was at that moment attempting to reverse a skip through the car park.

It’s tempting to diagnose all this as stupidity and extend a broad professional exemption to smart people like ourselves. Smart people can have a particular problem. They are capable of producing an explanation faster than anyone else can play the D again. Give a clever person ten minutes and they can make a thoroughly satisfying case for a loose joint, factoring in temperature, humidity and the probable movements of furniture during the Winter of Discontent. At the end you will know something impressive about the person. The piano has yet to be consulted.

In 1960 the psychologist Peter Wason gave people the three numbers 2, 4, 6. He told them these obeyed a rule. They could propose further triples and he would tell them whether each triple obeyed it, until they felt ready to say what the rule was. They had to find the rule the experimenter had chosen. For once the universe had agreed to give a straight yes or no and was employed by a university. [1]

Try it yourself on paper. If you think the rule is add two each time, then 8, 10, 12 ought to work. It does. So does 20, 22, 24. You now have two satisfying ticks in the margin. You can get a third tick with 106, 108, 110. The world is an orderly place and you have begun to suspect you might have a gift.

There’s another rule that gives the same set of ticks. Any three numbers in increasing order. How much did you learn about which of those rules is in force by getting three yeses? Count the predictions on which the two rules disagree. So far the score is zero. Thirty more examples of the same kind will bring your lifetime total of useful disagreements to zero. You’ll have spent considerably more pencil.

Try 3, 8, 19. The add-two rule predicts no. The increasing-order rule predicts yes. Wason’s actual rule accepts the triple. You have learned more from one carefully chosen question than from a whole page of the sort that make your idea look good. Now try 8, 8, 19. The actual rule excludes it. That distinguishes strict increase from allowing the next number to stay the same. None of this proves that your best remaining guess is the only conceivable rule. Somebody could invent one with a special exception for your mother’s telephone number. It does let you throw out guesses you had good reason to consider, at the cost of two short questions.

The example is often told as if the trick were to seek the answer no. That’s a wretched rule to run a relationship on and hardly better here. A yes to 3, 8, 19 is what kills the add-two theory. The trick is to make rival explanations give different answers before the experimenter opens his mouth. Sometimes trying the thing your theory predicts will work is the cleanest test available. It depends on what else could explain a success.

Even numbers present an attractive halfway house. Try 3, 5, 7 and you can get rid of even numbers going up by two while keeping all numbers going up by two alive. That’s useful information. It’s also a good reminder that confirmation isn’t a foul act in itself. If I ask whether the lift is still working and see somebody come out on the fourth floor, I’ve learned something relevant. I don’t have to try to get the thing to fall out of the roof in the interests of epistemic hygiene.

Why does the toy problem feel different when it’s your own answer in the margin? Because now a yes is doing two jobs. It’s telling you something about a set of numbers and something agreeable about the set of brains you brought with you. Wason studied people trying to infer a rule. He didn’t test Sam’s self-esteem or establish the inner motives of everybody who ever bought a blunt pencil. You can, however, feel the difference in your own hand between writing a triple likely to vindicate you and writing one that might require an entirely new page.

The boy has been practising the same two bars while Sam thinks. He’s called Kit. The note that rattles is the low D below middle C. He plays the D above middle C and waits. The rattle stays away.

“See?” he says.

Sam hears the see? as a challenge. He would like to ask what qualifications an eleven-year-old can ordinarily obtain in the maintenance of upright pianos. He is prevented by a sudden distressing awareness of the qualifications a grown man in this room can produce on the same subject.

“All right,” he says. “What do you think it is?”

“Dunno,” says Kit. He starts the tune again.

There’s an admirable piece of time management. He has identified the limit of his knowledge, regarded it for less than a second and gone back to the activity for which the room was booked. Sam can’t quite go with him. His name is already on the slip. He’d like to be able to take the words back without anybody seeing him do it.

What the glass of water showed

On 28 January 1986 the space shuttle Challenger broke apart shortly after launch. All seven members of its crew died. Richard Feynman served on the presidential commission investigating the accident. At a public hearing he compressed a piece of O-ring material and chilled it in ice water. When released, the cold sample did not promptly spring back to its previous shape. Anyone looking at it could see why the speed with which a seal recovered might matter. [2]

It makes an irresistibly tellable story. A room full of people with titles, an ordinary glass of water, a physicist who thinks to ask the material a question. If you’re putting together a television sequence you know where to point the camera. It’s harder to film somebody looking over records from a previous flight. It’s harder still to produce a satisfying close-up of an engineer who had expressed concerns before the launch, back when the audience for the story hadn't turned up.

Consider how narrow the question in the glass actually was. Would this compressed sample, under these cold conditions, rebound immediately when released? The behaviour was visible. What if the claim were larger? Had the joint on the vehicle encountered those exact conditions? Which part failed? What information had been available to the people deciding whether to launch? Was the very design of the joint vulnerable to influences that a flight system ought to have tolerated? Those are different questions. You can keep the glass on the table as long as you like. It has no further witnesses to call.

The commission’s investigation drew on flight evidence, hardware, testing and records. It found a failure of the seals at a joint in the right solid rocket motor. Temperature affected how the joint sealed. Its report also examined how information and objections had travelled, and failed to travel, before the decision to launch. There was a physical question about material changing shape. There was an institutional question about what happened when engineers brought bad news to people who needed to decide. A glass of cold water made one difficulty visible. It could not on its own have established the chain of events or settled who knew what and when. [3]

A funny thing happens if we turn Feynman into the only intelligent person in that room. We lose about half the reason to admire what he did. Anybody can announce afterwards that an organisation ought to have known better. The useful thing about a public demonstration is that it offers people with different standing in the room a way to look at the same result. The sample doesn’t have a job to keep. It doesn’t have a way of styling the first few seconds as a broadly positive development in the direction of resilience. It sits there, out of shape, while interested parties decide what they can honestly say about that.

There’s an ethical distinction here that gets lost in a lot of accounts of scientific courage. After a disaster, looking for evidence that challenges your preferred explanation honours the people whose deaths you’re investigating. Before a launch, it would be grotesque to impose the burden of a fatal demonstration on the people raising a safety concern. Sometimes the right way to act under uncertainty is to stop until you know more. An appetite for risk is not a qualification in experimental design. The seven people who climbed aboard that morning had every right to expect that work to have been done on the ground.

People have a habit of saying they’re just asking questions when they are in fact trying to make somebody repeat an answer until they hear the one they like. A genuine question carries a small risk for the questioner. The answer might run against them. It might give somebody else the first intelligent line in the room. It might require them to alter a piece of paper bearing their signature. A useful way to distinguish curiosity from an inquisition is to ask what answer would let the other person go home.

When Kit’s mother returns, Sam tells her what he’s actually heard. She listens and says, “Is he playing it wrong?”

Kit stops with both hands resting on the keys.

There are two separate things to find out now. One is what makes the instrument buzz. The other is what Kit heard when his mother asked that question. The instrument is a piece of equipment. With permission, you may try the same note seven times to see what it does. A child required to account seven times for what he meant by a facial expression will produce results of very little value to science and enormous interest to any child plotting the long game towards choosing your care home.

“Would it do it if I played?” Sam asks.

Kit gets up. Sam presses the key. Brrrp.

“Well, that’s a relief,” his mum says.

“To you, maybe,” says Kit. “Everyone thought I was doing something interesting.”

A decent question has a price

What could make a noise that appears only on one note? A sounding piano string sets other parts of the instrument and the air in motion. Objects coupled to it can vibrate too. A loose object can move enough at some frequencies and loudnesses for two surfaces to touch repeatedly. That little series of contacts makes a rattle containing sounds besides the original piano note. It doesn’t follow that the thing rattling is a broken piece of piano. A perfectly healthy instrument is capable of annoying some entirely unrelated article of crockery.

Imagine two possibilities. Something inside the piano is loose. Something resting on it is shaking. Play the D harder and both mechanisms might get louder. Try the D an octave up and both might go quiet. Those are interesting observations, especially if you’re trying to reproduce the result for a tuner, but they do rather little to choose between the two stories. Clear the top of the instrument and repeat the note at about the same force. There’s an experiment cheap enough to fit into a child’s remaining practice time, provided the child agrees to spend it on the research.

He would like his time back. His mum has set her phone timer so he gets the full half-hour before they have to leave. It’s been running for fourteen minutes. In that time he has played perhaps two minutes of his piece. Sam’s accidental promotion to Professor of Asking Questions has had precisely the same effect on the applicant’s education as the original buzz, with the minor difference that the buzz is in tune.

“You get on with it,” Sam says. “I’ll come back at the end.”

He goes downstairs. He has an alarming suspicion that the most useful exercise of curiosity available to him in this particular room is leaving it. We tend to admire the person who keeps asking after everybody else has had enough. People who do this for a living are occasionally called scientists. Depending on the room, they’re also called the reason everybody ducks out of the meeting before Any Other Business.

In the kitchen Bev is counting mugs. “We’ve been missing one since the first Tuesday,” she says. Sam remembers the blue enamel mug on top of the piano. Now, because there’s nobody to witness him thinking it, he’s willing to have a new idea. It’s extraordinary how much good work the mind can do once it feels safe from the possibility that its owner might look like a tit.

He could, of course, sprint upstairs brandishing the mug theory as confidently as he introduced the structural defect. You often see the two beliefs trade places with absolutely no reduction in the owner’s facial certainty. A man can go from knowing it’s gluten to knowing it’s sugar in the time it takes to download another podcast. The diet changes. The rather grand position occupied by the man in relation to the truth remains pleasantly undisturbed.

What might Sam be anxious about here? Partly he genuinely doesn’t want a child or a piano to get hurt. That’s decent. Partly he is used to being useful in practical situations, and there’s a specific little pain in seeing the person who brought you the problem lose confidence in your competence. That’s human. Partly there’s a woman upstairs whose approval he enjoyed for about four seconds. Also human. If any of those needs is running the inquiry, no quantity of information about piano strings will fully satisfy it. You cannot use a tuning fork to measure whether people still think you’re worth calling.

Perhaps your version is a message sent to a friend. You have a brief, direct question about tomorrow. No reply. You check the time. That is information if you need to book a table at six and the kitchen closes at eight. You open the message again and reread it for the word that offended them. You check whether they’ve posted something in the intervening seventeen minutes. You notice they have used a little red heart on a photograph of somebody else’s dog. The dog is now a material witness. This can easily go on until midnight without producing another single observation of any relevance to dinner.

There are two possible projects in this example. You want to find out whether you’re meeting a friend. You also want the feeling of not having been rejected. A message asking them to let you know before half past six can do the first job at fairly low cost. The second is an astonishing commission to award to a little drawing of a red heart, particularly one currently attached to a Labrador. It helps to know which project you’re funding.

And there are real occasions when the worry is right. A colleague has started leaving you out of meetings. A partner has developed a complicated new policy about which rooms permit them to answer the phone. A mechanic is telling you that three separate lights on the dashboard have all failed in a reassuring direction. There’s no prize for ignoring the pattern because you fear looking anxious. It can be worth writing down exactly what happened, setting out what else might explain it and asking a direct question while you can still bear to listen. A history of being dismissed can give a person a keen ear for an early warning. It can also cost them a great deal to live on a permanent state of alert. They deserve evidence and support, the same as anybody with a quieter nervous system.

Sam’s returning urge to check the D is working to a different clock from Kit’s half-hour. Downstairs he can just hear the piece coming together above him, the first few bars over and over. There’s a fat chord at the point Kit always stops. Then he starts again a little earlier, gives himself a run at it, and the next bar arrives. Sam hadn’t realised there was going to be a next bar. His entire association with the boy until now has been as the nearest available source of a reproducible fault.

The end of the investigation

At five minutes to the half-hour he goes back up and waits outside. Kit’s mum is sitting in the corridor doing something inscrutable to a shopping order on her phone. “You’ll have to wait,” she tells him. “He’s just getting to the bit I like.”

When the timer goes Kit opens the door and asks whether they want to hear a trick. He plays the low D. Brrrp. Plays it again. A clean sound this time. Again. Brrrp. He holds both hands up. Sam has an excellent view of the child’s hands and absolutely no view of what he’s doing with one elbow.

On top of the upright is the enamel mug. The teaspoon inside it leans against the thin enamel rim. He has been leaning his elbow against the spoon to keep it still. Release it and the note rattles it against the enamel again. He found this out while reaching for his music. The spoon made a clink like a tiny version of the noise he’d just been hearing. The boy took his sheet down, put the mug back and tried it.

There’s still something to find out. If you grip the piano cabinet with a forearm while holding the spoon, perhaps the grip changes how the piano vibrates. Sam asks Kit to play and rests just one fingertip on the end of the teaspoon, keeping the rest of his body away. The rattle stops. Now he leaves the mug on top and takes the spoon right out. The note rings clear, even hard. Spoon back in the mug at its old angle, same note, same ridiculous little noise. They try the note on either side, then move the mug to a chair, and the low D behaves itself. For the conditions under which they heard the complaint they can turn the fault on and off about as reliably as the light.

There is always one more qualification you could add. The test doesn’t certify the whole instrument. It could have a loose fitting elsewhere. The far end of the keyboard remains perfectly free to harbour its own mug. A piano tuner inspecting the thing next month may find a defect unrelated to the rattle and unrelated to the public education of a man who was supposed to be buying peas. What they’ve shown is enough to resolve the reported buzz and enough to spare Bev a service call about a teaspoon. Putting limits on a conclusion can actually make it sturdy enough to use.

The man in the expensive version of this story makes a little speech about young people possessing an instinctive curiosity we have tragically lost as adults. This would credit Kit with a large spiritual position just when he has been exceptionally clear about what he wants, which is for the grown-ups to bugger off while he tries the bit with the chord. Sam says, “Nice ear. Can you show me how the piece goes?”

Kit does. He starts halfway through. His mother gets the bit she likes a second time and you can see it coming in the way her foot starts tapping well before his left hand gets there. The boy misses a note and plays on. If you’ve ever met an adult who couldn’t manage that after a wrong fact came out of their mouth, you might think this is the more impressive of his two accomplishments.

On the fault slip Sam draws a line through probable structural defect. Underneath he writes that the spoon in the blue mug rattles when the low D is played hard, that lifting it stops the noise, and that the mug is now back in the kitchen. He adds Kit’s name under found by. It looks a little strange there, in part because so many of the standard forms for attributing responsibility were printed on the assumption that the person who found something would be the one holding the clipboard.

“What would you have done if it had still rattled?” Kit asks.

“Called the tuner.”

“Why didn’t you just say that?”

“I liked having something clever to put on the form.”

There’s a moment when Kit appears to be considering how to let him down gently. “You could’ve put where the mug goes,” he says. “Otherwise somebody’s just going to put it on there again.”

Bev gives him a bit of tape and he marks the shelf in the kitchen. That’s how the rattle investigation ends, with a slight improvement in the lifetime employment prospects of a mug and a child whose name has been spelled correctly at the first attempt.

Sam texts Jo about the peas. She’s already bought some. He reads her answer twice trying to decide whether she’s annoyed, then rings and asks. She is, a little. He’d said he’d be back an hour ago. She’s also eating them, they’re going cold, and if he wants any he should get his arse home. A clear answer is a wonderful thing. There is no commitment in the terms and conditions that you’re going to like it.

On the noticeboard on his way out he passes a proof of the poster for the music-room meeting. Somebody’s put a quotation on it from Albert Einstein. That’s a gratifyingly large increase in the committee’s intellectual resources, though Sam has a few doubts about how much of the discussion Albert got to hear. The question on the agenda is who pays Bev when the late class ends. There’s a pencil hanging off the board on a string in case the great man wishes to declare an interest.
