Chapter 19Feynman Was Curious. You Are Just Anxious.

Sam has gotten as far as writing probable structural defect before the boy tells him it only happens on D.

They’re in the practice room at the community center on a Tuesday evening. Jo sent him out for frozen peas, and Fran caught him on the way. She lives next door to his brother Dan, and she asked him to take a look, on the theory that a man who writes furniture assembly instructions for a living must be good with things. The piano is one of those big uprights that looks as if it was built in the room and will one day have to be buried with it. A child has reported a buzz somewhere behind the keys. Sam can feel it in his fingertips when he rests a hand on the lid, next to a stack of sheet music and a coffee mug somebody left up there. There it goes. Brrrp. He has seen the inside of a piano exactly once, in a bar where a man went through the front of one in a fight. Of the two adults in the room, that makes him the leading specialist.

“Is it dangerous?” asks the boy’s mom.

Several honest answers are available. “I don’t know” covers the present state of human knowledge in the vicinity. “Let’s leave it alone until somebody who works on pianos can listen to it” would do if he had grounds to suspect a real fault.

Sam says, “Probably something worked loose in transit.”

“In transit from where?” she says. “It was here when I was in Brownies.”

Curiosity and anxiety can sit in the same chair, and tonight both are in Sam’s. They want different things from the next five minutes. Curiosity would like to hear the D again. Anxiety would like this woman to leave believing a qualified man has the situation in hand.

She does leave. She has to move the car. The boy plays the D again. Sam gets one ear down against the side of the piano and tries to hear what his opinion sounds like from nearer the floor.

The most expensive word on the repair slip

The slip came off a pad on the bulletin board downstairs. It has three spaces. Fault reported. Action required. Name. Sam has filled in the first. Look at what the word probable is doing for him. Should a defect turn up, he found it. Should none turn up, he never promised one. A man with a head for business could rent that word out by the hour.

Now follow the slip. Bev collects them on Fridays and sends them downtown to the city’s recreation department. Suppose this one said Rattle on low D when played firmly. Heard it three times. Couldn’t find the source. Somebody can act on that. Play the note. Try it softly. Get the tuner in on a day the room is free. Probable structural defect asks for something else, which is a call about liability and a lock on a door. Sam has described the part he can’t locate in words that sound as if he’s located it.

And this is a bad month to hand the recreation department a locked door. With the building due to close at five from January 1, it’s collecting sign-in sheets to see whether anybody would notice. The practice room is one of the few things bringing anybody in. Sam knows none of this. He’s a man with a pen and an audience.

Most of us fill in a private version of that slip all day long. Fault reported: nobody said much after I spoke in the meeting. Probable structural defect: my colleagues think I’m a prick. If that’s the explanation, the action required is extensive. Retrain, move, start over among a people with no memory of your contribution to the stationery budget. If what you observed was a roomful of people looking toward the window, it might be worth checking whether a guy in a reflective vest was at that moment trying to back a dumpster through the parking lot.

It’s tempting to call this stupidity and give smart people like ourselves an exemption. Smart people have their own version. They can produce an explanation faster than anyone else can play the D again. Give Sam ten minutes and he’ll make a satisfying argument for a loose joint, taking in temperature, humidity and the probable movements of furniture during the Blizzard of ’78. At the end you’ll know something impressive about Sam. The piano has yet to be consulted.

In 1960 the psychologist Peter Wason gave people three numbers, 2, 4, 6, and told them the triple obeyed a rule he had in mind. They could propose triples of their own, and he’d say whether each one fit, until they felt ready to name the rule.[1] Try it. You think the rule is add two each time, so you offer 8, 10, 12. Yes. Then 20, 22, 24. Yes. You have two check marks and the beginnings of a suspicion that you’re gifted.

Another rule earns the same check marks. Any three numbers in increasing order. Count the times your questions made those two rules disagree. Zero. Thirty more of the same kind will bring the total to zero. Now offer 3, 8, 19. Add-two says no. Increasing-order says yes. Wason’s rule was the second one, so the answer comes back yes, and your first theory is dead of a single question. The story is often told as if the lesson were to go looking for a no, which is a wretched way to run a marriage and no better here. A yes did the killing. Joshua Klayman and Young-Won Ha argued in 1987 that testing what your theory says will work can be the best move available.[2] What counts is picking a question the rival explanations answer differently, before anybody opens his mouth.

Wason’s volunteers were guessing at a rule about numbers, and the man holding the answer told them the truth every time. None of them had already written a guess on a slip with a space for his name. You can still feel the difference in your own hand between a triple likely to vindicate you and one that might need a new sheet of paper.

The boy has been playing the same two bars while Sam thinks. His name is Kit. The note that rattles is the D below middle C. He plays the D above it and waits. No rattle. He glances over to see whether that registered and goes back to his two bars.

Sam would like to ask what qualifications an eleven-year-old ordinarily holds in the maintenance of upright pianos. He’s stopped by a sudden awareness of the qualifications a grown man in this room can produce on the subject.

“All right,” he says. “What do you think it is?”

“Dunno,” says Kit, and starts the tune again.

He has reached the edge of what he knows, looked at it for under a second and returned to what the room was booked for. Sam can’t go with him. His words are already on the slip. He’d like to take them back without anybody watching.

What the glass of water showed

On January 28, 1986, the space shuttle Challenger broke apart shortly after launch. All seven members of its crew died. Richard Feynman served on the presidential commission that investigated. At a televised public session he compressed a piece of O-ring material and chilled it in a glass of ice water. When he released it, the cold rubber didn’t spring back. Anybody watching could see why the speed at which a seal recovers might matter.[3]

It’s an irresistible story. A room full of titles, a glass of water, a physicist who thinks to ask the material. It’s harder to film somebody reading the records of earlier flights. It’s harder still to get a good close-up of an engineer who raised the alarm before the launch, when the audience hadn’t arrived yet.

The question in the glass was narrow. Does this sample, squeezed and this cold, rebound right away? It doesn’t. Whether the joint on the vehicle met those conditions, which part failed and what the people deciding had been told all needed other work. The commission did that work with flight data, recovered hardware, tests and records. It found that the seals had failed at a joint in the right solid rocket motor, and that temperature affected how the joint sealed. It also traced how objections had traveled before the launch, and where they stopped.[4]

Turn Feynman into the only intelligent person in the building and you lose half the reason to admire him. He wasn’t the first to worry about cold rubber. Engineers at Thiokol, the company that built the boosters, Roger Boisjoly among them, had argued against launching in the cold the night before.[4] Feynman himself was steered toward the O-rings by a fellow commissioner, General Donald Kutyna, who in Feynman’s telling brought it up while musing about the carburetor on his car.[5] Kutyna later said the tip had come to him from the astronaut Sally Ride, also on the commission, and that he kept her part quiet until after her death in 2012.[6]

What the demonstration gave everybody, whatever their rank, was the same result to look at. The rubber has no job to protect. It can’t describe its first few seconds out of the clamp as a broadly positive step in the direction of resilience. It sits there out of shape while the interested parties work out what they can honestly say about that.

One distinction gets lost in stories about scientific courage. After a disaster, hunting for whatever contradicts your favorite explanation honors the dead. Before a launch, it would be grotesque to make the people with a safety worry stage a fatal demonstration to earn a delay. Sometimes the right act under uncertainty is to stop until you know more. The seven people who climbed aboard that morning were entitled to expect that work had been done on the ground.

Nobody dies of a rattle, and very little of that night belongs in a practice room. One small thing carries over. The engineers that night said what they’d seen and how far it went, to an organization that had a use for a different answer. Sam is writing for an organization too. He’s about to tell it more than he knows.

When Kit’s mother comes back, Sam tells her what he’s actually heard. A rattle, on one note, three times. She listens and says, “Is he playing it wrong?”

Kit stops with both hands on the keys.

Two things need finding out now. One is what makes the instrument buzz. The other is what Kit heard in his mother’s question. The instrument is equipment. With permission, you can try the same note seven times and see what it does. A child made to account seven times for the look on his face will produce results of no value to science and great interest to any child playing the long game toward choosing your nursing home.

“Would it do it if I played?” Sam asks.

Kit gets up. Sam presses the key. Brrrp.

“Well, that’s a relief,” his mom says. Kit looks less relieved. For a quarter of an hour he was the cause of something.

A decent question has a price

What makes a noise on one note and no other? A sounding piano string sets the rest of the instrument moving, and the air, and anything resting on it. A loose object can shake enough at certain pitches for two surfaces to tap each other over and over. That tapping is the rattle. Nothing says the object has to be a broken piece of piano. A healthy instrument can annoy some unrelated article nearby.

So Sam has two stories. Something inside is loose, or something sitting on the piano is shaking. Play the D harder and both stories predict more noise. Try the next D up and both predict less. Those are 8, 10, 12. The question the two stories answer differently is cheap. Clear the top of the piano and play the note again at about the same force. It fits inside a child’s practice time, provided the child donates it.

He’d like it back. His mom set her phone timer so he’d get his full half hour, and fourteen minutes are gone. He’s played about two of them. Sam’s inquiry has cost the boy’s education exactly what the buzz did, except that the buzz is in tune.

“You get on with it,” Sam says. “I’ll come back at the end.”

He goes downstairs with the slip in his hand, suspecting that the most useful thing his curiosity can do for that room is leave it.

In the kitchen Bev is counting enamel mugs. “We’ve been missing one since the first Tuesday,” she says. Then she reads his slip upside down, a thing custodians can do, and stops counting.

“You know where those go?”

He doesn’t. She tells him. Downtown, to the office that’s reviewing whether this building is worth lighting after five. If the evenings go, her evening hours go, twelve a week. A slip that says structural about a room up there won’t bring an engineer. It’ll bring a padlock, and nobody signs in to use a padlocked room.

“Is it structural?”

“I don’t know what it is.”

“Then why’d you write that?”

Sam has put something clever on a form before. That one said APPROVED BY SAM, and it cost Ellen her job. Her sister Tess runs these Tuesdays. He hasn’t mentioned the connection to anyone in the building.

He remembers the blue enamel mug on top of the piano. Down here, with only Bev to see him think, he’s willing to have a new idea. The mind does some of its best work once it feels safe from looking like a jackass.

He could sprint upstairs waving the mug theory as confidently as he announced the structural defect. Beliefs trade places like that all the time with no change in the owner’s face. The theory changes. His grand position in relation to the truth stays where it was. And Sam now has a fresh reason to distrust himself. He wants it to be the mug. He wants it for Bev’s twelve hours and for the pleasure of tearing up the slip, and a theory you’re rooting for is a theory you’ll test with 8, 10, 12.

What’s he anxious about, anyway? Partly he doesn’t want a child or a piano hurt. That’s decent. Partly he’s used to being useful with objects, and it stings to watch the person who brought you a problem lose faith in you. Partly a woman upstairs approved of him for about four seconds. If any of those is running the inquiry, no amount of information about piano strings will satisfy it. A tuning fork can’t measure whether people still think you’re worth calling.

The price of asking properly is that the answer may go against him in a new direction. Suppose they clear the top and the D still rattles. Then something in the piano is loose, he won’t know what, and he’ll have to write exactly that on a slip addressed to people who’d be glad to read it. He can make the words narrow. He can’t make them say what Bev needs.

Upstairs the piece is coming together, the first bars over and over. A fat chord arrives at the point where Kit always stops. Then he starts again a little earlier, takes a run at it, and the next bar comes. Sam hadn’t known a next bar existed. Until now the boy has been the nearest available source of a reproducible fault.

The end of the investigation

At five minutes to the half hour he goes back up and waits outside. Kit’s mom is in the hallway doing something to a grocery order on her phone. “You’ll have to wait,” she says. “He’s just getting to the part I like.”

When the timer goes, Kit opens the door. He has something to show them and is trying to look as though he doesn’t. He plays the low D. Brrrp. Again, and it’s clean. Again. Brrrp. Sam has an excellent view of the boy’s hands and none of what he’s doing with one forearm.

The enamel mug is on the music shelf, next to his sheet music. A teaspoon stands in it, leaning on the thin rim. He’s been resting his forearm on the spoon to hold it still. Take the arm away and the note rattles the spoon against the enamel. He found it reaching up for his music, which was on top of the piano with the mug. The spoon clinked, a tiny version of the noise he’d been hearing all evening, so he brought the mug down where he could reach it and tried.

One thing is still unsettled. A forearm leaning on the shelf might change how the whole cabinet vibrates, spoon or no spoon. So Sam puts the mug back on top, where it stood when the complaint came in. He asks Kit to play and touches one fingertip to the end of the teaspoon, keeping the rest of himself clear. The rattle stops. He takes the spoon out altogether. The note rings clean, even hard. Spoon back in at its old angle, same note, same ridiculous little noise. They try the notes on either side. They move the mug to a chair. The low D behaves.

This is resonance. A spoon leaning in a mug has a natural frequency, a rate at which it prefers to shiver once disturbed, and this one sits close to the D below middle C, about 147 vibrations a second. That note pushes it in step, each push adding to the last, until the spoon chatters against the rim. The notes on either side push at the wrong moments and get almost nothing.

Under the conditions of the complaint, they can switch the fault on and off like a light. That’s all they’ve shown. Nobody has certified the piano. It may have a loose fitting somewhere else, and the far end of the keyboard is free to harbor a mug of its own. A tuner next month may find something unrelated to the rattle and to the public education of a man who was sent out for peas. But the reported buzz is accounted for, and a conclusion with its limits stated is sturdy enough to use.

A certain kind of adult would now make a short speech about the natural curiosity of children. Kit has been clear all evening about what he wants, which is for the grown-ups to get lost while he tries the part with the chord. Sam says, “Nice ear. How does the rest of it go?”

Kit shows him, starting in the middle. His mother gets the part she likes a second time, and her foot starts tapping well before his left hand arrives. He misses a note and plays on. If you’ve met an adult who couldn’t manage that after a wrong fact came out of his mouth, you may think it the more impressive of the boy’s two accomplishments.

Downstairs Sam draws a line through probable structural defect and asks Bev for a fresh slip.

“They’re numbered,” she says. “That one goes in whether you like it or not.”

So he writes underneath, small, in the space that’s left. Spoon in blue mug on top of piano rattled when low D was played hard. Taking the spoon out stops it. Piano not otherwise examined. The form has no space for who found it, so he makes one and puts Kit’s name there. His own goes under Name. The three words are still easy to read through the line. A line through a phrase tells a reader downtown that somebody thought it and then thought better of saying it.

“What would you have put if it still rattled?” Bev asks.

“Call the tuner.”

“You could’ve put that the first time.”

“I liked having something clever to put on the form.”

She doesn’t tell him it’s fine. She drops the slip in Friday’s envelope, sets the mug on the shelf with the others and counts them again.

In the parking lot Sam texts Jo about the peas. She’s already bought some. He reads her answer twice, trying to work out whether she’s annoyed, catches himself doing it and calls. She is, a little. He left for one bag of frozen peas an hour ago. She’s also eating them, they’re getting cold, and if he wants any he should get his ass home. That took forty seconds and he knows where he stands.

The slip is harder. It goes downtown on Friday with his name on it and a line through its first three words, and he won’t get to call anybody and ask how it was read.
