Chapter 08The Other Person Is Not a Supporting Character

At eleven at night Jo opens the dishwasher. A wall of clean plates rises up to greet her. Tomorrow’s breakfast bowls are in the sink, under tonight’s frying pan. Sam glances up from his phone with the unfortunate confidence of a man who remembers pressing a button.

“You said you’d take care of the dishes,” says Jo.

“I did,” says Sam. “I ran it.”

Jo lifts a cereal bowl out of the sink and holds it where he can see it.

In the pause that follows, the dishwasher, the only party in the room to have fully discharged its duties, beeps.

I know Sam’s confidence from the inside. Pressing start feels like finishing. It has a click, and a little light comes on, and a machine takes over. A man can sit on a couch under that light and feel that the household owes him a short parade.

Here’s the evening as Jo had it. She saw the full sink at six. She asked for help at seven. Now she’s checking a job she thought she’d handed over, and being the one who remembers, asks and checks feels a lot like still having the job. Here’s the same evening as Sam had it. He cleared the table, scraped the plates, loaded the machine and ran it while Jo was on the phone with her sister. He thinks he’s being told that an hour of work has ceased to exist.

They were both in the kitchen. Each spent the evening inside a slightly different script. In Jo’s, Sam’s next line is “Just tell me exactly what you want me to do.” In Sam’s, Jo is about to say “Do I have to do absolutely everything?” They start answering those lines before either has been spoken. This is an efficient way for two people to have a four-person fight.

Three histories of the evening are on offer. Jo’s begins at six, when she saw the sink. Sam’s begins at seven, when she asked. The dishwasher keeps a third on its little display, and it’s the one that promised fifty-eight minutes and then spent a geological age on the last one. Nobody in this kitchen is a neutral timekeeper.

A woman who reads minds for a living

Jo is forty-one, a veterinary technician at a 24-hour emergency animal hospital. Three days a week she works the late shift, which is when people bring in the dog that ate a sock and the cat that quit eating on Thursday. None of her patients can tell her where it hurts. She reads ears, gums, the set of a tail, the way an animal goes still when her hand reaches the place. She’s good at it, and part of being good at it is never trusting what the owner says the animal is thinking. “He’s just sulking,” says a man whose cat is in a great deal of pain. “She knows what she did,” says a woman whose dog is about to need surgery for it. Jo nods, and looks at the gums. On a late shift a wrong reading can kill something, so she checks hers against a thermometer.

Then she comes home to the one patient on her list who can talk, and she doesn’t ask him anything. She reads him. He reads her back. Sam has taken one look at her face over the dishwasher door and knows what she thinks, which is that he’s lazy and always has been. Jo has taken one look at the phone in his hand and knows what he thinks, which is that she can’t be pleased. Each of them would bet the security deposit on it.

It’s worth finding out where a reading that confident comes from. For that we need a horse.

The horse of Mr. von Osten

In 1904 Berlin had a horse that could do arithmetic. His name was Hans. His owner, Wilhelm von Osten, was a mathematics teacher, and he showed the horse in a courtyard to anyone who cared to come. Ask Hans what seven and five made, and he tapped a hoof twelve times and stopped. He did fractions. He did it for strangers, and he did it when von Osten wasn’t the one asking. Von Osten never charged admission, which made fraud hard to credit. A swindler would have sold tickets.

That September a commission of thirteen looked into it. It included a circus manager, a veterinarian, a cavalry officer, several schoolteachers and the psychologist Carl Stumpf. They were looking for a trick, some signal passed from man to horse. On September 12 they reported that they’d found none.[1], [2]

They were right. It was a sound finding by people who knew tricks. A circus man can spot a trainer’s cue across a ring, and no trainer was giving one. Nobody was signaling the horse on purpose. The follow-up went to a younger colleague of Stumpf’s, Oskar Pfungst, and Pfungst changed one thing the commission hadn’t. He arranged matters so that the person asking didn’t know the answer. Hans fell apart. Then Pfungst fitted him with blinders, so that he couldn’t see the person asking, and he fell apart again. In one series Hans got 50 answers out of 56 right when he could see his questioner and 2 out of 35 when he couldn’t.[1], [2]

So the horse had been reading a person, and Pfungst worked out what he read. Somebody waiting for the right number leaned forward a little to watch the hoof. When the count arrived, the head came up by a hair, and Hans stopped tapping. The questioners had no idea they were doing it. Von Osten had no idea. For years he’d been getting his own arithmetic tapped back at him by a hoof, and he’d taken it for the contents of a horse.

Then Pfungst did the part I like best. He went back to the laboratory and played the horse. He had people think of a number, and he tapped with his hand and watched their heads. Of twenty-five people, all but two gave the number away without knowing it. By his own account, when he was the one asking Hans, he made the same movement, and knowing about it didn’t stop him.[1], [2] Von Osten never accepted any of this. He went on showing Hans to large crowds until he died in 1909.[2]

Start with what the horse was good at. Hans caught a movement so small that thirteen investigators standing in front of it missed it. And the thing he read was never the sum. He had no idea what seven and five made. He knew when the man in front of him expected him to stop. Sam is excellent at this. After years with Jo he can tell to the half second when her face wants him to quit talking. He has no idea what she’s adding up.

Then there’s von Osten. He asked a question, got his own answer back out of another creature and experienced it as that creature’s mind. When Sam consults the Jo in his head about tonight, the one who thinks he’s lazy, it’s fair to ask who is doing the tapping.

What doesn’t carry over is the scale. Pfungst showed where one horse’s answers came from. A horse in a Berlin courtyard can’t tell you how often a man misreads the woman he lives with. For that, somebody had to count people.

Twenty-five experiments and a man in his socks

In 2004 Nicholas Epley and colleagues published a set of experiments on how people work out what somebody else is thinking. Their participants tended to start from what they knew themselves and adjust toward the other person, and the adjusting stopped short. Hurried, they stopped shorter.[3] A kitchen at eleven supplies hurry, noise and a strong sense of being the injured party.

Later Tal Eyal, Mary Steffel and Epley tested the standard advice head-on. Across twenty-five experiments they told people to take the other person’s perspective, to imagine their way into that person’s head, and then checked the guesses against what the person in question said. Imagining didn’t make the guesses more accurate. If anything it made them a little worse, and now and then it made people more sure. In the last experiment some participants got to do something else. They got to ask. That helped.[4] These were calm tasks about opinions and reactions, so they say more about what imagining can’t do than about what a fight will do.

This puts Jo in an enviable position. The world’s leading authority on what Sam meant is standing three feet away in his socks. She hasn’t consulted him, and for the same reason he hasn’t consulted her. The Sam in her head is much easier to argue with. He says the lines he was written. He can be dealt with at leisure after the real one has gone to bed. The real one might say something that ruins a very good speech.

“Hold on,” Jo says. “When did you think you were done?”

He looks honestly puzzled that a second answer could exist. “When I pressed start.”

In Sam’s head the job was called run the dishwasher, and he ran it. In Jo’s it was called the dishes, and it ended with bowls on a shelf. Nobody was lazy and nobody was impossible. They’d been using two names for the job. That’s the first new fact anyone has brought into the room all night, and it took one question to get it.

The production starring Sam

Sam, to his credit, now tries the very thing the twenty-five experiments warn about. He pictures the evening as Jo. In this production he’s still a pretty good guy, and around nine o’clock she finally notices. Jo has a part in it. She has about three lines, and all of them are about him. A revealing feature of putting yourself in somebody else’s shoes is how often you bring your own head.

He gets as far as she’s tired, which is true. Then he works out what he’d want if he were that tired, and it’s a hand on his back. So he puts a hand on her back. Jo is looking past his shoulder at a machine still goddamn full of plates.

She moves out from under the hand.

“You load it just like your father,” she says.

It’s a good line. She’s had it for years. It gives Sam nothing to do, since his father isn’t here to defend his silverware basket, so he goes and finds a line of his own.

“If I’m that useless, what are you doing here?”

Neither of them had planned on that sentence. It sits on the counter between the fruit bowl and the mail. The Sam in Jo’s head would have said it to win. The one in the kitchen looks as if he’d like to know.

The hand she moved out from under isn’t nothing. In 2006 James Coan and colleagues put sixteen married women in a brain scanner and warned them, on some trials, that an electric shock might be coming. Each woman went through it holding her husband’s hand, a stranger’s hand or no hand at all. With the husband’s hand, several regions of the brain responded less to the threat. The stranger’s hand did less. And the better a woman rated her marriage, the more her husband’s hand did.[5] Sixteen women and a few minutes in a scanner make a small study, and it tested one kind of threat in one kind of couple.

It still says something about what’s at stake over this dishwasher. Sam’s hand is a piece of equipment. On a bad day in a waiting room it could take some of the fear out of Jo’s body, and hers could do the same for him. How well it works seems to depend on what else has been going on between the two people attached to it, and what’s been going on tonight is this. If you’ve ever held the hand of the person who was frightening you, you’ll know why “human contact helps” needs a few more words.

Jo doesn’t answer the question. She takes a breath and gets specific, which is harder.

“Tuesday I reminded you twice. Saturday we ran out of mugs and I got off a call to wash some. I’m tired of supervising.”

I’m tired of supervising is a complaint Sam can look into. Tuesday has a date on it. Saturday has mugs. He remembers Tuesday differently, and he says so. Now they have a day and some mugs to disagree about, which beats his father.

None of this makes Jo’s reading wrong. A guess that came out of your own head can still be correct. Hans was correct again and again. Tuesday happened and so did Saturday, and two reminders in one day is something a person can count. What the horse and the experiments take away is her certainty about the reason. She knows what Sam did. She’s been supplying why.

Tell me if you want a hand

The research has a strong objection coming, and it’s standing at the sink. Ask is fine advice for Sam. For Jo, being asked is the complaint.

Suppose tomorrow night Sam hears a bowl clink and calls from the couch, kindly, “Tell me if you want a hand.” He’s remembered she was tired. He’s even halfway up. And the person who has to notice the sink, decide what it needs and recruit a helper is still Jo. His memory has worked beautifully on the wrong detail. Asking, done that way, is one more thing she has to answer.

So it matters what gets asked. The sink is in plain view. Sam doesn’t need Jo to tell him about the sink. He needs her for whatever can’t be seen from his end of the kitchen, like what done means to her, or what tonight cost her. Ask about the mind. The frying pan he can find by himself.

Sam gets there slowly, out loud. “So even if I unload it every time you ask, you still had to notice. And ask.”

She nods.

He isn’t finished, and he’s entitled not to be. He tells her he resents a job being called unfinished when nobody ever said what finished was. He writes assembly instructions for a furniture company, and every job he’s put on paper has a last step with a number on it. He cleared and scraped and loaded for an hour while she was on the phone, and he came out of it feeling like a man who’d done nothing.

“I didn’t know we had different definitions,” she says. She’s allowed to be angry about the half he left and glad of the half he did. That’s awkward for a person whose speech is still warm.

In the late 1990s Jean-Philippe Laurenceau and colleagues had people keep diaries of their everyday conversations and rate how close they felt after each one. Saying something personal went with feeling closer. So did being told something personal. A good part of the effect ran through a third thing, which was whether the speaker came away feeling understood and cared for.[6] Diaries show what tends to travel together. They can’t hand Sam a sentence that works. They do suggest where the closeness gets made. It gets made on the return trip, when what you sent comes back and you can tell it arrived.

“Do you know what I’d like back?” Jo says. “The end of that phone call. She was telling me something.”

She’d hung up on her sister in the middle of a sentence, because half her mind was on whether there’d be clean bowls in the morning. She’s on the late shift tomorrow. On those days she isn’t home till seven-thirty, and the morning is the part she gets to herself.

Sam asks what her sister was telling her.

Jo looks at him for a second. “I don’t know, Sam. That’s the part I didn’t hear.”

He has a working model of Jo’s sister too, and it has a guess ready. He keeps his head still.

The good part

Sam unloads the machine. It takes four minutes by the clock on the stove, which he notices and decides not to mention. They agree on an experiment. For one week, whoever takes the kitchen after dinner owns the whole job, including noticing. No reminders, and no supervising. They’ll see on Sunday how it went. It may go badly. Sam is forty-six, and his noticing has had most of those years off.

Nothing has been solved. The sentence on the counter is still there, and neither of them has picked it up. What they have is one new fact, two dated complaints and seven days. Couples have stayed together on less. Couples have also split up with more. If Sunday comes and the noticing is still all Jo’s, she’ll have learned something no question could have told her, and she’ll be entitled to act on it. Asking was never a promise to keep explaining away what she can count.

Jo dries one plate, puts down the towel and picks up her phone. Part of her life can’t be reconstructed from his end of the kitchen by the most attentive man alive. Her sister had a first sentence ready, and Jo had some idea where the second was headed. Certain conversations only happen when those two are alone, and Sam has never been in the room for one. He can’t read it off her face, and this time he can’t ask his way in either. She has an evening of her own going, and he has a small part in it. He can love her well enough to mind his own business for ten minutes.

He washes the cereal bowls by hand and sets them on the shelf beside the last of the plates. From the doorway he hears her say, “You were just getting to the good part.”

Half of his own kitchen was invisible to Sam while he was standing in it. Now, with his thumb and forefinger, he enlarges somebody else’s.
