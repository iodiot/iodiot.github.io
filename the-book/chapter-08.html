Chapter 08The Other Person Is Not a Supporting Character

The dishwasher is not the point

It is late. One of you is standing in the kitchen looking at the dishwasher. The other is looking at a phone. The dishwasher is full of clean dishes, which means it has not been emptied. A second load is waiting in the sink, which means nobody has loaded it. A third party, presumably the dishes themselves, has filed a complaint.

“You said you’d do this,” one of you says.

“I did do it,” the other replies. “I ran it.”

“You know what I mean.”

And now the conversation is no longer about plates. It is about whether anyone sees how much work you do, whether promises count if completed at the wrong stage, whether love should come with a user manual, and whether your partner has always been selfish or merely became selfish after the one time you forgot their birthday in 2019.

The dishes were real. So was the fatigue. But the argument has grown a second body: the story each of you is telling about what the other person’s behavior means. One person hears “I’m not doing this for you” inside a complaint about chores. The other hears “You are incompetent and disappointing” inside a request to unload the machine. Soon each is defending against an accusation the other may not have made.

We are social animals with private access to only one mind: our own, and even that one keeps a suspicious amount of information behind a locked door. Other people arrive through words, gestures, timing, silence, choices, and the occasional message that says “fine” with the emotional warmth of a parking ticket. We infer what they feel and mean. We need to infer; life would be tedious if every interaction required a signed affidavit. But inference is not access. The person across from you is not a supporting character in the film your mind is making about you.

This chapter is about the strange work of being close to creatures you cannot directly inspect. It is about empathy without pretending to be telepathic, conflict without instant character assassination, care that does not erase boundaries, and the small acts that make people feel understood. The aim is not to become perfect at relationships. It is to stop treating our guesses about other people as if they arrived with official stamps.

Your mind is a confident interpreter

When someone behaves in a way that hurts, the mind is quick to fill in a reason. They did not call: I do not matter. They corrected you in front of the group: they wanted to humiliate you. They made plans without you: they are replacing you. Sometimes one of these interpretations is right. People can be careless, cruel, avoidant, manipulative, or simply uninterested. Being open-minded does not require you to keep explaining away a pattern that is harming you.

But a single event rarely comes with subtitles. The fact is what happened. The motive is a hypothesis. We tend to collapse them, perhaps because uncertainty is uncomfortable and a bad explanation can feel better than no explanation at all. “They forgot” feels different from “They don’t care.” The second sentence hurts more, but it also feels complete. It offers the bitter comfort of a verdict.

Research on perspective taking suggests that we often begin from our own viewpoint and adjust toward another person’s. In one set of experiments, Nicholas Epley and colleagues found that judgments about another person’s perspective were anchored in one’s own and then corrected, often insufficiently. Time pressure increased the bias; incentives for accuracy reduced it. This does not mean we are doomed to selfishness or incapable of understanding anyone. It means that understanding takes work, and our first estimate may be more autobiography than observation.

Say you tell a friend, “You seem quiet.” You may mean concern. They may hear criticism because they have spent the day being told they are too sensitive. You may see a short reply and assume anger because you are already afraid of being rejected. They may be walking through an airport with one hand on a suitcase and the other trying to type “I’ll call you after I land.” Your mind does not know this until it asks or waits for evidence. It can, however, generate twenty-seven theories before the elevator reaches the lobby.

This is why “I know exactly how you feel” is often meant kindly and can still be a small theft. You may know something about the territory. You may have lost someone, been embarrassed, been left out, or cared for a person in pain. But their experience is not yours with a different coat on. Similarity can make empathy easier; it can also make projection faster. The more certain you feel that you understand, the more useful it may be to ask what part of the story belongs to them.

A better sentence is sometimes less impressive: “I have an idea, but I could be wrong. Is this close?” Or: “Do you want advice, help, or just company?” These are not magic phrases. They are small admissions that another mind exists outside your interpretation. That is an astonishing achievement for a species that can turn a three-word text into an international tribunal.

Empathy is not mind-reading

Empathy is a crowded word. It can mean noticing another person’s emotion, imagining their point of view, sharing some of their feeling, caring about their welfare, or trying to respond helpfully. These abilities overlap, but they are not identical. You can recognize that someone is terrified and still not care what happens to them. You can care deeply and misunderstand what they need. You can take someone’s perspective so intensely that you lose track of your own.

This is one reason empathy should not be treated as a mystical moral superpower. It is a set of human capacities with limits, habits, and blind spots. We are often more attuned to people who resemble us, people we love, people whose pain is visible, and people whose story we can follow. We can feel more for one person in front of us than for a much larger group we cannot picture. Emotion brings moral attention into focus; it does not guarantee a fair map of the whole situation.

In ordinary relationships, the practical failure is often not a lack of compassion but a rush to solve. Your friend tells you she is exhausted by work. You produce a list of options: quit, talk to your manager, update the résumé, take a course, start a newsletter about work-life balance. She says, “I can’t just quit.” You feel frustrated because you offered four solutions and she rejected all of them. She feels alone because she tried to show you the weight of the problem and you immediately started rearranging the furniture inside it.

Advice can be useful. It can also be a way to escape the discomfort of witnessing pain you cannot remove. If you fix the problem quickly, you do not have to sit next to someone’s helplessness—or your own. But another person may be asking for recognition before strategy: “That sounds exhausting. What part is hardest right now?” Listening is not a passive activity. It is information gathering, emotional restraint, and the decision not to make your cleverness the main character.

Nor does empathy require agreement. You can understand why a sibling is furious and still think they are wrong about what happened. You can care about someone while refusing to lie for them, lend more money, tolerate an insult, or pretend that a harmful pattern is harmless. Understanding a motive is not the same as granting permission. A person’s history can explain the shape of their behavior without making you responsible for absorbing its effects.

Sometimes the most empathic thing is a question. Sometimes it is a boundary. Sometimes it is saying, “I love you, and I’m not going to keep having this conversation while you’re calling me names.” Compassion without reality becomes indulgence. Reality without compassion becomes a courtroom. Relationships need both: the willingness to imagine another person’s experience and the willingness to name what is happening in yours.

We lend each other nervous systems

A child falls and looks toward a parent before deciding how bad the fall was. A friend’s face changes when you say the thing you were scared to say. Someone waits with you outside a hospital room. A partner puts a hand on your back while you try to explain why you are frightened. The event remains; the company changes how alone you are inside it.

Social contact is not just decoration around individual life. Other people can influence our attention, stress, behavior, and sense of what is manageable. In a 2006 fMRI experiment, James Coan and colleagues studied sixteen married women who anticipated a possible electric shock while holding a spouse’s hand, a stranger’s hand, or no hand. Threat-related neural responses were attenuated most broadly during spouse handholding, with a more limited attenuation during stranger handholding. The study was small and specific: married women, a laboratory threat, a hand to hold. It does not prove that every relationship is calming, that every stranger is useless, or that human beings need romantic partners to regulate themselves. It does suggest that, in that setting, social contact changed the response to an anticipated threat.

The reverse is familiar too. Exclusion can hurt with a force out of proportion to the apparent event. You are not invited. A group chat continues without you. At a party, everyone turns toward someone else and you stand holding a glass of warm wine like a prop abandoned after filming. Your mind may know that one missed invitation does not establish your value. Your body may still react as though access to the group has become uncertain.

In an early fMRI study, Naomi Eisenberger, Matthew Lieberman, and Kipling Williams had participants play a virtual ball-tossing game in which they were eventually excluded. The researchers observed activity in areas they associated with the distress of social exclusion and compared it with activity discussed in physical pain research. The result was influential, but it should not be flattened into the slogan “rejection is literally the same as physical pain.” Brain regions are involved in many processes; a scan does not translate an experience into a simple label. The safer conclusion is that social exclusion can produce measurable distress, and the brain’s responses to social and physical events can overlap in ways that require careful interpretation.

None of this means people are helpless without other people. We can self-soothe, think, move, and build routines. Nor does every social bond make life better. Some relationships are frightening, degrading, or exhausting. “Humans need connection” is not a command to stay connected to whoever is currently hurting you. It is a description of a broad vulnerability: being treated as if you belong can matter deeply, and being excluded can matter even when you are trying to act above it.

The stubborn part is that we do not always know what kind of support someone needs. A hand, a question, a meal, a distraction, a ride, some silence, or a firm “you need to call the doctor” can help in different circumstances. Caring does not make you automatically accurate. Ask. Pay attention to the answer. Remember what helped last time without assuming the same thing will help today.

Closeness is built in the return

People sometimes describe intimacy as total access: the right person should know what you need before you ask; a good partner should see through your defenses; a real friend should never make you explain the same thing twice. It sounds romantic in the abstract and exhausting in a kitchen at 10:55 p.m. No one can be constantly available, perfectly observant, and permanently fluent in your private emotional dialect. Even you are not.

Closeness is less like mind-melding and more like a repeated exchange. You reveal something that matters. The other person responds in a way that makes you feel noticed, respected, and taken seriously. Then roles change. This can happen in a long conversation, but it also happens in dozens of short moments: a person remembers how your meeting went; asks what you meant before judging; makes space for your answer; follows through on a promise; tells you when they cannot show up instead of disappearing and letting you write the ending.

Jean-Philippe Laurenceau and colleagues tested an interpersonal model of intimacy using diary reports collected after everyday interactions over one- or two-week periods. Across their studies, self-disclosure and perceived partner responsiveness were associated with intimacy at the level of interactions; emotional disclosure was a stronger predictor than disclosure of facts in one study. These were diary studies of reported interactions, not proof that a particular sentence causes intimacy for everyone. Still, they point toward an ordinary truth: intimacy is not just what you reveal. It is also what happens when the other person receives it.

Responsiveness is not agreeing with every feeling or saying the perfect thing. It is a recognizable effort to respond to the person in front of you. “I can see why that landed badly.” “I don’t remember it the same way, but I want to understand.” “I’m too tired to talk properly now; can we do it after breakfast?” The last sentence may be more loving than a tired performance of attention followed by resentment. Clarity is a form of care when it prevents the other person from having to live inside your silence and guess what it means.

A relationship also needs room for mistakes. A missed cue is not automatically evidence of indifference. A bad day does not prove a bad character. What matters is what happens after the miss: can you talk about it, take in the other person’s account, apologize where you caused harm, and make a different attempt? Repair is not a ceremonial phrase delivered to reset the relationship to factory settings. It is an effort to understand what happened and adjust what happens next.

Of course, repeated apologies with no change are not repair. “I’m sorry you feel that way” is often an attempt to return the discomfort to sender. If someone asks you to keep accepting the same harm because they have explained it beautifully, the explanation has become part of the machinery. Patterns deserve more weight than speeches. An isolated failure can be human. A pattern is information.

Conflict is a collision of stories

Conflict often begins with an event and expands into a theory of the person. “You interrupted me” becomes “You never respect me.” “You forgot to call” becomes “I can’t count on you.” “You asked where I was” becomes “You’re trying to control me.” Sometimes the bigger pattern is true. Sometimes a painful event has recruited the word always because always feels like armor. Either way, once a conversation becomes a trial of someone’s entire character, the original issue has to compete with the need to win the trial.

A specific complaint gives the other person something they can understand and respond to: “When you joked about my job at dinner, I felt embarrassed. Please don’t do that again.” A global indictment offers a verdict: “You humiliate me because you need everyone to think you’re smarter.” That verdict may feel emotionally precise. It is also a claim about motive that you may not be able to prove. If the motive matters, ask. If the behavior matters, name the behavior.

The same principle applies inward. “I shut down when the conversation gets tense” can open a question: what happens in my body, what am I afraid of, what could help me stay present? “I’m impossible to love” turns a response pattern into a whole identity. Identity verdicts feel final. Behaviors are more workable. This does not let anyone off the hook; it gives responsibility a shape that can be acted on.

A useful conflict does not require both people to feel equally satisfied at every second. It requires enough safety and honesty to address the actual issue. Sometimes the issue is a practical agreement: who does which task and when. Sometimes it is a repeated imbalance that requires changing the arrangement. Sometimes the relationship is not safe enough for direct confrontation, and the priority is outside support and protection. Advice to “communicate better” can be useless or dangerous when one person uses communication to punish the other for speaking.

When direct conversation is possible, try to separate four things that are often fused: what happened, what you felt, what you inferred, and what you want now. “You left while I was talking” is an event. “I felt dismissed” is an experience. “You don’t care what I think” is an interpretation. “I want you to tell me if you need to leave, and let me finish this point later” is a request. A request can be refused. That may hurt, but it is more honest than disguising a demand as a test of love.

Listening also means repeating back what you understand before building your defense. It does not mean surrendering your account. “So you felt cornered when I asked again after you said no. Is that right?” lets the other person correct you. If you hear “yes,” you can then add your own experience. This is a small procedural change, not a peace treaty. It can slow the conversation down enough for two people to be present in the same room instead of arguing with their private imagined versions of each other.

A boundary is not a punishment

A boundary is sometimes described as a rule you impose on someone else: “You are not allowed to make me uncomfortable.” This is impossible. Other people can disappoint you, disagree, say no, and make choices you do not like. A boundary is more usefully understood as a statement of what you will participate in and what you will do to protect your own limits.

“You have to stop being angry” tries to control another person’s inner life. “I’m going to step away if this turns into yelling” describes your action. “You can’t see those friends” is a command. “I won’t stay in a relationship where I’m expected to cut off my friends” is a limit on what you will accept. A boundary can still be communicated badly or used as a threat. Calling something a boundary does not make it virtuous. But a clear limit should concern your own participation, safety, time, body, or resources—not the fantasy that you can manage another adult into never upsetting you.

Boundaries are also not punishments for having feelings. If your partner is jealous, the answer is not automatically to prove your innocence by surrendering privacy. If your friend is hurt, you can listen without agreeing to every demand that follows. If a family member is angry when you decline a request, their anger does not by itself prove your boundary is cruel. Discomfort is not always a sign that someone did something wrong. It may be the sensation of a familiar arrangement changing.

At the same time, boundaries do not make every decision morally equivalent. You can set a limit to avoid accountability: “My boundary is that you never criticize me.” That is not protection; it is insulation. If you caused harm, the other person may need room to name it. A healthy boundary leaves both people’s realities intact. It says, “I can hear what I did, and I won’t stay for abuse,” or “I can support you, and I cannot be your only support.” It does not require one person to disappear so the other can feel comfortable.

The hard part is following through. If you state a limit and never act on it, the other person may learn that the words are scenery. If you state it in the heat of anger and use the relationship as leverage, you may create fear rather than clarity. Good boundaries are usually less theatrical: clear in advance, proportionate, and connected to something you can actually do. “If you keep texting after I’ve asked for the night alone, I won’t answer until tomorrow.” Ordinary sentence. No fog machine required.

Care without rescue

When someone you love is struggling, your own helplessness can turn into a project. You want to make them better quickly because their pain hurts you too. You begin monitoring, reminding, advising, arranging, and checking. You take over decisions they could make, then feel resentful that you are doing all the work. They feel managed, and you feel unappreciated. The rescue has quietly become a relationship in which one person is the patient and the other is an underpaid operations department.

Support is not the same as taking responsibility for another adult’s entire life. You can accompany someone to an appointment, help them make a plan, listen when they call, or say clearly that a behavior is harming you. You cannot guarantee that they seek treatment, stop drinking, leave a bad job, accept your advice, or become a more comfortable person to love. Wanting a different outcome does not give you control over the other person’s choices.

This limit can feel like abandonment, especially if you learned that love means anticipating needs and preventing every crisis. But care without control is possible. “I care about you, and I’m not able to lend you more money.” “I’ll help you look for a therapist; I can’t be the only person you call in a crisis.” “I want to talk about what happened, but not while either of us is intoxicated.” These sentences do not guarantee that the other person will like you. They let care and limitation occupy the same life.

Receiving help can be difficult too. If you have learned that needing someone makes you weak, you may reject ordinary kindness and then feel alone. If you have learned that love must be earned, you may perform competence until you collapse in a bathroom with the ventilation fan on, because the fan is the only friend who has never asked what is wrong. Asking for support is not a contract that gives the other person ownership of you. It is an invitation, and the other person is allowed to answer honestly.

There is no relationship with perfect symmetry. One person will need more on Tuesday. The other will need more in March. Health, money, children, grief, work, and chance alter what each can give. Fairness is not a spreadsheet with equal columns every day. But chronic one-way care is still a fact, and love does not make it sustainable forever. A close relationship should have some capacity for both people to matter, even when the balance is uneven for a time.

Let the other person be real

One of the most respectful things you can do is allow someone else to be different from the person you need them to be. Your friend may not want advice. Your partner may need quiet before talking. Your child may not share your ambitions. Your parent may never offer the apology you have imagined. A colleague may have a reason you do not know and still be behaving unfairly. Real people are more inconvenient than our theories about them because they have their own histories, limits, preferences, and days that do not orbit our needs.

That freedom has a cost. You may ask clearly and hear no. You may explain how something affected you and discover that the other person does not want to change. You may offer a repair and not be forgiven. You may give someone room and watch them choose a different life. Respect is not a down payment that entitles you to closeness. But without respect, the closeness you get may be obedience, fear, or a carefully managed performance.

This is why relationships cannot be secured by perfect interpretation. You will misunderstand people. They will misunderstand you. You will sometimes be the person who makes a careless assumption and the person who has to correct one. The work is to keep the misunderstanding revisable: to ask, to listen, to state what you know, to own what you did, and to pay attention to whether the same injury keeps returning.

You are not responsible for reading every mind. You are responsible for what you do with the evidence you have. You can say what you need without making the other person guess. You can hear what they need without automatically granting it. You can notice a feeling without turning it into a motive report. You can care without disappearing. You can leave when staying requires you to become smaller or less safe.

The dishwasher still needs emptying. One of you can do it tonight, or you can agree on a better arrangement tomorrow. But the question beneath the dishes is not solved by correctly identifying which person is the villain. It is whether two people can make the invisible work visible, say what they mean, and build a way of living that neither has to silently resent.

That is already a lot to ask of creatures who cannot even read each other’s minds. It is also how intimacy becomes real: not through perfect access, but through repeated attempts to meet someone who remains separate from you. And once we step beyond the couple, family, or friendship, the same need for recognition grows larger. We do not only want to belong to people. We want to know what kind of “we” we are part of, and what it asks us to become.
