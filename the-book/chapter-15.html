Chapter 15Probability Doesn't Care About Your Plans

The chief witness for the prosecution is a kitchen sponge. Dan’s youngest holds it up in the courtyard so that everybody can see it’s dry. She left it on the windowsill for the whole film show. At no point did the heavens deposit so much as a damp freckle upon it.

“And how much did you pay for the room?”

“Sixty pounds,” says Fran.

The child looks from the sponge to the hall and back again. She has recently learned to multiply by ten. “That’s a lot of ice creams.”

Fran would like to appeal to a higher court, preferably one sitting before Friday at six, when she had to decide whether to book the hall. Back then the chief witness was under the sink and had nothing to contribute.

A thin strip of Sunday evening light still hangs above the roofs. The neighbours have had a pretty good time. A kitchen colander has enjoyed two minutes and eleven seconds of local celebrity. Someone who recognised the striped sock would like to know whether it’s available for other work. There’s enough money in the tin to settle the bills. There would be sixty pounds more if Fran had done nothing on Friday about the possibility of rain. The girl is perfectly right about the ice creams.

We’re going to give this case a hearing. You may wish to remember what you think about those sixty pounds right now, with the sponge in evidence. It’s remarkably easy to revise an opinion and then remember having held the revised version since breakfast.

Friday, while the sky still has its options

There’s room for everyone in the hall as well as outside. If they book the hall outright and move the whole show indoors now, they can spare themselves the entire conversation. Quite a few people want the outdoor screening, though. They arranged to bring rugs. Some live in little flats and fancy an evening watching films through the gaps between other people’s heads under an actual sky. Fran’s dad has submitted an alien film and would enjoy hearing his audience laugh within sight of the washing line on which the action unfolded. Fair enough. A neighbourhood is allowed a preference more demanding than remaining dry.

The hall manager offers them a choice. For £60 now, she’ll keep the indoor space free on Sunday. If they need to use it, there’s another £80 for the caretaker and changeover. If they turn down the offer, she’ll release the booking. They can stay outdoors for no extra charge in decent weather. If rain makes it impossible to show the films outside, they’ve promised the contributors another date. Rebooking the projectionist and equipment comes to £300. The volunteers have checked the quotes. Both rooms have enough seats and a step-free entrance. All the ordinary costs of putting on the original show are the same whichever decision they make. Those are already covered. What’s at issue on Friday is the extra cash they might be committing.

This is a made-up film night. The prices and the weather estimate belong to this courtyard. We get to do something unusually generous for people with an opinion about somebody else’s decision. We can sit in the room with the information they had before they made it.

Fran has the forecast open on her phone. There are percentages attached to little raindrops at different hours of the evening. One of the helpers asks a good question. Is a forty percent chance of rain a forty percent chance the show has to move? The UK Met Office says the percentage on its hourly display refers to the chance of at least 0.1 millimetres of precipitation during that hour at the forecast location.[1] Such a shower might have finished by the time anybody tries to plug a projector in. Rain at four can leave chairs wet at seven. Drizzle during the reels might come in under the canopy, or it might not matter at all. There’s no icon for woman in fourth row beginning to regret wearing linen.

They’d like a probability for a more specific event. Will the weather prevent the screening from going ahead in the courtyard? They talk to the person running the equipment, look at the hourly pattern and consider how much rain the site can take before an indoor move is necessary. Their working estimate is about one chance in three. Fran writes down 35 percent so they can run some numbers, with a note that she wouldn’t be astonished if a better estimate put it anywhere from a quarter to nearly a half. That 35 is a judgement. No public meteorological body has issued a forecast concerning their chairs.

Already two sensible people have managed to disagree. One adds up two forty percent symbols and gets an eighty percent chance of rain at some point. Another figures that if the odds in both hours are the same there’s no reason to count the second hour at all. Consider two imaginary hours, each with a forty percent chance. If the very same persistent cloud makes it rain in both or in neither, the chance of getting wet at some point is forty percent. If the hours are independent, the chance both stay dry is 0.6 times 0.6, which is 0.36. That leaves sixty-four percent for rain in at least one. If all the showers in the first hour and all the showers in the second belonged to entirely different evenings in a long run of cases, the two forty percents would indeed add to eighty. Each answer comes with a theory about what the clouds are doing. The symbols on a screen haven’t told you which theory you’re entitled to. British rain, on the whole, does not disappear on the hour to allow a statistically independent successor to take up its duties.

Beside Fran there’s a boy trying to work out whether he’s allowed a second biscuit. Nobody wishes to model that decision as carefully. He eats it.

Fran turns over a programme. Suppose they run a hundred imaginary Sundays with exactly their current estimate. Put sixty-five dry-enough ones to the left, thirty-five washed-out ones to the right. If they skip the backup, the ones on the left cost nothing extra. The thirty-five on the right cost £300 apiece. That’s £10,500 in this entirely fictitious collection of evenings, or £105 of extra cost per Sunday on average.

Now buy the backup every time. Sixty pounds on each of the hundred Sundays makes £6,000. On the thirty-five wet evenings they use the hall, which adds £2,800 at £80 a time. Total £8,800. Average extra cost £88 a Sunday. At the estimate they’ve chosen, booking the option costs £17 less on average.

An awkward silence follows. There’s something peculiarly unsatisfying about promising people a saving of seventeen pounds on an evening when there is absolutely no chance of saving seventeen pounds. One of the options is going to leave the organisers either £60 worse off than the alternative if the weather holds, or £160 better off if it doesn’t. They can’t put an average in the petty cash tin. It won’t rattle.

Dan’s daughter has been colouring in the hundred imaginary evenings. She asks whether they could do the show on one of the sixty-five clear ones. At least one grown-up in the room has been thinking essentially the same thing in a more expensive vocabulary.

How wrong can you afford to be?

The useful number on the programme is the one they work out next. The hall fee is £60 whether they go inside or not. If the weather makes a move necessary, it lets them avoid £300 of rebooking at the cost of £80 for the changeover. Every evening that would otherwise be washed out makes the option worth £220 before its fee. How often would they need those £220 savings to justify paying £60 up front? Divide 60 by 220. A little over 27 percent.

If one in four comparable screenings would be washed out, skipping the option costs £75 on average, a quarter of £300. Buying it costs £80, made up of the £60 fee and a quarter of the £80 changeover. At those odds the arithmetic favours taking their chances, by a fiver. At 45 percent it favours the backup by £39. You can move from one recommendation to the other while having a perfectly civil disagreement about the amount of rain this particular courtyard can tolerate. Some important arguments get a great deal more bearable as soon as you can say what, approximately, you’d have to learn to change your mind.

The treasurer thinks a quarter is closer. He’s operated the projector here before. They kept going through a shower last summer by bringing the front row under cover. The folding chairs dried quickly. He’s got evidence for that view, and he has a use for the £60. He wants to pay the woman who captions the locally made films a bit more than the near-insult they’ve managed this time. He did some of the timing himself and discovered that subtitling a three-minute conversation involves listening to several people say yeah until you no longer believe in the existence of language.

Fran thinks they’re nearer to thirty-five percent. Last summer’s shower was in daylight during a shorter event. Sunday’s screening is due to run into the evening and they need a usable stretch long enough for all six films. She would quite like the woman doing the captions to be paid more as well. They don’t get to solve this by assigning all the modern virtues to the person with the hall key.

There’s a further number in the tin. They have £310 in reserve for last-minute expenses. They could survive the £300 rebooking, just. Ten pounds would be left to absorb absolutely anything else that happened. They’d be placing an unusual degree of faith in the mechanical integrity of several items of donated equipment, one of which currently stands level courtesy of a beer mat. Booking the backup puts a ceiling of £140 on the extra weather bill under their arrangement. In the bad-weather case that would leave £170. To somebody who has to keep a film club going past next Tuesday, the difference between ten pounds and a hundred and seventy is more interesting than it looks to a person doing the averages on a generous supply of imaginary Sundays.

Put it this way. Would you take a bet on a fair coin that pays you £110 for heads and costs you £100 for tails? Your average gain over an enormous run of bets at those terms would be £5 per toss. You might enjoy it with money you set aside for the pleasure of occasionally saying told you so to a round piece of metal. What if the hundred in your hand pays for your train home and nobody at the station is interested in average trains? Same metal. Same probabilities. A person who turns it down has understood something about the problem that the coin will never need to learn.

Or turn the committee’s position round. Suppose they have £60 and absolutely no possibility of finding another penny. Putting down the reservation would buy an indoor option they couldn’t exercise, because the £80 for opening the hall would be beyond them. And their promise to spend £300 on a rescheduled evening would be a piece of community theatre considerably more ambitious than anything on the programme. Before calculating the likely value of your available choices, make sure the ones on the page are choices you actually have.

Fran phones the hall manager. Could they wait until the next morning, when there’d be a fresher forecast? No. Somebody wants the hall and needs an answer tonight. Could they pay the £60 and get it back if the manager finds another booking after they release it? Also no. The manager sounds mildly amused. She’s being asked to provide exactly the thing she charges £60 for, with the agreeable possibility of never receiving £60.

It’s worth noticing what Fran has done. She has looked for a way to change the options before treating them as laws of nature. Nothing doing on this occasion. Elsewhere, a small phone call can do more for your odds than a much more detailed conversation with your forehead.

At ten minutes to six the volunteers take a vote. Two want to book, one would take the chance outdoors. They reserve the hall. The treasurer makes a note of his reason for voting against. Fran writes down her estimate, the price of a cancellation and the minimum they’d like left in the tin. She adds a sentence about the captions. Their next budget ought to take the woman’s work seriously before the last-minute money is up for grabs. Then she puts the programme in an envelope and writes Friday on it. On Sunday night they can see whether the treasurer was right. Or rather, they can see what happened. It takes Fran a moment to recognise those as different appointments.

An experiment in being wise afterwards

While the envelope is closed, imagine two committees in adjoining towns. Same forecast, same money, same estimates, same vote. Both book the hall. On Sunday the first town gets rain so hard the water bounces off the paving and goes back upstairs for something it forgot. Children watch films indoors. People congratulate the organisers on their foresight. In the second town there’s a fine evening and a small person conducts an audit with a sponge.

Which committee would you like in charge of the next event? If you find your hand edging towards the wet town, look again at what distinguished the two groups at ten to six on Friday. The weather boundary runs down a road between the towns. You appear to have established a preference for a set of minutes taken on the western side of a roundabout.

Knowing how things come out interferes very efficiently with our ability to judge the person who didn’t know. Jonathan Baron and John Hershey explored this in five experiments published in 1988. Students judged decisions involving medical scenarios or monetary gambles. The researchers gave them the information available to the person choosing, and told them the outcome. Better outcomes tended to draw better evaluations of the thinking, the decision maker, or the willingness to trust that person with another choice. In the gambles, even hearing that the rejected option had done badly could improve the rating of the choice that was made.[2]

Consider the exquisite unfairness of that last bit. I decide to leave my money in my pocket. The thing I declined to buy promptly drops in value. You may now think better of my reasoning. The same thing trebles in value and your opinion deteriorates. I have spent both afternoons asleep on the very same sofa. My reputation has been out having adventures of its own.

Of course results sometimes tell you a great deal about the person who chose. If the roof leaks and you discover your builder fitted all the tiles upside down, do feel free to take a lively interest in the outcome. You have learned something inspectable about the connection between their work and the wet patch above your bed. If the carpenter down the road has put up forty roofs in the same weather and yours alone gives you the opportunity to wash your hair during breakfast, that also merits a look. The mistake would be to treat a single wet pillow as proof, by itself, that a suitably placed person ought to have known a branch would come through the skylight at four in the morning. Start with the wet pillow. Look up.

At the cinema, by half past seven, reality is showing an almost offensive commitment to the dry version of events. People have brought cushions. The treasurer is marking the subtitled films on his older neighbour’s programme. The woman herself, Eileen, has put on lipstick and brought a coat in an optimistic shade of cream. She’s here to see a film her grandson made about what happens to your bicycle if you leave it under a tree full of pigeons. It has very little dialogue. She thinks she’ll be all right.

Dad has spent the day claiming complete indifference to the reception of his picture. He has subsequently asked four separate people how many places they’ve set out, an interesting level of operational engagement for a man at peace with his art. He sits beside his daughter. When the gloved hand appears he folds his own hands under his coat. The audience laughs in just the place he’d hoped for. He never turns his head. Fran can tell from the little dip of his chin how much it mattered that they did.

A person could improve the hygiene of decision making considerably by eradicating every feeling of this kind. Make a film. Evaluate the information available when you made it. Decline to care whether anybody laughs. What a magnificently well-calibrated way to die of boredom. Outcomes matter. That’s why taking a chance hurts. If you and your partner decide to move closer to someone you love and six months later they have to move again, you are allowed to sit on the stairs of your well-chosen new house and be thoroughly bloody miserable. Knowing you did the right thing may be of some comfort later. Just now it’s six in the evening and you were counting on having their kids round for tea.

You may also decide to move back. An earlier choice made on good evidence doesn’t acquire lifelong custody of you. There’s fresh information. It goes on the new page. The household that made the first move and the household packing for the second can both be composed of reasonably intelligent adults, although by the third encounter with the self-assembly wardrobe they may have considerably revised their opinion of the species.

Perhaps this is the cruelest misuse of hindsight. A person has lost the thing they hoped for, and now has to defend having hoped for it. In our gallery of imaginary Sundays we could walk down the row and visit all their other lives. In the one they actually have, the next-door kids aren’t coming after all. Before offering an opinion on the quality of their planning, it might be nice to ask whether they’d like some help carrying a wardrobe.

Keep the bad forecasts

Back in the dry courtyard, Dan’s daughter is still staring at the numbers on the programme. If a thirty-five percent prediction can be followed by rain and it can be followed by sunshine, how does anybody ever have to admit to being wrong? Her father once explained that the answer to whether she could get a rabbit was possibly. She has not forgotten what an exceptionally versatile word that turned out to be.

It’s a serious objection. Somebody could put thirty-five percent on every picnic from now until the planet becomes uninhabitable, then greet every passing shower and every sunburnt relative with the words as predicted. They owe us a way to tell when they’re doing a good job. In 1950 the meteorologist Glenn Brier published a way of scoring forecasts that attach probabilities to events.[3] Here’s the commonly used version for a yes-or-no event. Write zero for no, one for yes. Subtract what happened from the probability you gave, then square the difference. A smaller score is better.

Give a washout a probability of 0.35 on an evening the show goes ahead and your score is 0.35 squared, or 0.1225. If the screening is washed out, you get the square of the gap between 0.35 and 1, which is 0.4225. Give a washout a probability of zero and you’ll score a perfect zero on the dry evening. You’ll take the full one on the wet one. A person who is prepared to be absolutely certain about everything will have a number of occasions on which to give an extremely good speech. Keep the other speeches too.

Why square the miss? Here’s one thing it buys you. Suppose across a long succession of similar calls the event really does happen thirty-five times in every hundred. Always put down zero and your average score is 0.35. Put down 0.35 each time and you average about 0.228, multiplying the dry score by 0.65 and the wet one by 0.35 and adding them. Plainly you’d do better still if you could reliably distinguish more dangerous days from safer ones and adjust your estimate accordingly. What you can’t do is beat an honest estimate, on average under those very odds, merely by saying zero more emphatically. The squaring in the calculation rewards something rather different from the speech.

Rain at a weather station offers a long record of measurable outcomes. A woman with an envelope who helps to organise an annual neighbourhood film show may need until the heat death of the universe to assemble a sample her statistician is happy with. Even so, she can do better than walking around on Monday remembering she was vaguely concerned about something wet. She has written down what would count as a washout. She can keep each call, even the dull ones. She can ask whether future estimates are using conditions at the right hour. The next committee can see where her number came from and, if it has a better one, say so before the first umbrella goes up.

They open the Friday envelope. The treasurer still thinks twenty-five percent was nearer. Dry weather does fit that estimate a little better than it fits thirty-five percent, just as it fits a lunatic issuing a lifetime guarantee of no more rain better than either. One Sunday gives the treasurer a small piece of evidence. It gives the lunatic an excellent photograph for his brochure.

Fran has one fresh fact that alters her view of the event quite a lot. The first film had opened with pale lettering. At the back of the courtyard, while there was still light in the sky, Eileen couldn’t read it against the screen. Fran only finds out because she asks Eileen what she made of her grandson’s title. The old woman tells her the title must have been splendid from the front. At the back she saw a rectangle changing its mind about which shade of grey it would like to be.

This has a different bearing on the organisers’ judgement from the unused hall. They could have tested pale lettering at the actual start time from the actual back row. They tested at night on Thursday from three chairs away. The weather gamble was recognised and priced. The hope that everybody could read the screen was an assumption about the audience that nobody had bothered to inspect. A dry evening has given the organisers a good hard piece of news. It concerns a mistake almost none of the people congratulating them have noticed.

If they want to do better, they could go out just before dusk with a test slide and ask someone to stand at the back. Costs practically nothing. They could change the title contrast with the filmmaker’s permission. They could start later, although Eileen has to get home and would have something to say about a group of younger people spending the last bus on her behalf. There is a manageable problem here. By now most of the committee is enjoying the much bigger and quite insoluble argument about whether the sky validated their individual character. Fran writes ask Eileen on the back of the programme before joining in.

The price of the key

The hall manager wants the key back. Dan’s daughter offers to come too. She’s still got the sponge. The hall has that peculiar washed-floor smell of an institution unsullied by actual human use. Fran turns the lights on to check the doors, then off again on the way out. A small torch clicks on behind her.

On the far wall an animal appears. Its ears are different lengths. One of them bends right over and the animal makes a series of chewing movements with what is clearly a little girl’s thumb. For a few seconds the custodians of sixty pounds of entirely unused floor space are having a private screening of Rabbit Eats Its Own Foot.

“Would you pay sixty pounds to see that?” the girl asks.

“I’d probably wait for it to come out on television.”

There’s no need to pretend the rabbit made the reservation pay for itself. They spent the money. The treasurer’s objection remains good. The woman who did the captions could have used sixty actual pounds yesterday. It would be dishonest to dismiss that because some imaginary wet Fran saved £160 in a courtyard nobody had to clean up. All the same, the responsibility on Friday belonged to a woman who didn’t know which courtyard was coming. She paid the fee to secure a genuine possibility of opening these doors, under conditions when the possibility meant something. If they had known for certain the show could go ahead outdoors, they would have saved the money and everybody would have agreed about it, including the rabbit.

Dan’s daughter hands her the sponge. “I’d have bought the ice creams.”

“How many would you buy next year, then? Tell me before you know how many people are coming.”

The child considers this. “Can I ask them first?”

This is by some distance the most promising contribution anybody has made to the next committee meeting.

On their way out they pass Fran’s dad carrying the colander and one of the folding chairs. He has been approached about expanding the film into a series. The suggestion came from a person who has no intention of dressing up in rubber gloves on a Sunday and is therefore in an ideal position to commit the filmmakers to a body of work. Dad rather likes the thought. He also likes the idea of coming to someone else’s show next year and sitting at the back where he can put his feet up. For the moment he has left both futures in excellent condition by saying he’ll think about it.

At the gate Eileen is waiting for her grandson. He’s got the bicycle film on his phone. Fran asks if they can see the first few seconds again. There in the dark, in letters the boy has actually drawn himself, is the title his grandmother missed. My Brother Said It Was a Good Place to Leave It. Eileen laughs hard enough to get lipstick on her sleeve.

“Put me wherever you like next time,” she tells Fran. “Just come and sit with me for the first one.”

Fran writes it down underneath Friday’s numbers. Then she returns the hall key. They can’t have the sixty pounds back. They can do something about the seat.
