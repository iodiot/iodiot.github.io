Chapter 15Probability Doesn't Care About Your Plans

The chief witness for the prosecution is a kitchen sponge. June, who lives next door to Fran, holds it up in the courtyard behind the community center so everybody can see it’s dry. She left it on a windowsill for the whole film night. At no point did the heavens deposit so much as a damp freckle on it.

“How much was the room?”

“Sixty dollars,” says Fran. She’s still holding the key. Nobody went in.

June looks from the sponge to the building and back again. “That’s a lot of ice cream.”

It’s more than that. Until Friday the film group owned $310 in a coffee can, and that was all it owned. Fran spent sixty of it on a room that stayed dark, for what was probably the last film night this courtyard will see. She would like to appeal to a higher court, preferably one sitting before Friday at six, when she had to decide. Back then the chief witness was under a sink and had nothing to contribute.

A thin strip of Sunday evening light still hangs above the roofs, about all that late September can spare at this hour. The neighbors have had a good time. A colander has enjoyed two minutes and eleven seconds of local celebrity. Somebody who recognized the striped sock wants to know if it’s available for other work. The can holds enough to settle the bills. It would hold sixty dollars more if Fran had done nothing on Friday about the possibility of rain.

We’re going to give this case a hearing. Before we do, make a note of what you think about those sixty dollars right now, with the sponge in evidence. It’s easy to revise an opinion and then remember having held the revised version since breakfast.

Friday, while the sky still has its options

The notice went up on the center’s front door the Tuesday before the show. The city’s recreation department is short of money. Starting January 1, the building will close at five. Evening programs are invited to submit attendance figures “for review.” Bev, the custodian, read it while she was unlocking the door it was taped to, which is how she learned what was happening to her evening hours.

So on Friday the three people with a key sit down knowing a few things. An outdoor film night needs a building behind it, for the power and the restrooms and somebody to lock up, and by next summer this building will be locked hours before dark. The group’s entire fortune is the $310 in the can. Howard, the treasurer, counts it at every meeting in case it has bred. And the forecast for Sunday has little raindrops in it.

Everyone would fit in the center’s big room. Move the show indoors now and they can skip this whole conversation. But people have arranged to bring blankets. Some of them live in small apartments and want one evening of watching films between other people’s heads under an actual sky. Fran’s father, Walt, has an alien film in the program and would like to hear it laughed at in the open air. If this is the last one, nobody wants to hold it under fluorescent tubes.

The center’s manager offers them a choice. For $60 now, she’ll hold the big room on Sunday. If they use it, that’s another $80 for Bev’s time and the changeover. If they pass, she releases the room. In decent weather they stay outside at no extra charge. If rain washes out the courtyard and they have no room, they’ve promised the six filmmakers another date, and rebooking the projectionist and his equipment costs $300. Every other cost of the evening is the same either way and already paid.

This is a made-up film night. The prices and the weather estimate belong to this courtyard. What it offers is something we rarely get with other people’s decisions, a seat in the room before they knew.

Fran has the forecast open on her phone, percentages beside little raindrops, hour by hour. Denise, who captions the films and holds the third key, asks whether a forty percent chance of rain means a forty percent chance the show has to move. It doesn’t. The National Weather Service defines its probability of precipitation as the chance that at least 0.01 inch of precipitation falls at a given point during the forecast period, which on an hourly display means that hour.[1] A shower like that could be over before anybody plugs in a projector. Rain at four can leave chairs wet at seven. The app has no icon for woman in fourth row beginning to regret wearing linen.

The two evening hours each show forty percent, and already two sensible people disagree. Howard figures forty and forty is still forty. Denise adds them and gets eighty. Each has a theory about clouds, and the raindrops on the screen don’t say which theory anybody is entitled to.

What they need is the chance of a more specific event, weather that stops the screening in the courtyard. They call the projectionist, study the hourly pattern and argue about how much rain the site can take. Fran writes down 35 percent. That 35 is a judgment. No public body has issued a forecast for their chairs.

She turns over a program and draws a hundred imaginary Sundays, sixty-five dry enough on the left, thirty-five washed out on the right. Skip the room, and each of the thirty-five costs $300. That’s $10,500, or $105 a Sunday.

Howard can’t watch arithmetic without joining in, so he does the other column. Sixty dollars on all hundred Sundays is $6,000. Bev’s $80 on the thirty-five wet ones is $2,800. That’s $8,800, or $88 a Sunday. He has just shown that the room he doesn’t want wins by $17, and he isn’t impressed. Nobody is offering them a hundred Sundays. They get one, and on that one the booking either wastes $60 or saves $160. You can’t put an average in a coffee can. It won’t rattle.

How wrong can you afford to be?

So Fran asks a different question. How wrong would she have to be before Howard was right? Every washout the room rescues saves $300 of rebooking at a cost of $80 for the changeover. That’s $220. The fee is $60. Divide 60 by 220 and you get a little over 27 percent. If washouts are likelier than that, book. If they’re rarer, don’t.

Howard thinks they’re rarer. He says a quarter, and he has reasons. He’s run the projector in this courtyard. Last summer they kept going through a shower by moving the front row under cover, and the folding chairs dried fast.

He also has a use for the sixty. Denise captioned every film with talking in it for a fee he calls a near-insult. He did some of the timing himself and discovered that subtitling three minutes of conversation means listening to several people say yeah until you no longer believe in the existence of language. His neighbor Eileen is coming on Sunday, and she doesn’t hear well. If the city has left them one film night, he’d like to spend the money on the captioner. He would not like to spend it renting a room from the same city that’s putting them out of it.

“Don’t make me the reason,” says Denise.

Fran thinks 35 is closer, and it could be worse. Last summer’s shower came in daylight during a shorter program. Sunday runs into the dark and needs a dry stretch long enough for six films. She’d like Denise paid properly too. Nobody gets to settle this by handing all the virtue to the woman with the key.

And then the can. A washout with no room takes $300 out of $310. Ten dollars would be left to cover anything else that went wrong, among donated equipment that includes a projector stand kept level by a beer coaster. A film group with ten dollars and a building that locks at five has given its last show, whether or not anybody announces it. Book the room and the worst the weather can do is $140. An average is a fine guide to a bet you can afford to lose thirty-five times in a hundred. This one they can’t afford to lose once.

Fran calls the manager. Could they decide in the morning, when the forecast will be fresher? No. Half the neighborhood has a last evening to hold before January, somebody else wants the room, and she needs an answer tonight. Could they pay the $60 and get it back if she rents the room to that somebody? Also no. The manager sounds mildly amused. She’s being asked to provide exactly the thing she charges $60 for, with the agreeable possibility of never receiving $60.

It was worth the call. Options sometimes turn out softer than they look, and a phone call can do more for your odds than a longer conversation with your forehead. Not tonight.

At ten to six they vote. Fran and Denise want the room. Howard would take his chances outdoors. Denise has just voted against her own sixty dollars and is taking a great interest in the lid of the can. They book it. Howard writes down his reason for voting no. Fran writes down her estimate, the price of a washout and the least she wants left in the can. Then she puts the program in an envelope and writes Friday on it. On Sunday night they can see whether Howard was right. Or rather, they can see what happened. It takes Fran a moment to recognize those as different appointments.

The forecast for Tuesday

While the envelope is closed, here is a committee with more to lose.

In the spring of 1944 the Allied invasion of France needed a moon for the paratroopers and a low tide at dawn for the men crossing the beaches. That combination came in two windows, June 5 to 7 and June 19 to 21. General Dwight Eisenhower chose June 5. His chief meteorologist was Group Captain James Stagg, a Scot in a Royal Air Force uniform, and Stagg’s job was close to impossible. Three teams of forecasters, one American and two British, argued over the same charts on a conference line. The Americans thought June 5 would do. The British saw a storm coming. Stagg had to walk into a room full of admirals and generals and give them one forecast.[2]

Before dawn on Sunday, June 4, at Southwick House near Portsmouth, he told them Monday would bring high wind and low cloud. Eisenhower postponed the invasion by a day. When Stagg stepped outside afterward, the sky was clear and the wind was still.[2] He had just stopped the largest seaborne invasion ever assembled, on a fine morning, on the strength of a chart.

By that night the rain had arrived and Stagg was back with something stranger. Behind the storm he expected a short break on Tuesday, June 6, weather that would be poor but possible. He couldn’t promise what came after it, and an army put ashore on Tuesday has to be supplied on Wednesday. Eisenhower later wrote that this was the fear, getting the first waves in and then being unable to follow them.[3] Waiting for a better forecast meant waiting two weeks for the next tides. He heard Stagg once more before dawn on Monday and gave the order.

The break came. That’s the part everybody tells. The forecast was also wrong in ways that get told less often. The calm the forecasters expected behind the storm never built. The storm lingered and weakened instead. The cloud lifted, but the wind on June 6 blew harder than forecast, and rough seas made trouble for the landing craft and drove the tide in faster than the planners had allowed for. The meteorologist Anders Persson, who went back through the transcripts of the forecasters’ phone conferences, calls the result right for the wrong reason.[4]

And on June 19, the first of the dates Eisenhower would have fallen back on, a gale hit the Normandy coast, blew for three days and wrecked the artificial harbor the Americans had built off Omaha Beach.[4]

It’s hard to hear that last fact and not feel the decision has been proved right. It hasn’t. No chart in the room showed the gale of June 19. Everything that made the choice sound was on the table before dawn on Monday: a narrow window, a forecast good for one day and honest about the next, and the price of waiting. Had the gap closed over the Channel on Tuesday morning, the same men with the same charts would now be a case study in recklessness. Eisenhower knew it. He spent part of Monday writing a few lines that took the blame if the landings failed.[5] All the weather did for his decision was make it famous.

Eisenhower’s worst case was counted in men, and Fran’s is counted in folding chairs. What carries over is the shape, a deadline that won’t wait for a fresher forecast, experts who disagree, and a judgment that everyone else gets to make after the sky has spoken.

Hindsight

What happened to you a moment ago, at the news of that gale, has been measured. Jonathan Baron and John Hershey reported five experiments in 1988. Students judged decisions about medical cases and monetary gambles. They were given what the person choosing had known, and then told how things came out. Better outcomes tended to draw better ratings of the thinking, of the decision maker, and of whether they’d trust that person with the next choice. In the gambles, even hearing that the rejected option had done badly could improve the rating of the choice that was made.[6] These were students rating written cases, with nothing of their own at stake.

Consider the unfairness of that last part. I decide to leave my money in my pocket. The thing I declined to buy drops in value, and you think better of my reasoning. It triples, and your opinion sinks. I spent both afternoons asleep on the same sofa. My reputation has been out having adventures of its own.

Results do sometimes tell on the person who chose. If the roof leaks and it turns out the roofer put the shingles on upside down, take a lively interest in the outcome. The mistake is to treat one wet pillow as proof that somebody should have known a branch would come through the skylight at four in the morning.

In the courtyard, by seven-thirty on Sunday, reality is showing an almost offensive commitment to the dry version of events. People have brought cushions. Howard is marking the captioned films on Eileen’s program. She has put on lipstick and brought a coat in an optimistic shade of cream. She’s here for a film her grandson made about what happens to a bicycle left under a tree full of pigeons. It has very little dialogue. She thinks she’ll be all right. Bev stands at the back door with her keys. Rain would have meant extra paid hours of moving chairs. The fine evening is costing her those hours, and nobody will ask a sponge about that.

Walt has spent the day claiming complete indifference to the reception of his picture. He has also asked four separate people how many chairs they’ve set out, an interesting level of operational engagement for a man at peace with his art. He sits beside his daughter. When the gloved hand appears he folds his own hands under his coat. The audience laughs exactly where he’d hoped. He never turns his head. Fran can tell from the small dip of his chin how much it mattered.

A person could tidy up their decision making a great deal by getting rid of feelings like that. Make a film. Evaluate the information you had when you made it. Decline to care whether anybody laughs. What a magnificently well-calibrated way to die of boredom. Outcomes matter. That’s why taking a chance hurts. Had it poured on the last film night with no room booked, Howard would have been entitled to sit on the center’s steps and be thoroughly goddamn miserable. Knowing that he’d reasoned decently from a quarter might have been some comfort by spring. The cruel part of hindsight would have come from everybody else. He’d have lost the thing he hoped for, and then been asked to defend having hoped.

Keep the bad forecasts

Howard has an objection, and he makes it while folding chairs. If thirty-five percent can be followed by rain and can be followed by a sunset, when exactly does Fran have to admit she was wrong? She could write thirty-five percent on every picnic for the rest of her life and greet every shower and every sunburn with the words as predicted.

It’s a serious objection, and she owes him a way to tell. In 1950 the meteorologist Glenn Brier published a method for scoring forecasts that attach probabilities to events.[7] The common version for a yes-or-no event goes like this. Write zero for no and one for yes. Subtract what happened from the probability you gave, then square the difference. A smaller score is better.

Fran said 0.35 and the show went ahead, so her score is 0.35 squared, or 0.1225. Had it been washed out, she’d have scored the square of the gap between 0.35 and 1, which is 0.4225. A man who gave a washout no chance at all scores a perfect zero tonight and a full one on the wet night. Anybody prepared to be certain about everything will have a number of occasions on which to give an extremely good speech. Keep the other speeches too.

The squaring is there for a reason. It charges so much for a confident miss that over a long run of evenings nobody beats an honest estimate by saying zero more firmly. You beat it by learning to tell the dangerous evenings from the safe ones.

A weather station gets a long record. A woman who helps run one film night a year, and may have run her last, never will. Even so, she can do better than walking around on Monday remembering that she was vaguely worried about something wet. She wrote down what would count as a washout. She can keep the call, and the next one, including the dull ones.

They open the Friday envelope. Howard still thinks a quarter was nearer. A dry evening does fit a quarter a little better than it fits thirty-five percent, just as it fits a lunatic issuing a lifetime guarantee of no more rain better than either. One Sunday gives Howard a small piece of support. It gives the lunatic an excellent photograph for his brochure.

Fran has one new fact that changes her view of the evening a good deal. The first film opened with pale lettering. From the back of the courtyard, while the sky still had light in it, Eileen couldn’t read it against the screen. Fran only finds out because she asks Eileen what she made of her grandson’s title. Eileen says it must have been wonderful from the front. From the back she saw a rectangle changing its mind about which shade of gray it wanted to be.

This bears on the organizers differently from the unused room. The rain was a risk they recognized and priced. Whether everybody could read the screen was an assumption nobody inspected. They tested the lettering on Thursday night, in full dark, from three chairs away. They argued for an hour over sixty dollars for captions, and the woman who needed the captions most couldn’t read the first title. A dry evening has handed them hard news about a mistake that none of the people congratulating them noticed.

The fix costs almost nothing. A test slide just before dusk, with somebody standing at the back. By now most of the committee is enjoying the much larger and entirely insoluble argument about whether the sky has vindicated their characters. Fran writes ask Eileen on the back of the program before joining in.

The price of the key

The key to the big room has to go back. June comes along, still carrying the sponge. The room has the washed-floor smell of an institution unsullied by human use. Fran turns the lights on to check the doors, then off again on the way out. A small flashlight clicks on behind her.

On the far wall an animal appears. Its ears are different lengths. One of them bends right over, and the animal makes a series of chewing movements with what is clearly a seven-year-old’s thumb. For a few seconds the custodians of sixty dollars’ worth of unused floor are having a private screening of Rabbit Eats Its Own Foot.

“Would you pay sixty dollars to see that?” June asks.

“I’d wait for it to come out on television.”

Nobody has to pretend the rabbit made the booking pay for itself. The money is spent. The can holds $250. Howard’s objection stands, and Denise could have used sixty actual dollars. It would be dishonest to wave that away because some imaginary wet Fran saved $160 in a courtyard nobody had to mop. All the same, Friday’s decision belonged to a woman who didn’t know which Sunday was coming and had the group’s last $310 in front of her. She paid for the right to open these doors on the one night it would have mattered. The sponge is an honest witness. It can swear to Sunday. On Friday it was under a sink.

June hands the exhibit over. “I’d have bought the ice cream.”

“So would Howard,” says Fran.

On the way out they pass Walt, carrying the colander and a folding chair. He has been approached about a sequel. The suggestion came from somebody with no intention of putting on a rubber glove on a Sunday, who is therefore ideally placed to commit the filmmakers to a body of work. Walt likes the idea. He also likes the idea of coming to somebody else’s show next year and sitting at the back with his feet up, if the city leaves anybody a back to sit at. He has kept both futures open by saying he’ll think about it.

At the gate Eileen is waiting for her grandson. He has the bicycle film on his phone. Fran asks if they can see the first few seconds again. There in the dark, in letters the boy drew himself, is the title his grandmother missed. My Brother Said It Was a Good Place to Leave It. Eileen laughs hard enough to get lipstick on her sleeve.

“Put me wherever you like next time,” she tells Fran. “Just come and sit with me for the first one.”

Fran writes it down under Friday’s numbers. Then she gives the key to Bev, who has until January to go on locking this door at night. They can’t have the sixty dollars back. They can do something about Eileen’s seat, if the city leaves them an evening to put it in.
