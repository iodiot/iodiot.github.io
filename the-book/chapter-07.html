Chapter 07The War Against Feeling Bad

You’re on the couch with a mug of coffee you’ve reheated twice. This morning your friend from across the hall moved three hundred miles away for a job she wanted. You carried boxes down three flights and meant every word you said about how good this was going to be for her. Then the truck pulled out, you came back upstairs, put her spare key on the table and discovered that, apparently, you’re an asshole.

The key was supposed to go in the landlord’s drop box on your way up. You got as far as the slot. It’s a small, accurately cut piece of metal that now opens nothing you’re allowed into. Your roommate is at work until six.

For years she knocked twice on your door whenever her kettle boiled. She’s the only person you know who owns a real kettle, a dented thing that screams, and she made coffee with it one cup at a time through a paper cone, as if a machine couldn’t be trusted with the job. You’d cross the hall in your socks. Nobody ever had to check a calendar.

What kind of person gets sad about a friend’s good news? You set about making the feeling go away. You list the advantages of the move. You remind yourself that highways exist and that three hundred miles is five hours if nobody needs a bathroom. You try being happy for her harder, like a person leaning on a stuck door. Then the coffee needs reheating again.

How happy are you about her job, one to ten? A nine. How much do you miss her? Also a nine. All afternoon you’ve been trying to subtract the second number from the first, as though a properly functioning adult would get zero and go do laundry. Whose damn units are these?

One of those nines isn’t clean, either. She applied to that place three times. You know because you proofread all three cover letters. Twice they turned her down, and twice you crossed the hall with a bottle and got to be the friend who says it’s their loss. You were good at it. Some part of you had been getting ready to be good at it a third time. The truck hadn’t reached the highway before you were looking up her new employer, hoping for a lawsuit or at least some bad reviews. Four and a half stars. It’s possible to miss your friend, be glad she got the job, and take those stars personally. People have room for all three before lunch.

So here’s the question you’ve been walking around since the truck left. Is this sadness, or is it envy of her job? Run it both ways. Suppose she’d moved three hundred miles for a job she didn’t want. You’d feel better, and you know you would. Now suppose the same people had opened an office four blocks from here and she’d never left the building. You’d still have had a bad day sometime in her first week. So it’s both. The missing is bigger. The envy is what made you an asshole, because for as long as there’s been a hall you’ve been crossing it to complain about your own job, and she listened to every word, and then she went and did something about hers.

You decide not to think about it.

The bear and the bell

That decision has been tested. For the next few seconds, keep a white bear entirely out of your mind. You can probably guess who just lumbered in through the kitchen door.

In 1987 Daniel Wegner and his colleagues gave people that assignment. Volunteers spent five minutes saying whatever went through their heads while trying not to think of a white bear, and they rang a bell each time the bear got in anyway. It got in more than once a minute. Then they were told to go ahead and think about it. The people who’d spent five minutes keeping the bear out now produced more bear than people who’d been invited to think about it from the start.[1] Their bear got five minutes. Yours has had the couch since the truck.

What you can recognize from the couch is the extra shift you’ve taken on. Between missing her and checking whether you still miss her, you’ve arranged to think about her full-time.

At the truck this morning you gave a clean performance of a person who was only happy. She asked if you were okay and you told her you were thrilled, three separate times, in the voice of somebody giving away a car on television. “You’ll come up,” she said. “Try and stop me,” you said. She either bought it or was kind enough to look like she had. You’ve been tired ever since in a way that carrying a couch doesn’t explain.

James Gross put that performance in a lab. He showed a hundred and twenty people a film chosen to turn a stomach. Some were told to think about it in a way that would leave them feeling nothing. Some were told to behave so that nobody watching could tell they felt anything. Both groups kept their faces quiet. The ones who’d changed how they thought about the film also reported less disgust. The ones who’d only hidden it reported just as much, and their bodies showed more alarm than the bodies of people who’d simply watched.[2] In later surveys with Oliver John, people who made a habit of hiding what they felt also did worse on measures of how their relationships were going, though a questionnaire can’t say which came first.[3]

So you try the phone. Forty minutes go by, during which you learn what a stranger thinks of a sandwich and watch a man pressure-wash a driveway from start to finish. When you look up, the feeling is sitting exactly where you left it, and it looks rested.

At four you hear two knocks. You’re halfway off the couch before you understand it’s the pipes. Somebody upstairs has shut off a bath too fast, and the water in the line, still moving, has nowhere to go. The pressure spikes and a shock wave runs back down the pipe. Plumbers call it water hammer.[4] Your knees haven’t heard about the truck. You sit back down.

Gentlemen, this is no humbug

Wanting a pain gone is about as old and as reasonable as wants get, and one Friday in Boston the wish came true.

On October 16, 1846, in the operating theater under the dome of Massachusetts General Hospital, a young man named Gilbert Abbott waited to have a tumor cut from his neck, just under the jaw. The surgeon was John Collins Warren, a senior man who’d spent his whole career cutting people who were awake. Everybody in that room knew what an operation was. It was fast, because it had to be, and it was loud. They also knew what a painless one was, which was a con. The year before, a dentist named Horace Wells had tried laughing gas in front of Boston medical students. The patient groaned and the room called it a humbug. Now another dentist, William Morton, had promised to do better with an inhaler and a vapor. He showed up about twenty minutes late.[5]

Warren let him try. Abbott breathed the ether and went slack. Warren cut. Nobody had to hold anybody down. When Abbott came around, he didn’t claim he’d felt nothing. In the report a Boston surgeon named Henry Jacob Bigelow published a month later, the pain had been there, only dulled, “as though the skin had been scratched with a hoe.”[6] In that room a hoe was a miracle.

Warren is supposed to have turned to the gallery and announced, “Gentlemen, this is no humbug.” Everybody knows the line. It first turns up in print in 1859, in a biography of Morton, and a historian who went back through what the people in the room wrote down concluded that Warren may never have said it.[7] The war against pain got a better opening line than it had, which tells you how badly everybody wanted it to be a war.

Morton, meanwhile, set about owning the ether. He mixed in oil of orange so nobody would know it by the smell, called it Letheon and patented it. Patent No. 4,848 came through on November 12, less than four weeks after Abbott woke up, and Morton dreamed of collecting royalties from every etherizer to come.[8] Pain had been abolished, pending licensing.

You’d think nobody could object to the abolition itself. Charles Meigs, who taught obstetrics in Philadelphia, opposed anesthesia in childbirth for the rest of his career. He’s quoted calling the pain of labor “a most desirable, salutary and conservative manifestation of life force,” a position he was well placed to hold calmly.[9] In 1854 John Hall, the principal medical officer of the British army about to sail for the Crimea, sent his surgeons a memorandum warning them off chloroform for the badly wounded. “The smart of the knife is a powerful stimulant,” he wrote, “and it is much better to hear a man bawl lustily than to see him sink silently into the grave.”[10]

They weren’t simply fools. The early anesthetics killed some of the people who breathed them, and Meigs put a fair question to a colleague in Edinburgh who was giving chloroform to women in labor. What motive was good enough to risk the life of even one in a thousand?[9] The relief wasn’t shared out evenly, either. For decades after 1846, the historian Martin Pernick found, doctors went on deciding patient by patient who needed it, and the people thought to feel pain less were the ones you’d guess: the poor, Black and Native patients, men in general.[11] Even with the bottle on the shelf, somebody else still had to decide your pain was worth the trouble.

The objectors lost anyway, and they deserved to. Abbott had nothing to learn on that table that he needed to be awake for. The surgeon knew where the tumor was. The pain was attached to nothing Abbott wanted to keep, so when it went, nothing went with it but a few minutes he was glad to miss.

Ether works by removing the patient. For as long as it lasts nobody’s home, which is ideal when the only thing happening to you is a knife. The thing happening to you this afternoon is her. Every anesthetic within reach, the phone, the second beer, the list of reasons this is all for the best, works the way ether does. None of them can find the ache without finding her, and whatever they take out, they take out together. If a pill could remove the truck from your chest and leave her in, you should take it, and I’d want the name of the pill. A person whose bad afternoon has lasted a year should be asking a doctor for exactly that, and Meigs can go to hell.

Boston left the rest of us suspecting that feeling bad is always a malfunction somebody should’ve fixed by now, and that somebody will sell you the fix.

What she did with feeling bad

Twice those people told her no. Both times she drank your wine and said she was done with them, and both times she had the listing open again by the weekend. Getting turned down twice by the same people is a specific, repeatable misery, and she signed up for a third round because she wanted what was on the other side. She’s paying again tonight. She’s three hundred miles from everybody who knows her middle name, including a mother with a portal login, in an apartment full of boxes, and she picked that too.

You have a job posting open in a browser tab. It’s been open since March. You haven’t applied, because of how it would feel to be told no, and you’ve arranged a good deal of your life around not feeling that. The arrangement has a perfect record. Nobody has turned you down for anything since March.

It isn’t that you can’t feel bad on purpose. You’ll get out of bed at seven and run four miles in the cold for a race nobody asked you to enter. This fall you made a phone call to your sister that was every bit as bad as you’d expected, and you aren’t sorry you made it. You carried a couch down three flights this morning for a woman who was leaving. Line those up and you can read off what you’ve decided is worth it. The tab isn’t on the list. That’s information about the job, or about you, and you’ve spent eight months not finding out which.

That’s the envy, once you make it hold still. You don’t want her job. You wouldn’t last a week in her job. You want to have wanted something enough to feel that bad for it three times.

The other nine comes out of the same sum with a better answer. A morning of boxes, a five-hour drive whenever you want to see her, and an afternoon like this one are what the hallway turns out to cost, and nobody had to talk you into any of it.

Around five you get off the couch, because it turns out a person can feel like this and still be hungry. The first slice of toast burns while you stand there composing a more mature reaction to the smell of burnt toast. Into the trash it goes. The second one gets butter.

At the table you balance the toast on top of the mug, the way she always hated, and send her a picture. “Still no plate.”

The dots appear, stop, and appear again. What comes back is a photograph. She’s on the floor of an empty room with her coat still on, holding a dinner plate up to the camera like a traffic cop. One box is open behind her. “First thing I unpacked,” she writes. “I knew you’d do this.”

You laugh hard enough to have to steady the mug. You miss her during the laugh, too.

Then you type the thing you’ve spent the day trying not to have. “I’m happy for you. I’m also jealous as hell and I already miss you. I wanted you to hear the middle one from me.” Your thumb waits over it. At noon you’d have paid cash not to feel what sending it feels like. You send it and stay in the chair.

Her answer takes four minutes. You spend them not reheating the coffee.

“Good, be jealous,” she writes. “I’m eating cereal out of a saucepan and I don’t know one person in this state. I cried at a rest stop. Can I call you Thursday?”

“Sure.” You put the period on it. Let her wonder.

Her key is still on the table. The landlord wants it by Monday, and on the first somebody else will be boiling water behind that door, or, God help you, not boiling it. Nothing got fixed today. She’s gone, the tab is still open, and at nine the pipes knock twice and get you halfway out of the chair again.
