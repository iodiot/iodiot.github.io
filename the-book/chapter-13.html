Chapter 13Love Is Not a Customer Service Agreement

The box on the form says Relationship to participant. Jo has written depends how badly I fall.

“Apparently they want something shorter,” she says. “Can I put you down as my emergency contact?”

Sam comes over with the tea. She’s still stretched across the sofa where he found her when he got back from the snooker club. There’s a faint blue thumbprint on his mug. He reads the form over her shoulder. “Roller skating? Since when?”

“Since I was eleven. There’s been a slight gap in provision.”

Her sister has given her four Friday-night lessons for her birthday and found her a second-hand pair of skates with yellow laces. Jo wants to learn to go backwards. When she was little, a woman at the local rink used to collect the orange cones at closing time. She’d glide backwards down the middle, bending to lift each cone into the one in her hand. On the last lap she’d be carrying a tower up to her chin. Everyone else was a kid in a borrowed pair of boots trying to retain legal custody of their feet. This woman wore her own skates and had a pencil behind her ear. Jo thought she was the most beautiful person in the world.

“Did you know her name?”

“God, no. What was I going to do, introduce myself and ask if she had any vacancies in cones?”

Sam settles for partner on the form. It’s a nice word. It fits into a little white rectangle and permits whoever rings from the scene of the accident to say, with some confidence, that the left wrist belongs to one of theirs.

There’s a line beneath for the phone number. No space for what the partner is expected to do on a Friday when the participant walks out of a shuttered building with two tender shins and he’s given his word he’ll be there. It would take a considerably bigger form to describe what the two of them understand by partner. Jo would fill in a fair bit of it one way. Sam would fill it in another. And there are whole pages each believes the other signed years ago.

Nine at the side door

The first week Jo comes home delighted to have fallen in company. A man twice her size went down beside her with such lingering courtesy that she had time to remove her hand from the bit of floor he was about to occupy. The second week she manages half a lap backwards holding the instructor’s hand. The third week she comes off the rink early to catch the bus. Just as she’s unlacing her boots the others get to try it on their own. Through the glass she sees a woman in yellow socks lift both arms in astonishment at her own moving body. Jo thinks about those yellow socks the whole way home.

“Last class next week,” she tells Sam on Thursday. “Meet me at nine at the side door and walk home with me? Then I can stay till the end.”

“Nine it is.”

They both like the walk along the old canal. Together it takes about half an hour. Alone at that time on a Friday, with the towpath empty, Jo takes the bus. The last one that goes straight to their street leaves at eight minutes to nine. The timetable and the timetable of people’s lives appear to have been drawn up in separate buildings by men in the middle of a divorce.

Sam has given a perfectly clear answer to a perfectly clear question. Nobody needs a psychology degree to work out where the man is supposed to be at nine.

At twenty to nine on Friday he’s still at home, on the phone to Ellen. She’s found two different measurements for the same piece of timber in a guide that is about to go to print. The printer needs the correction by Monday morning. Sam has an old file somewhere. He can hear the small catch in Ellen’s voice that means she’s afraid it’s her mistake. He’d like to find the file while she’s there and let her have a weekend. This is a considerate instinct. It becomes rather more expensive once he starts paying for it with somebody else’s Friday.

At eight forty-five he sees Jo’s message asking whether they’re still on. He could write, I’m at home. I’ll be late. She’s standing by the locker with her coat in her hand. She could still get the bus.

Yep. On my way, he writes. His slippers offer a dissenting view.

He finds the file. The incorrect measurement came from the supplier. Ellen sighs with relief. She says, “Oh God, have I kept you?” He says, “You’re fine,” which is accurate about Ellen. By the time he gets his shoes on, the bus has gone and so has Jo’s chance of making a different decision. He jogs most of the way, arriving at the side door at seventeen minutes past nine just as a minicab turns in.

Jo gets in the back and moves over. It’s raining. He gets in too. A tiny knitted strawberry hangs from the rear-view mirror, swaying agreeably each time the driver goes round a corner. Sam has seldom wanted so badly to have a conversation about a strawberry.

“I was helping Ellen,” he says at last.

“I know. You told me on the phone when you actually left.”

“We had two different numbers.”

“I believe you, Sam.”

He has several more pieces of supporting evidence immediately available. A time-stamped email. A photograph of a bracket. If called upon, the woman who drew the bracket could probably be persuaded to appear in person. He discovers, to his considerable irritation, that he has prepared a defence against an allegation nobody has made.

At the flat Jo pays the fare of thirteen pounds sixty. She keeps the receipt. Sam wants credit for the fact that he ran. He can feel the ache under his ribs. It seems a shame for it to go to waste. He’d also like last Tuesday taken into consideration, when he fetched her prescription in his lunch hour, and the time he sat up with her dad watching a documentary about paving. Ideally there would be a permanent record of the paving. At the end of the second hour a man in a fleece had compared three kinds of sand.

Before we give him too hard a time, consider the last occasion on which someone you love was quite reasonably pissed off with you. Did your mind stick entirely to the point? Or did a junior employee somewhere behind your forehead begin pulling receipts going back to the first time you bought this ungrateful bastard a croissant? Mine has impeccable files. Mislay a set of keys and the department closes for lunch. Ask for a kindness nobody thanked me for in 2014 and a boy on a bicycle will have it on your desk before noon.

The paving really happened. So did Tuesday. Here’s what Jo lost tonight. At eight forty-five she had a choice between catching the bus and staying on the floor for one more go. She put her coat down because a person she had good reason to trust told her he was on his way. Sam can replace thirteen pounds sixty. All the paving in Belgium won’t get her back to that locker at eight forty-five.

One point for Tricia

In a psychology experiment published in 1979, male students doing a task with letters believed they could pass some of their letters to a young woman called Tricia. She appeared to have a harder selection to work with. The men helped. Tricia appeared to finish her task in time to earn four points towards possible course credit. A folded note came back. Depending on the experimental condition, it contained either a thank-you or a thank-you and one of her points. The researchers, Margaret Clark and Judson Mills, wanted to know what difference that point would make.[1]

They also varied what the participant subsequently heard about Tricia. Some men were told her husband would be along shortly. Others heard she was new to the university and didn’t know many people. This was 1979, and the experiment placed a fairly period-specific bet on what an undergraduate man would infer from those facts. Among the men given reason to expect a more personal connection, the men on average liked Tricia less when she returned a point. In the condition designed to suggest an exchange, returning a point made her more appealing.

One point, travelling from one room to the next, appears capable of carrying two different messages. You’ve done me a good turn and I’d like to give something back. Or, we’re square now and there’s no particular reason for us to see each other again. These were brief encounters in an arranged setting, measured in ratings of liking. Nobody in the lab had spent a wet February looking for a care home with Tricia. It’s a small experiment about the meaning people can read into repayment. That meaning is quite big enough to cause trouble in a taxi.

Suppose you visit a friend after an operation and wash her hair over the kitchen sink. She’s frightened about getting the dressing wet. You cup your hand along her forehead and she tips her head back into it. Afterwards she puts a banknote on the draining board. If you’re a hairdresser who’s given up a paying appointment to make this visit, you may be touched that she’s remembered. If the two of you have washed each other’s hair since you were small, you might want to leave very quickly before she sees what’s happened to your face. Same banknote. Same clean head. The two of you are in the middle of finding out what sort of thing you just did.

Now change it again. You’ve been there for a wash every afternoon this month. You’ve lost hours at work. Your friend can afford visiting care, very much prefers you and has become rather eloquent on the subject of how things like this can’t be measured in money. That banknote on the draining board is beginning to resemble an excellent opening to a conversation.

This is where the chapter title could be put to some very shitty uses. One person gets to declare that love is above keeping score. The other has to remember which nights the chemist stays open, has paid the cab fare three Fridays running and can show you what unmeasured devotion looks like on a bank statement. Counting can be the first honest thing that happens in a relationship. If the two of you say that you alternate visits to a parent and one person has done seven of the last eight, write the eight dates down. Seven eighths is 87.5 percent. There may be loving, freely agreed reasons for the split. There may be a second person who remembers the one visit because it was last night. At least now both people are having an argument about the same eight evenings.

In a decent relationship you can produce that piece of paper without having to keep it for the rest of your natural life. You need to find out whether the other person can take in what it says and let it change what happens next Thursday. If the reply every time is that people who really loved each other wouldn’t write these things down, I’d get a bigger notebook.

Jo takes off her damp socks and hangs them over the bath. Sam transfers her the fare from the money he keeps for his own spending. Then he knocks on the open bathroom door.

“I could have told you I was still here,” he says. “You asked me in time. I said I’d left because I wanted a few more minutes and I knew you’d take the bus if I told you.”

This is the first account of the evening in which Jo recognises herself as one of the people who was there.

She tells him something in return. “I kept looking at the little window in the door. Thought I might see your face.”

“What, before nine?”

“There’s a bench where you can watch.”

“But you asked me to meet you outside.”

“Yes,” says Jo. It’s a nuisance how exactly he’s remembered this.

Until now she’s had a tidy grievance. It fits in her hand and has the cab firm’s name printed on the top. Now she’s got a second thing in there which she doesn’t much fancy taking out in the light. She wanted him to come in early and see what she could do. He agreed to meet her when the room was closing. She could quite easily have said eight fifty. Some part of her felt that an invitation issued in plain English would spoil the result.

If you’ve ever wanted somebody to bring you flowers and had to explain, to your considerable distress, that you no longer wanted the flowers now they knew you wanted them, you have been down this particular manhole. The object you’d hoped to receive was evidence about what happened in another person’s mind while you were absent. Daffodils are widely available. A certificate stating that these daffodils occurred spontaneously to a mildly distracted mammal is a much more specialised product.

What I like about Jo is that she can see the problem and still quite badly wants the certificate.

The woman who hates being watched

As it happens, Sam has good evidence about the woman he lives with. On their third date he watched her reverse a borrowed van into a space between two skips. She lowered the window and asked whether there was anything else he’d like tickets for. He turned his back. When he looked again she had parked the van and bought him an ice cream. On the basis of a fairly memorable afternoon he has come to believe Jo hates being watched while she’s learning to do something.

This belief has served them both very well. He’s the person who will leave the room when she’s ringing about a job. He knows that if she’s making something for the first time and it starts to go wrong, he should develop an interest in the view. There are people who would quite cheerfully transplant their own lung in the bathroom if the alternative was to have a partner stand in the doorway saying, “Are you sure it goes in that way round?” Sam’s willingness to go away is one of the things she loves about him. The annoying part about being loved intelligently is that sometimes you have to send round a correction.

“You made such a thing about the van,” he says.

“I was driving somebody else’s van into a skip, Sam. How long were you planning to get out of that?”

Jo’s been keeping a picture of Sam too. He’s the man who sees a woman struggling and offers to draw her a little diagram. She’s had years of good material for this. Once, after she mentioned losing a glove, he sent her a drawing of two gloves connected by a length of elastic. Regrettably she opened it on a train and frightened a fellow passenger by laughing. She remembers the elastic. She has rather let herself forget the fellow passenger. Somewhere along the way a thing she’d found funny has become a standard piece of evidence against the defence.

In an essay called The Idea of Perfection, Iris Murdoch imagines a woman whom she calls M and a daughter-in-law whom she calls D. M considers her son’s choice of wife a social disappointment. She doesn’t like how D dresses or speaks. She also has very good manners. From the outside, you’d see a mother-in-law being consistently courteous. What interests Murdoch is the possibility that something morally important could happen even if an observer could detect no change in M’s behaviour at all.[2]

M begins to suspect her own picture has been arranged to flatter the painter. Perhaps she is a snob. Perhaps she’s jealous. Once she entertains those possibilities, she can look again at the young woman and find, in conduct she’d dismissed as childish, the energy of somebody young. Murdoch goes so far as to allow that D might by now be dead. The change she’s trying to describe could take place in M with no fresh performance by D and certainly no opportunity for the daughter-in-law to improve her table manners.

Imagine being the daughter-in-law in a less tidy, living version of this story. You visit your husband’s mother for years. She learns how you take your tea. On winter nights she puts a hot water bottle in your bed. And every time you open your mouth she hears a woman who has married above her station. What do you do with the hot water bottle? You’re cold. The kindness is real. So is the peculiar exhaustion of taking your coat off under that gaze. If one day she asked what you’d been trying to tell her about your work and could bear to hear a good answer in the accent she dislikes, you might finally get warm before bedtime.

Murdoch has given us a demanding idea of what we owe one another. A person can behave correctly towards you for a very long time and still do you an injustice in the privacy of their own head. There’s an injustice in the other direction too. Imagine deciding that M is a snob on the basis of an afternoon’s tea and then congratulating yourself on having fully understood an old woman. Where’s the convenient place to stand in this argument? The minute I find one, it turns out I’ve been standing on somebody’s foot.

Jo is sitting on the edge of the bath, still holding one wet sock. Here’s what she can ask of Sam. He could retire the woman in the van as the sole authority on what she wants. He could try an ordinary question next time. Here’s what she can offer him. When he says she told him to wait outside, she can admit that he paid her the considerable compliment of listening. Occasionally being properly listened to results in getting the exact thing you requested, a dreadful fate that could befall any one of us.

She puts the sock down. “I wanted one go where I could turn round and say, did you see that?”

“Did you do it?”

“Yes.”

“Oh, Jo. How far?”

“From the fire door to the drinks machine.”

He knows how far that is because he used to go to the same building for badminton. It’s a respectable distance, particularly for a person who could see where she’d been rather better than she could see where she was going. She’s pleased he asked the second question. He’s pleased she told him. The cost of the cab still belongs to him.

A little generous in the light

You might conclude that we’d all be better off with an absolutely accurate picture of the person we’ve chosen. A neutral assessor could pop round once a year, watch your partner put on a duvet cover and issue an amended certificate. Satisfactory in company. Several opportunities for development under linen. If you’ve ever tried to get a double duvet into its cover with a person you love, you’ll know the person conducting that assessment would require some form of witness protection.

Accuracy about what, though? There’s a generous way of seeing somebody that gives them a bit more room to become themselves. There’s also a generous way that wallpapers over a hole in the floor. I’d like to keep the first one. I would prefer to find out about the second before my leg goes through the kitchen.

In a study published in 1996, Sandra Murray, John Holmes and Dale Griffin followed dating couples. Both partners completed measures three times over a year. People who began with more idealised views of one another tended to report a more favourable change in satisfaction as the year went on. Their relationships were also more likely to last.[3] The researchers didn’t assign half the couples a pair of rose-tinted spectacles and subsequently count survivors. They tracked how perceptions and relationships developed together. The finding makes it a little harder to argue that a perfectly dispassionate eye is always the best equipment to bring to a first date.

Consider two possible reactions to Jo on the rink. Her knees wobble. She holds her arms as if carrying a valuable cake across a boat. You could describe this in detail and leave out the quite evident fact that she keeps coming off the rail. Sam, who loves her, might see somebody brave. He’s got evidence. On the other hand suppose he’s committed to having a brave girlfriend because he likes the sound of that at dinner parties. She tells him she’s frightened and wants to go home. He puts her back on the floor because he knows what a courageous woman she is. The same flattering adjective can offer somebody company all the way back to the rail or prise their fingers off it.

A decent test of the picture you have of someone is what happens when they decline a compliment. Your partner has been the strong one through a difficult year. Everybody says so. Then comes a wet ordinary morning in which they can’t quite face opening a letter. Can they put it on the table and tell you that? Or must they now handle the letter and a small public-relations incident concerning their brand? There are people crying in locked bathrooms because everybody outside has been wonderfully supportive about their frankly legendary resilience.

This applies to the flattering picture you have of yourself, too. Sam likes being dependable. When Ellen asked for the file, he rather liked the thought that of all the people she could ring to settle the matter, she’d rung him. Telling her he had to leave at eight thirty would have required him to finish the conversation while she was still worried. A man who cares can do that. A man who needs to see the grateful look on everybody’s face before he leaves is going to be late quite often.

On Monday he tells Ellen that he’s put the old measurements where she can get at them herself. “Good,” she says. “I felt awful about keeping you. You did say you had time.”

There’s practically a queue of women waiting to be told what time this man is leaving the house.

Jo has her own little brush with the difficulty of allowing other people to be inconveniently real. On Tuesday night her sister rings. Did she enjoy the birthday lessons? Jo tells her about the woman in yellow socks, about the drinks machine, even about wanting Sam to have seen it. Her sister waits for the end and then says, “I used to hate taking you to that place.”

“You did?”

“I was fifteen. Mum paid me a quid. There was a boy in the record shop I’d have liked to spend my Saturdays looking at. Instead I spent them inhaling whatever they spray into boots.”

All these years Jo has put her sister into the memory with a little smile on her face. There is plenty of evidence that she sat by the rail. The smile appears to have been added during restoration. It stings a bit to scrape it off. Then something much nicer comes into view. Her sister hated going, and when she saw the beginners’ course in the leisure centre window thirty years later she went in and bought four Fridays for Jo.

“What was the boy called?”

“Christ. No idea. Remembered your bloody skate size, though, didn’t I?”

The good shoes

There’s an open session the following Saturday. Jo asks Sam to come for the last twenty minutes. She tells him exactly what she wants. If she gets as far as the vending machine again, she’d like someone there to see it who knew her before she could get as far as the vending machine. He’s free that afternoon. She also asks if he’d like to go every Saturday, an idea whose appeal to him she may have slightly overestimated.

“Every Saturday for how long?”

“Well. In my head we’re both still quite spry in our seventies.”

“Can we do this one and see?”

She considers the possibility that he loves her, wants to see this particular thing and would prefer to retain a few Saturdays in his seventies for his own purposes. It takes a little getting used to. She says yes.

At twenty to four he gets to the side door. Jo has left his name at the desk. A woman in a green fleece stamps the inside of his wrist with a flamingo that will remain legible through two subsequent showers. He can hear the wheels long before he sees anybody using them. A single skater makes a pleasant soft sound. Twenty together on a wooden floor sound like a chest of drawers attempting to flee a piano recital.

Jo sees him and comes over to the barrier, travelling the last few inches by hauling on the rail. She hands him her trainers. She’s been keeping them under the bench and somebody has already put one in the lost-property bin twice. His first duty as a witness is to take charge of the shoes.

She pushes off. It is initially very difficult to tell that anything is happening. She’s facing him. The woman in the yellow socks is alongside her, facing the other way. For a few seconds the two of them keep pace. If all you saw was their faces you might think they were having a stationary conversation under peculiar difficulties. Then a section of railing slides into view behind Jo’s shoulder. She’s moving. He can see the gap opening between her back wheels and a dark knot in the floor.

This is how big the thing looks from where he sits. A woman has gone a couple of metres across a leisure centre. A child goes past her so fast his T-shirt lifts. There is an audience of one for whom the significant event taking place in this building is the passage of a single adult woman past a knot in a plank. That’s quite a privilege, knowing where to look.

At the fire door Jo begins a second run. Left foot, right foot. She gets past the machine and a good yard beyond it before she has to catch the rail. Her first look is at the floor. Her second is at Sam. He is clapping rather badly, since he still has a shoe on each hand. His left shoe would like a word with his right about taking some of the skin off a knuckle. She starts to laugh and has to get a firmer hold on the rail.

“That far?” she calls.

“Past the orange drink.”

“Didn’t know you could see that from there.”

For the next few laps she barely looks over. She likes that he came. She also likes having a stretch of floor on which, having been seen, she’s free to get on with something nobody needs to see. Sam watches a bit longer, then reads the list of provisions available in the event of a children’s party. Twelve sausage rolls, access to one dedicated party host and a choice of squash. There’s a service agreement he feels sufficiently qualified to enter into.

On the way out the woman in the green fleece asks if Jo is coming back. They could use a hand gathering the cones once the beginners are off the floor next week. Jo is about to say every Saturday when she catches herself. She can do next Saturday. One day goes into the book beside her name. The woman lends her a pencil from behind her ear. Jo writes her name and tucks it behind her own.

At the doors Sam holds out her shoes. He wants to say how glad he is that she’s found something she can do for the rest of her life. Fortunately, before these inspiring and entirely unrequested words come out, she looks at the clock. There are still two minutes on the floor before the birthday party starts. A single orange cone sits near the drinks machine.

“Can I try something?” she calls to the woman at the desk.

Sam sits back down with her shoes. There’s just time to get his phone out, unlock it, find the camera and miss the whole thing. He leaves it in his pocket.

On her first pass Jo clips the cone and sends it spinning. There’s a minute left. She comes round again, gets her fingers under the rim and straightens. She’s still going backwards. There’s a pencil behind her ear.
