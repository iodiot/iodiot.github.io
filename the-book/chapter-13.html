Chapter 13Love Is Not a Customer Service Agreement

The box on the form says Relationship to participant. Jo has written depends how badly I fall.

“Apparently they want something shorter,” she says. “Can I put you down as my emergency contact?”

Sam comes over with her coffee. She’s still stretched across the couch where he found her when he got back from the pool hall. There’s a faint blue thumbprint on the mug. He reads the form over her shoulder. “Roller skating? Since when?”

“Since I was eleven. I took thirty years off.”

Her sister has given her four Friday-night lessons for her birthday and found her a secondhand pair of skates with yellow laces. Jo wants to learn to go backward. When she was little, a woman at the rink used to collect the orange cones at closing time. She’d glide backward down the middle, bending to lift each cone into the stack in her hand. On the last lap she’d be carrying a tower up to her chin. Everyone else was a kid in rental skates trying to stay on speaking terms with their feet. This woman wore her own skates and had a pencil behind her ear. Jo thought she was the most beautiful person in the world.

“Did you know her name?”

“God, no. What was I going to do, introduce myself and ask if she had any openings in cones?”

Sam settles for partner on the form. At the animal hospital the same box says owner and nobody argues. Partner fits in a small white rectangle. It tells whoever calls from the rink which number gets the news about the wrist.

The form has a line for that number. It has no line for what the partner is expected to do on a Friday night when the participant walks out of a closing building with two sore shins and he’s given his word he’ll be there. That would take a much bigger form. Jo would fill in a good part of it one way. Sam would fill it in another. And each believes the other signed whole pages of it years ago.

Nine at the side door

The first week Jo comes home delighted to have fallen in company. A man twice her size went down beside her so slowly and politely that she had time to move her hand from the piece of floor he was about to occupy. The second week she manages half a lap backward holding the instructor’s hand. The third week their one car is at Dan’s dealership waiting on a part, and she comes off the floor early to catch the bus. Just as she’s unlacing her skates the others get to try it on their own. Through the glass she sees a woman in yellow socks lift both arms in astonishment at her own moving body. Jo thinks about those yellow socks the whole way home.

“Last class tomorrow,” she tells Sam on Thursday. “Meet me at nine at the side door and walk home with me? Then I can stay till the end.”

“Nine it is.”

They both like the walk along the old rail trail. Together it takes about half an hour. Alone at that hour on a Friday, with the trail empty, Jo takes the bus. The car is still at the dealership, where Dan describes the part as on its way. The last bus that goes straight to their street leaves at eight fifty-two. The bus schedule and the schedule of people’s lives appear to have been drawn up in separate buildings by men in the middle of a divorce.

Nobody needs a psychology degree to work out where the man is supposed to be at nine.

At twenty to nine on Friday he’s still at home, on the phone with Ellen. Since the layoff the company pays her by the job to check what the drafting program draws. She’s found two different measurements for the same bracket in a guide that’s about to go to print. The printer needs the correction by Monday morning. Sam has an old file somewhere. He can hear the small catch in her voice that means she’s afraid it’s her mistake. A freelancer with a mistake in print doesn’t get the next guide. Ellen needs the next guide. Her car needs an alternator. He approved the thing that took her desk. He isn’t going to be the one who tells her he has somewhere to be. He’d like to find the file while she’s on the line and let her have a weekend. It’s a considerate instinct. It gets more expensive once he starts paying for it with somebody else’s Friday.

At eight forty-five he sees Jo’s message asking whether they’re still on. He could write, I’m at home. I’ll be late. She’s standing by the lockers with her coat in her hand. She could still get the bus.

Yep. On my way, he writes. He’s on the couch. His slippers offer a dissenting view.

He finds the file. The wrong measurement came from the supplier. Ellen sighs with relief. She says, “Oh God, have I kept you?” He says, “You’re fine,” which is accurate about Ellen. By the time he gets his shoes on, the bus has gone and so has Jo’s chance of making a different decision. He calls her from the doorstep and admits he’s only just leaving. He’s forty-six. The textbook formula, 220 minus his age, says his heart tops out at 174 beats a minute. In 2002 two exercise physiologists went looking for where the formula came from, found no original research behind it and concluded it had no scientific merit.[1] He jogs most of the way anyway, which is one way to check. He reaches the side door at nine seventeen, just as a cab turns in.

Jo gets in the back and moves over. It’s raining. He gets in too. A tiny knitted strawberry hangs from the rearview mirror, swaying agreeably each time the driver takes a corner. Sam has seldom wanted so badly to have a conversation about a strawberry.

“I was helping Ellen,” he says at last.

“I know. You told me on the phone when you actually left.”

“We had two different numbers.”

“I believe you, Sam.”

He has more where that came from. A time-stamped email. A photograph of a bracket. He could get Ellen on speaker. He’s brought a whole presentation, and it irritates him to find that nobody in the cab has asked the question it answers.

At the apartment Jo pays the fare of thirteen dollars and sixty cents. She keeps the receipt. Sam wants credit for the fact that he ran. He can feel the ache under his ribs and it seems a shame to waste it. He’d also like last Tuesday counted, when he picked up her prescription on his lunch break, and the night he sat up with her dad watching a documentary about paving. Ideally somebody would have kept a receipt for the paving. At the end of the second hour a man in a fleece compared three kinds of sand.

Before we give him too hard a time, think of the last time someone you love was reasonably pissed off at you. Did your mind stick to the point? Or did a junior employee somewhere behind your forehead start pulling receipts going back to the first time you bought this ungrateful bastard a croissant? Mine keeps impeccable files. Lose a set of keys and the department closes for lunch. Ask for a kindness nobody thanked me for in 2014 and a boy on a bicycle will have it on your desk before noon.

The paving really happened. So did Tuesday. Here’s what Jo lost tonight. At eight forty-five she had a choice between catching the bus and staying on the floor for one more try. She put her coat down because a person she had good reason to trust told her he was on his way. Sam can replace thirteen dollars and sixty cents. All the paving documentaries in the world won’t get her back to that locker at eight forty-five.

One point for Tricia

In an experiment published in 1979, Margaret Clark and Judson Mills gave male students a task with letters and let them believe they could pass some of theirs to a young woman named Tricia, who appeared to have a harder set. The men helped. Tricia appeared to finish in time to earn four points toward possible course credit, and a folded note came back. For some men it held a thank-you. For others it held a thank-you and one of her points.[2]

The researchers also varied what each man then heard about her. Either her husband would be along shortly, or she was new to the university and didn’t know many people. This was 1979, and the experiment placed a period-specific bet on what an undergraduate man would infer. The men given reason to hope for something more personal liked Tricia less, on average, when she returned a point. The men expecting the husband liked her more.

One point, traveling from one room to the next, can carry two different messages. You’ve done me a good turn and I’d like to give something back. Or, we’re square now and there’s no particular reason for us to see each other again. These were brief encounters in an arranged setting, measured in ratings of liking. Nobody in the lab had spent a wet February looking for a nursing home with Tricia. The meaning people read into repayment is still big enough to cause trouble in a cab.

Suppose you visit a friend after an operation and wash her hair over the kitchen sink. She’s frightened of getting the dressing wet. You cup your hand along her forehead and she tips her head back into it. Afterward she puts a folded twenty on the counter. If you’re a hairdresser who gave up a paying appointment to be there, you may be touched that she remembered. If the two of you have washed each other’s hair since you were small, you might want to leave quickly before she sees what’s happened to your face. Same bill. Same clean head. The two of you are finding out what kind of thing you just did.

People who say love doesn’t keep score have generally had a look at the score. The other person remembers which nights the pharmacy stays open, has paid the cab fare three Fridays running and can show you what unmeasured devotion looks like on a bank statement. Counting can be the first honest thing that happens in a relationship.

In a decent relationship you can produce the count without having to keep it for the rest of your natural life. You need to find out whether the other person can take in what it says and let it change what happens next Thursday. If the reply every time is that people who really loved each other wouldn’t write these things down, I’d get a bigger notebook.

Jo takes off her damp socks and hangs one over the tub. Sam sends her the fare out of his own spending money, rounded up to fourteen dollars. Forty cents of remorse. Then he knocks on the open bathroom door.

“I could have told you I was still here,” he says. “You asked me in time. I said I’d left because I wanted a few more minutes and I knew you’d take the bus if I told you.”

This is the first account of the evening in which Jo recognizes herself as one of the people who was there.

She tells him something in return. “I kept looking at the little window in the door. Thought I might see your face.”

“What, before nine?”

“There’s a bench where you can watch.”

“But you asked me to meet you outside.”

“Yes,” says Jo. It’s a nuisance how exactly he’s remembered this.

Until now she’s had a tidy grievance. It fits in her hand and has the cab company’s name printed across the top. Now she’s holding a second thing she doesn’t much want to take out in the light. She wanted him to come in early and see what she could do. He agreed to meet her when the room was closing. She could have said eight fifty. Some part of her felt that an invitation issued in plain English would spoil the result.

If you’ve ever wanted somebody to bring you flowers and then had to explain, to your own distress, that you no longer wanted the flowers now that they knew you wanted them, you’ve been down this particular manhole. What you were hoping for was news of what happened in another person’s mind while you were absent. Daffodils are widely available. A certificate stating that these daffodils occurred spontaneously to a mildly distracted mammal is a much more specialized product.

What I like about Jo is that she can see the problem and still badly wants the certificate.

The woman who hates being watched

As it happens, Sam has done his research on the woman he lives with. On their third date he watched her back a borrowed van into a space between two dumpsters. She lowered the window and asked whether there was anything else he’d like tickets for. He turned his back. When he looked again she had parked the van and bought him an ice cream. On the strength of one memorable afternoon he’s come to believe Jo hates being watched while she’s learning to do something.

In fairness to Sam, learning in public has a poor safety record. In 1760, by the usual account, the inventor John Joseph Merlin wore wheeled shoes of his own design to a masquerade ball in London and rolled in playing the violin. He had no way to slow down and no way to steer. He went into a large mirror. The mirror broke. So did the violin, and so, in places, did Merlin. Skates that turned easily had to wait for 1863, when James Plimpton set the wheels in two side-by-side pairs.[3] Jo’s yellow laces are threaded through a descendant of Plimpton’s skate. What she wants from Sam is Merlin’s audience without Merlin’s mirror.

His belief has served them both well. He’s the person who leaves the room when she’s calling about a job. Sam’s willingness to go away is one of the things she loves about him. The annoying part of being loved intelligently is that sometimes you have to send out a correction.

“You made such a big deal about the van,” he says.

“I was driving somebody else’s van into a dumpster, Sam. How many years were you planning to get out of that?”

Jo’s been keeping a picture of Sam too. He’s the man who sees a woman struggling and offers to draw her a little diagram. She has years of good material. Once, after she mentioned losing a glove, he sent her a drawing of two gloves joined by a length of elastic. She opened it on a train and frightened a fellow passenger by laughing. She remembers the elastic. She’s let herself forget the fellow passenger. Somewhere along the way a thing that made her laugh on a train got filed under what’s wrong with him.

Iris Murdoch built a thought experiment out of this habit. In an essay called “The Idea of Perfection” she imagines a mother, M, and a daughter-in-law, D. M thinks her son has married beneath him. She doesn’t like how D dresses or talks. She also has very good manners, so from the outside you’d see a mother-in-law being consistently courteous. Murdoch wants to know whether something morally important can happen while an observer detects no change in M’s behavior at all.[4]

M begins to suspect her picture has been arranged to flatter the painter. Maybe she’s a snob. Maybe she’s jealous. She looks again and finds, in conduct she’d dismissed as childish, the energy of somebody young. Murdoch goes so far as to allow that D might by now be dead. The change takes place in M, with no fresh performance by D and no opportunity for the daughter-in-law to improve her table manners.

It’s a demanding idea of what we owe each other. A person can behave correctly toward you for years and still do you wrong in the privacy of their own head. Picture the old M putting a hot water bottle in D’s bed every winter night and hearing, each time D opens her mouth, a woman who married above her station. The bottle is warm. The kindness is real. D still can’t get warm in that house.

Jo is sitting on the edge of the tub, still holding one wet sock. Here’s what she can ask of Sam. He could retire the woman in the van as the sole authority on what she wants. He could try an ordinary question next time. Here’s what she can offer him. When he says she told him to wait outside, she can admit that he paid her the compliment of listening. Being properly listened to sometimes results in getting the exact thing you asked for, a fate that could befall any of us.

She puts the sock down. “I wanted one try where I could turn around and say, did you see that?”

“Did you do it?”

“Yes.”

“Oh, Jo. How far?”

“From the fire door to the soda machine.”

It’s a respectable distance, particularly for a person who could see where she’d been better than she could see where she was going. She’s pleased he asked the second question. He’s pleased she told him. The cost of the cab still belongs to him.

A little generous in the light

So should Sam trade in his picture of Jo for an accurate one? Watch her on the rink. Her knees wobble. She holds her arms as if carrying a valuable cake across a boat. You could describe all that in detail and leave out the fact that she keeps coming off the rail. Sam, who loves her, sees somebody brave. He has his reasons.

In a study published in 1996, Sandra Murray, John Holmes and Dale Griffin followed dating couples. Both partners completed measures three times over a year. People who began with more idealized views of one another tended to report a more favorable change in satisfaction as the year went on. Their relationships were also more likely to last.[5] Nobody handed out the rosy view at random, so the study can’t say it caused the lasting. It does make it harder to argue that a cold eye is the best equipment to bring on a first date.

The generous picture has a second use, though. Suppose Sam is committed to having a brave girlfriend because he likes the sound of it at dinner. She tells him she’s frightened and wants to go home. He puts her back on the floor because he knows what a courageous woman she is. The same flattering adjective can keep somebody company all the way back to the rail or pry their fingers off it. There are people crying in locked bathrooms because everybody outside has been so supportive about their legendary resilience.

Sam keeps a flattering picture of himself, too. He likes being dependable. When Ellen called about the bracket, he liked that of all the people she could have called, she’d called him. Telling her he had to leave at eight thirty would have meant hanging up while she was still worried. A man who cares can do that. A man who needs to see the grateful look on everybody’s face before he leaves is going to be late a lot.

On Monday he sends Ellen the old supplier files so she has them herself. “Good,” she says. “I felt awful about keeping you. You did say you had time.”

On Tuesday night Jo’s sister calls. Did she enjoy the birthday lessons? Jo tells her about the woman in yellow socks, about the soda machine, even about wanting Sam to have seen it. Her sister waits for the end and then says, “I used to hate taking you to that place.”

“You did?”

“I was fifteen. Mom paid me a buck. There was a boy in the record store I’d have liked to spend my Saturdays looking at. Instead I spent them inhaling whatever they spray into rental skates.”

All these years Jo has put her sister into the memory with a little smile on her face. Her sister did sit by the rail. The smile appears to have been added during restoration. It stings to scrape it off. Underneath is something better. Her sister hated going, and when she saw the beginners’ class in the rink window thirty years later she went in and bought four Fridays for Jo.

“What was the boy’s name?”

“Christ. No idea. Remembered your damn skate size, though, didn’t I?”

The good shoes

The rink has an open session the following Saturday. Jo asks Sam to come for the last twenty minutes. She tells him exactly what she wants. If she gets as far as the soda machine again, she’d like someone there to see it who knew her before she could get as far as the soda machine. She also asks if he’d like to come every Saturday, an idea whose appeal to him she may have overestimated.

“Every Saturday for how long?”

“Well. In my head we’re both still pretty spry in our seventies.”

Sam is forty-six. Seventy is twenty-four years off. Twenty-four times fifty-two is 1,248 Saturdays, give or take a hip.

“Can we do this one and see?”

She considers the possibility that he loves her, wants to see this particular thing and would like to keep a few Saturdays in his seventies for his own purposes. It takes a little getting used to. She says yes.

At twenty to four he gets to the side door. A woman in a green fleece stamps the inside of his wrist with a flamingo that will stay legible through two showers. He can hear the wheels long before he sees anybody using them. A single skater makes a pleasant soft sound. Twenty together on a wooden floor sound like a dresser trying to flee a piano recital.

Jo sees him and comes over to the wall, traveling the last few inches by hauling on the rail. She hands him her sneakers. His first job as her audience is to take charge of the shoes. He sits on the bench holding them up by the laces, like a man who’s brought flowers.

She pushes off. At first it’s hard to tell that anything is happening. She’s facing him. The woman in the yellow socks is alongside her, facing the other way. For a few seconds the two of them keep pace. Then a section of railing slides into view behind Jo’s shoulder. She’s moving. He can see the gap opening between her back wheels and a dark knot in the floor.

This is how big the thing looks from where he sits. A woman has gone a couple of yards across a rink. A child goes past her so fast his T-shirt lifts. The building holds an audience of one for whom the significant event of the afternoon is the passage of a single adult woman past a knot in a plank. It’s a privilege, knowing where to look.

At the fire door Jo begins a second run. Left foot, right foot. She gets past the machine and a good yard beyond it before she has to catch the rail. Her first look is at the floor. Her second is at Sam. He’s clapping badly, since he now has a shoe on each hand. His left shoe would like a word with his right about the skin on a knuckle. She starts to laugh and has to get a firmer hold on the rail.

“That far?” she calls.

“Past the orange drink.”

“Didn’t know you could see that from there.”

For the next few laps she barely looks over. She likes that he came. She also likes having a stretch of floor on which, having been seen, she’s free to get on with something nobody needs to see. Sam watches a bit longer, then reads the list of provisions available in the event of a children’s party. Twelve pigs in a blanket, access to one dedicated party host and a choice of juice boxes. There’s a service agreement he feels qualified to enter into.

On the way out the woman in the green fleece asks if Jo is coming back. They could use a hand gathering the cones once the beginners are off the floor next week. Jo is about to say every Saturday when she catches herself. She can do next Saturday. One day goes into the book beside her name. The woman lends her a pencil from behind her ear. Jo writes her name and tucks the pencil behind her own.

At the doors Sam holds out her shoes. He wants to say how glad he is that she’s found something she can do for the rest of her life. Fortunately, before these inspiring and entirely unrequested words come out, she looks at the clock. Two minutes are left on the floor before the birthday party starts. A single orange cone sits near the soda machine.

“Can I try something?” she calls to the woman at the desk.

Sam sits back down with her shoes. There’s just time to get his phone out, unlock it, find the camera and miss the whole thing. He leaves it in his pocket.

On her first pass Jo clips the cone and sends it spinning. There’s a minute left. She comes around again, gets her fingers under the rim and straightens. She’s still going backward. There’s a pencil behind her ear.
