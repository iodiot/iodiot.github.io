Chapter 12The Loneliness Paradox

“You interviewed a sprout,” Dan says.

Of all the answers Sam was prepared for when he got into his brother’s car to ask what had been so good about that Christmas, this one was quite a long way down.

“It had rolled into one of the little dents in the ironing board. You put your spoon up to it like a microphone. Asked how it felt to be going home so early in the tournament.”

“What did it say?”

“Nothing. That was what was so funny. You waited for an answer. Mum had to go in the hall.”

Dan can still see her through the frosted glass. One wobbly pink shoulder, going up and down. Sam had thought she’d gone out because she was upset about the table. It appears he’s been feeling sorry for the wrong woman for almost thirty years.

They sit for another minute. Upstairs a tap runs. The little girl who insisted on doing the washing-up will have soaked most of her front by now. Dan picks his keys off the dash, then puts them down again.

“I miss being the sort of person who gets to hear what the sprout thinks,” he says.

A remarkable sentence to find yourself saying on a public street, even with the windows up. Sam would like to know what it means. Dan finds it surprisingly difficult to tell him. Things are good. The kids are good. Work is all right. If he got stuck halfway up a ladder with his trousers on fire, six people in his phone would come and get him, and a seventh would already be editing the video. He knows this. Some nights he sits on the arm of the sofa after everybody’s in bed and scrolls through those names. There isn’t anybody he can think of ringing to say he’s sitting on the arm of the sofa.

“Ring me,” Sam says.

“And say what?”

There it is. Any man with a burning arse has a perfectly good opening line. What happens to the rest of us?

A man in considerable demand

The following Saturday at his daughter’s school, Dan is the person with the extension lead. Sam knows this because three adults tell him so on his way from the gate to the hall. His brother has lent the school two trestle tables, brought change for the raffle and established which of the wall sockets actually supplies electricity. A boy in a cardboard crown has followed him into the gents to report a balloon emergency. In any fair system of government this would entitle Dan to land.

He quite likes being the man you look for when something goes wrong. Somebody comes towards him with their eyebrows up around their hairline. He fiddles with a plug. The eyebrows go down. You can see what you’ve done for another human being. Try getting that kind of confirmation after saying you’d quite like company.

By half past twelve he has spoken to dozens of people. A parent has remembered he takes his tea with a drop of milk. Somebody has put aside a slice of the good lemon cake for him. A girl he’s never met has confided that the papier-mâché badger in the craft display is hollow and there’s a grape in its head. For five whole minutes his afternoon promises to go somewhere interesting. Then a tap starts dripping into the box of programmes and he’s got to find a towel.

In the lull before the raffle, Sam finds him sitting on the stage steps eating the lemon cake off a folded paper napkin. There’s a damp patch on the knee of his jeans. “Sometimes,” Dan says, “I think about who’d come to my funeral.”

Sam sits down next to him. “Everybody in here, I should think.”

“Yeah. Who’d put the chairs out?”

Over at the far wall a woman has formed an intimate attachment to the wrong end of a length of cable. Dan starts to get up. She waves him down. “I only wanted to know where it goes,” she calls. Both men begin to laugh. Dan has a sudden, highly satisfactory vision of himself at his own funeral, giving directions from a horizontal position.

People do this rather nasty bit of arithmetic when someone they know says they’re lonely. We run a quick inventory of the friends they have, the people who love them, the perfectly good cake in their lap. Then we offer the inventory back as grounds for dismissing the complaint. How can you be lonely? Look at everybody who turned up. If I tell you my feet hurt, do you come round to count my shoes?

There is a reason the people who design surveys on loneliness actually ask the person. The UK’s Office for National Statistics treats social isolation and loneliness as distinct. You can count how often somebody has contact with other people. Finding out whether they feel short of the company they want calls for a different question. The guidance even advises against getting another member of the household to answer it for them.[1] The person who knows how often you go out could be asleep by the time you sit down on the arm of the sofa.

Dan would have trouble ticking a box himself. On the school steps his cake tastes good. His daughter runs past with something purple in her hair and calls him Dad in the falling, two-note way she’s been saying it since she could talk. For that particular noise he’d put the chairs out twice. What gets him is the journey home after a day like this. He’s been in company since breakfast. He tries to remember what he talked about. There was a minor crisis over whether the raffle could proceed without the ticket numbered 200. A grape is slowly undergoing changes in the skull of a badger. Something he’s been wanting to say for weeks has made it through another day without coming up.

You can see how this keeps happening. Ask a useful man how he is while handing him a broken microphone and there are two questions on the table. One of them has a loose battery cover. Dan could leave it broken for another twenty seconds. He could say his piece before he fixes it. Conversely you could put the bloody thing behind your back until he’s finished answering. Neither possibility has occurred to anybody in the hall, including Dan. If this were a conspiracy, it would be unusually well staffed.

But suppose we could see what every one of these people did once they went home. Who ate alone? Who slipped gratefully out of the kitchen for ten glorious minutes in the shed? Who sat quite close to somebody they loved, listening for the break in the television programme in which they might get to speak? Dan sees them at the gate on the way in, when every one of them has, by definition, turned up. Watching the arrivals is an odd way to get an impression of everybody else’s life.

The popular bastard in the middle

There’s a small piece of mathematics hiding at this school fair. Take a pencil and draw four people on the back of a raffle ticket. We’ll call them Jo, Al, Mo and Kit. In our tiny universe Jo is friends with the other three. They don’t know each other. Draw the three lines. That’s the whole society. Its chief constitutional weakness is what happens if Jo gets the flu.

How many friends does a person in this world have? Jo has three. The others have one each. Add those numbers and divide by four people. Six divided by four gives you an average of one and a half. We have achieved a statistical description of life in which no actual person has one and a half friends, although anybody who’s ever helped someone move a piano and got half a pizza for it may recognise the sentiment.

Now have each person write down the names of all their friends. Mix the six slips in a bowl and draw one. Jo’s name is on three slips. Al, Mo and Kit turn up once apiece. Half the time you’ll pull out somebody with three friends. The other half you’ll pull out somebody with one. Average the friend counts of the people whose names are in the bowl and you get two. Same four people, same three friendships. The bowl is generally having a better social life than the village.

This is the effect the sociologist Scott Feld set out in a 1991 paper on why your friends have more friends than you do.[2] A person with many friends supplies their name to many people’s lists. If you survey the entries on those lists, the well-connected are more heavily represented. It works even when every single person tells the truth. Nobody had to airbrush their kitchen or photograph a dinner party from three angles to give the impression of a larger dinner party.

What you sampled makes a difference. In our drawing Jo can look at all her own friends and correctly conclude that every one has fewer friends than she does. She might also be the loneliest person on the ticket. There’s no pencil mark for whether she can turn up at Mo’s with her collar on inside out and ask to lie on his floor. You could collect a thousand more slips from the same bowl and still know bugger all about that.

What the little model does explain is part of a sour feeling you may have had at a party. You’re standing by the crisps counting how many people seem to know the man in the blue shirt. My God, here comes another one. You’ve personally managed one conversation tonight and that was with a small child who wanted the bowl. Before drawing any conclusions about your adequacy, consider how you came to pick this particular man out of a room full of shirts. Somebody keeps going up and saying hello to him. There’s a quiet fellow three feet to your left. You’ve scarcely registered his existence. He is currently counting all the people who seem to know the man in the blue shirt.

Dan is quite often the blue shirt. Sam has seen him walk into their local and get three nods on his way to the bar. He has never thought to ask how many of those people know what Dan does on the arm of the sofa. He’s asking his brother a version of that question when the raffle starts. A boy in a crown wins a bottle of gin and must enter immediate negotiations with his mother. The day has finally produced a crisis worthy of its equipment.

Afterwards the brothers stack chairs. Sam gets five into a pile before realising he’s put the first one round the wrong way. He nearly asks Dan to sort it out. This time he does something more difficult. He turns five chairs round.

“Who would you have rung?” he asks. “Before all this. When you didn’t have anything much to say.”

“Mal, I suppose.”

Sam knows the name. He used to see it above his brother’s on the snooker club board, where it was written in white chalk with a small crown over the M. He hasn’t thought about it in years.

“How is he?”

“No idea,” Dan says. He has the air of a man who’s just found a date on a packet in his fridge and would rather nobody asked him to read the year aloud.

Someone who knows where the story starts

Mal could balance a cube of blue chalk on the end of his nose. He’d only do it when his opponent was lining up a shot, which tells you most of what you need to know about him as a sportsman. Dan used to play him on a Thursday evening. They began going when they were nineteen and still went in the year Dan’s first kid was born, although for a while there Dan had to hold a cue in one hand and a baby monitor in the other. This arrangement worked beautifully for the twenty minutes they played on the little table in his front room. Then the infant developed a very strong opinion about snooker.

There was one thing Dan could tell Mal which he couldn’t tell Sam. He could tell him what it had felt like to be the older brother the year Sam started at the same school. How teachers who knew Dan’s name suddenly had this other little chap to ask about. How nice it was to see his brother across the yard. How even nicer, on certain days, to go somewhere the little chap couldn’t follow. Sam was central to the story and uniquely badly placed to hear it. Mal could take the piss out of both of them in one sentence. For a boy who worried that enjoying an afternoon away from his brother made him a bad brother, this was something in the nature of a public service.

Then Mal moved away. His place on Thursdays remained vacant for a bit. One Thursday Dan stayed home with a child who had an earache. Somebody scheduled a late meeting the following week. His cue ended up behind the coats. The two men exchanged birthday messages. Last year Dan received a small animated pint performing a dance that, in an actual pub, would have got the pint barred. He sent one back. That tiny beer has maintained a relationship its human sponsors haven’t touched in two years.

Listen to the change in the cost of making a phone call. At nineteen, Dan could pick up the phone in the middle of a sentence. “Still think you’d make that shot?” The person answering already had the beginning. Two years go by and Dan feels he ought to begin by explaining the two years. He owes Mal an apology, except that Mal hasn’t rung him either, so now he has to word the apology in a way that doesn’t sound like an accusation. He should probably open by asking after Mal’s family. His hand pauses over the screen. Was it Mal’s mum or his aunt who’d been ill? An opportunity has arisen to offer a living woman his sincere condolences on her death. The little beer starts to look like a sound piece of engineering.

In a series of studies published in 2024, Lara Aknin and Gillian Sandstrom looked at precisely this gap. In two studies people identified an old friend they wanted to reconnect with, expected would welcome hearing from them and knew how to contact. They had time, there and then, to compose a message. Fewer than a third reported sending one.[3] An eagerness to reconnect and a thumb willing to press Send appear to be sold separately.

Think how much the experimenters had already cleared off the table. No detective work required. No need to ask your mother if she still has an envelope with a return address on it. The intended recipient was someone the participant actually wanted to reach. Even in those circumstances, most kept the message to themselves. The result tells us something about the person holding the phone. It can’t tell us what your particular old friend would say on the other end. The beer might be the full extent of Mal’s present ambition. He has had two years in which to become somebody Dan hasn’t met.

This is where a lot of cheerful advice does a little damage. Just reach out is cheap to say when you picture two people who lost touch because the calendar got full. People also lose touch because one made the other feel small. Or one kept ringing from the pub to ask for a lift and couldn’t remember the lift the next day. You can miss the friend who knew your dad before he got sick and be perfectly right to let a call from that friend go to voicemail. Sometimes the person you miss has died. You still know their number by heart. The technical ease of making a call has remarkably little bearing on whether the person you want is available to answer it.

None of those things happened between Dan and Mal. Something less dramatic and therefore harder to explain to yourself happened. The last time they spoke, Mal was telling him about a training course he might take. Dan was putting shelf brackets up and got the rawlplug into the hole at exactly the moment his friend was saying why he wanted a change. Dan made the satisfied little grunt of a man who’s got a rawlplug into a hole. Mal said he’d better let him get on. If only somebody had smashed a window. There’d have been a recognisable occasion on which to come back and put things right.

“I really miss him,” Dan says. He’s surprised by how easily it comes out with his head stuck in the school’s lost-property cupboard. There are seventeen left gloves in here and what appears to be a set of false teeth. He decides a parent can take some responsibility for reclaiming those without a personal appeal.

Sam doesn’t say anything for a second. He’s been hoping, all afternoon, for his brother to say what he’s actually feeling. Now Dan has done so, Sam would like a minor edit. He’d hoped the person Dan really missed might be him.

That’s worth sitting with. Here’s a man who went back downstairs on Sunday to ask his brother a question because he genuinely wanted to know the answer. Six days later he has an answer. He’s hurt by it. We like the idea of being someone another person can tell absolutely anything to. We tend to picture the absolutely anything as a difficult truth that will reflect rather well on our listening skills. A difficult truth which involves our being the second choice for Thursday night requires additional processing time.

“Would you go now?” Sam asks.

“To Mal’s? It’s seventy miles.”

“To the club. I’ve got an hour.”

Dan shuts the cupboard. “You hated snooker.”

“I’d forgotten. Thanks.”

A reasonably terrible frame

The woman on the desk at the club has a paperback open, face down, over one of the scoreboards. Dan says he hasn’t been in for years. She says all right. He’d been expecting the building, at least, to give some indication that a man who once spent three evenings a week inside it had been gone a long time. She asks whether they want the table for half an hour or an hour. He has to admit it’s a sensible question.

The cloth has been replaced. There’s a card machine where the jar of pickled eggs used to be, an improvement by almost any measure of human progress. There is still the little shelf beneath the mirror, precisely wide enough to put your pint down and walk into it backwards. Dan finds it with his hip. For about half a second he entirely expects to hear Mal say careful of that. It’s the speed of the expectation that catches him. He puts his pint somewhere else.

Aristotle, winding up a discussion of friendship, pays attention to what friends actually do with the hours they spend together. Some drink together, some play dice, some train or hunt, some do philosophy. People want company in the activities that make up a life they like living.[4] I appreciate the placement of philosophy on a list that also offers the possibility of a drink. It gives everybody at the table somewhere to go if the philosopher gets his notebook out.

The activity matters in ways that are easy to miss if you think of snooker as a pretext to get two men into a room to discuss their feelings. Mal has already lost the argument about whether it’s physically possible to pot that red from behind the pink. The red’s off the table. This establishes a condition familiar to close friends and almost impossible to reproduce at a networking lunch. One of you gets to be magnificently wrong about a small thing in full view of somebody who’ll enjoy it for years. Mal once took a shot so confidently he walked round to collect the ball from a pocket it had no intention of visiting. Who else can make Dan laugh by pointing at six inches of wood?

You accumulate a little language by being there when things happen. Point at the shelf. Tip an imaginary pint. Nobody else knows what’s funny. Years later you have a horrible day and would like to spend twenty minutes among people who speak it. A full account of yourself may be the very last thing you want. You’ve spent the day providing full accounts, complete with supporting documentation, to people who will shortly ask you to reset your password. You’d like somebody to understand careful of that.

It takes a while to acquire a language like that. This is especially uncomfortable when you’ve just moved somewhere. At your first choir rehearsal everyone laughs when the accompanist reaches the third verse. They may be the nicest people on earth. You’re still standing there holding the same music, unable to hear whatever else they heard. You could be brave all evening and go home feeling worse. At a later rehearsal somebody will tell you that last winter the fire door blew open on that exact note and half the tenors left the building in the sincere belief that God had called them personally. And eventually, if there’s a next winter and you can manage the shift changes and the bus fare, you might be there when something else happens. The first evening asked you to pay in advance for a thing the room couldn’t supply yet.

There’s money and time in that if. Take away the club, change a bus route, put a parent on alternating shifts, and it becomes harder for two people to keep a harmless argument going over several years. You can like each other with a quite exceptional degree of sincerity and still never both be free on a Thursday. The woman minding her dad may be offered a dozen excellent suggestions about joining something when what would actually give her an evening is somebody staying with him long enough for her to go out. See if you can find that option on the little leaflet about making friends.

Sam has been trying to hit a red for some time. He wants one decent shot before the hour is up. Dan starts to correct the angle of his elbow. It’s a nice automatic gesture, done with precisely the same wrist action he uses to stop a child from putting a finger in a socket.

“Will you let me miss the bloody thing?”

Dan puts his hand over his own mouth. Sam misses. The white ball travels the length of the table and comes back almost to the point from which it set out, having interfered as little as possible with the course of nature.

His brother has enough decency to wait for Sam to start laughing. Sam sees the tips of his ears going red with the effort and starts a little sooner than he might have done.

“Do the voice,” Dan says.

“What voice?”

“The man who interviews the sprout.”

So Sam leans over the cushion and asks the white ball how it feels to be back home after such an uneventful trip. In the library hush of a half-empty snooker club, a small middle-aged sound escapes from somewhere in Dan’s chest. The woman at the desk turns a page of her book.

What a person can reasonably know

They get their coats. Sam has chalk on the cuff of his and a streak of it under his chin. Dan sees it and, instead of rubbing it off for him, indicates the place on his own face. He has done a fair bit of work today on the complex moral question of what to do with his hands.

The strange thing is that he still misses Mal. He has had a thoroughly good hour in the company of somebody he loves. A small part of that hour has made him miss his friend more acutely. If you think of company as something like central heating, there seems to be a fault in the system. You have supplied an extra unit of warmth. Why is this particular patch still cold? But there are things people know about you because of where they were standing in 1998. No quantity of affection from a person who was then in another building can put them there. Sam can learn the story of the shelf and the pint. Mal can remember the noise the glass made.

Sam, in turn, has something Mal could never give Dan. He saw their mother through the glass of the hall door. For years he misunderstood what was happening on the other side. That’s how close you can be. In one family, a few feet of lino from the same woman, two boys took home different Christmases. A good conversation has given each of them a little more of the afternoon. Neither one got all of it. Their mum was entitled to part of it herself.

Outside the club, Dan takes out his phone. “What do I say to him?”

“The course,” says Sam. “Did he ever take it?”

Dan had already forgotten that he’d told Sam about the rawlplug in the school cupboard. His brother had been listening back there among the gloves.

He sends Mal a voice message. He tells him where he is. There’s a short silence while he tries to turn the next bit into something elegant. He gives up. “I was drilling a bloody hole the last time you tried to tell me about that course. Did you ever do it? I’d like to know.” He very nearly finishes by telling Mal to get back to him whenever, if he feels like it, absolutely no worries either way. Three successive ways of opening the door for his friend to leave. He presses Send.

No little burst of music comes out of the phone. The club door closes behind a man who has remembered to wipe the chalk off his face. Sam has wiped the wrong side and now has a matching set. Dan feels better for a moment, then considerably worse. He’s sent a message that can be heard without him there to explain it. This is one of the disadvantages of successfully making yourself heard.

On the walk home Sam admits he was hoping Dan missed spending time with him too. It costs him something to put that too at the end. It lets another person stay in the sentence.

“I do,” Dan says. “You keep wanting to have us all over. I thought perhaps you didn’t much enjoy just me.”

“Oh, for Christ’s sake,” says Sam, affectionately, to both of them.

They part at the crossing. Dan has promised his daughter he’ll tell her which stall won the vote for best exhibit, and he isn’t absolutely sure she knows that the care of a grape inside a badger involves a longer commitment than the one she’s made. Sam gets home to find Jo lying on the sofa. She’s put the cushions on the floor and stretched out the whole length of her body. He’s pleased she had an evening like this. He also has a great deal he wants to tell her. He puts the kettle on. It gives her a chance to say which sort of evening she’s still having.

Dan’s phone lights up the next morning. Mal has sent forty-seven seconds of audio. There’s wind in the microphone. He took the course. Got all the way through the training and spent six months doing the job before he discovered there was an aspect he absolutely hated. Can Dan talk later? Also, is that bastard shelf still up? Dan plays it back. Even after two years Mal retains a keen interest in Dan’s prospects of injuring himself.

By nine that night the two of them are on the phone. The kids are in bed. Dan starts to sit down on the arm of the sofa, then moves the pile of clean towels and makes room for himself on the seat. Mal is trying to tell him about those six months. It takes a few goes to explain why he left the barber’s where he first got a chair. Dan pictures Mal with a cut-throat razor and a customer sneezing at a critical juncture. He puts a hand over his mouth.

It was the bottles on the shelf behind him. The owner wanted every customer to leave with something extra. One Saturday Mal sold forty quid’s worth of thickening shampoo to his own dad.

“Was he losing his hair?”

“You met my dad. Where would he have put it?”

Dan laughs. Mal does too, then goes quiet. “I think he just wanted me to get on,” he says. “I knew that when I took his money.”

Down the line Dan can hear a teaspoon going round in a cup. Usually this is where he comes in with something useful. Tonight he lets his friend finish stirring.

After a bit, Mal carries on.
