Chapter 12The Loneliness Paradox

“You interviewed a Brussels sprout,” Dan says.

Sam got into his brother’s car to ask what had been so good about that Thanksgiving. Of all the answers he was ready for, this one wasn’t on the list.

“It rolled into one of the little dents in the ironing board. You put your spoon up to it like a microphone. Asked how it felt to be going home so early in the tournament.”

“What did it say?”

“Nothing. That’s what was so funny. You waited for an answer. Mom had to go out in the hall.”

Dan can still see her through the frosted glass. One wobbly pink shoulder, going up and down. Sam had thought she’d gone out because she was upset about the table. He’s spent more than thirty years feeling sorry for the wrong woman.

They sit for another minute. Upstairs a faucet runs. June insisted on doing the dishes, so she’ll have soaked most of her front by now. Dan picks his keys off the dash, then puts them down again.

“I miss being the kind of person who gets to hear what the sprout thinks,” he says.

It’s a strange sentence to hear yourself say on a public street, even with the windows up. Sam would like to know what it means. Dan has trouble telling him. Things are good. Carla’s good. The girls are good. At the dealership people are delighted to see him right up until he gives them the estimate. If he got stuck halfway up a ladder with his pants on fire, six people in his phone would come and get him. He knows this. Some nights he sits on the arm of the couch after everybody’s in bed and scrolls through those names. He can’t think of one he could call to say he’s sitting on the arm of the couch.

“Call me,” Sam says.

“And say what?”

Any man with his ass on fire has a good opening line. What happens to the rest of us?

A man in demand

The following Saturday at his daughters’ school, Dan is the person with the extension cord. Sam knows this because three adults tell him so between the door and the gym. His brother has lent the school two folding tables, brought change for the raffle and established which of the outlets actually supply electricity. A boy in a cardboard crown has followed him into the men’s room to report a balloon emergency. In any fair system of government this would entitle Dan to land.

He likes being the man you look for when something goes wrong. Somebody comes toward him with their eyebrows up around their hairline. He fiddles with a plug. The eyebrows go down. Try getting that kind of confirmation after saying you’d like some company.

By twelve-thirty he’s spoken to dozens of people. Four have asked how he’s doing, each while handing him something broken. Somebody has set aside a slice of the good lemon cake for him. A girl he’s never met has confided that the papier-mâché badger she made for the craft display is hollow and there’s a grape in its head. For five whole minutes his afternoon promises to go somewhere interesting. Then a faucet starts dripping into the box of programs and he has to find a towel.

In the lull before the raffle, Sam finds him sitting on the stage steps eating the lemon cake off a folded paper napkin. There’s a damp patch on the knee of his jeans. “Sometimes,” Dan says, “I think about who’d come to my funeral.”

Sam sits down next to him. “Everybody in here, probably.”

“Yeah. Who’d put the chairs out?”

Over at the far wall a woman has formed an intimate attachment to the wrong end of a length of cable. Dan starts to get up. She waves him down. “I only wanted to know where it goes,” she calls. Both men start to laugh. Dan can see the service now. A good turnout, standing room only, and the funeral director leaning over the casket with a knot of extension cord to ask where it lives. He’d point. He knows damn well he’d point.

Sam has just done the nasty bit of arithmetic we all do when somebody we love says a thing like this. He’s counted the friends, the full gym, the good cake in his brother’s lap, and handed the total back as proof that nothing’s wrong. How can you be lonely? Look at everybody who turned up. If I tell you my feet hurt, do you come over to count my shoes?

In July 1969 a good part of the planet did that count for one man and got it wrong in the other direction. Michael Collins stayed in the command module Columbia, circling the Moon, while Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin took the lander down. Every orbit carried him behind the Moon, where no radio could reach him, not from Houston and not from the two men below. A NASA commentator in Mission Control told the world that “not since Adam” had a human being known such solitude, forty-seven minutes of every orbit with nobody to talk to but his tape recorder.

Collins gave his own account five years later in a memoir, Carrying the Fire. He doesn’t deny the solitude. He’s “truly alone,” he writes, cut off from any known life. “I am it.” He even does the count. On the other side of the Moon are “three billion plus two” people. On his side, one man and God knows what. He feels this powerfully, he says, but “not as fear or loneliness.” He lists awareness, anticipation, satisfaction and confidence. “I like the feeling.”[1]

What frightened him was a different sum. If the lander’s engine didn’t light, he couldn’t go down and get them. Columbia wasn’t built to land. He’d have to fire his own engine and fly home beside two empty seats. He calls this his “secret terror” of the previous six months, and he writes that he’d have been a marked man for life. By his own account the far side of the Moon didn’t scare him. Coming home to three billion people did.

A test pilot writing up his best day isn’t a controlled experiment, and a job with a splashdown date isn’t a life. Still, look at the two counts. Collins had two thousand miles of rock between him and the nearest human being and wasn’t short of anybody. Dan has a full gym and he’s short by one. The US Surgeon General’s 2023 advisory, Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation, keeps the two words in its title apart on purpose. Social isolation is the part you can count from outside. Few relationships, few roles, not much contact. Loneliness is the distress of a gap between the connection a person has and the connection they want.[2] Nobody can measure the second kind from the door of a gym. You have to ask the man. Carla could tell you to the hour how often Dan goes out, and she’s asleep by the time he sits down on the arm of the couch.

Dan would have trouble answering for himself. June runs past with something purple in her hair and calls him Dad in the falling, two-note way she’s said it since she could talk. For that noise he’d put the chairs out twice. What gets him is the drive home after a day like this. He’s been in company since breakfast and he tries to remember what he talked about. A grape is slowly undergoing changes in the skull of a badger. Something he’s been wanting to say for weeks has made it through another day without coming up.

The popular bastard in the middle

A small piece of mathematics is hiding at this school fair. Take a pencil and draw four people on the back of a raffle ticket. We’ll call them Pat, Al, Mo and Lou. In our tiny universe Pat is friends with the other three. They don’t know each other. Draw the three lines. That’s the whole society. One funeral would finish it.

How many friends does a person in this world have? Pat has three. The others have one each. Add those numbers and divide by four people. Six divided by four gives an average of one and a half. No actual person has one and a half friends, although anybody who’s helped someone move a piano and gotten half a pizza for it may recognize the feeling.

Now have each person write down the names of all their friends. Mix the six slips in a bowl and draw one. Pat’s name is on three slips. Al, Mo and Lou turn up once apiece. Half the time you’ll pull out somebody with three friends. The other half you’ll pull out somebody with one. Average the friend counts of the names in the bowl and you get two. Same four people, same three friendships. The bowl is having a better social life than the town.

This is the effect the sociologist Scott Feld set out in a 1991 paper on why your friends have more friends than you do.[3] A person with many friends supplies their name to many people’s lists. Survey the entries on those lists and the well-connected get counted again and again. It works even when every single person tells the truth. Nobody had to photograph a dinner party from three angles to make it look like a bigger dinner party.

In our drawing Pat can look at her friends and correctly conclude that every one of them has fewer friends than she does. She might also be the loneliest person on the ticket. No pencil mark shows whether she can turn up at Mo’s with her collar inside out and ask to lie on his floor. You could pull a thousand more slips from the same bowl and still know squat about that.

What the little model does explain is part of a sour feeling you may have had at a party. You’re standing by the chips counting how many people seem to know the man in the blue shirt. You’ve managed one conversation tonight and that was with a small child who wanted the bowl. Consider how you came to pick this man out of a room full of shirts. Somebody keeps going up and saying hello to him. Three feet to your left stands a quiet guy you’ve barely registered. He’s currently counting all the people who seem to know the man in the blue shirt.

Dan is often the blue shirt. Sam has watched him walk into a bar and collect three nods before he reached a stool. He’s never thought to ask how many of those people know what Dan does on the arm of the couch. Then the raffle starts. A boy in a crown wins a gift certificate for a couples massage and must enter immediate negotiations with his mother. The day has finally produced a crisis worthy of its equipment.

Afterward the brothers stack chairs. Sam gets five into a pile before he realizes he’s put the first one on backward. He nearly asks Dan to fix it. This time he does something more difficult. He turns five chairs around.

“Who would you have called?” he asks. “Before all this. When you didn’t have anything much to say.”

“Mal, probably.”

Sam knows the name. He used to see it above his brother’s on the league board at the pool hall, written in white chalk with a small crown over the M.

“How is he?”

“No idea,” Dan says. He has the air of a man who’s just found a date on a package in his fridge and would rather nobody asked him to read the year aloud.

Someone who knows where the story starts

Mal could balance a cube of blue chalk on the end of his nose. He’d only do it when his opponent was lining up a shot, which tells you most of what you need to know about him as a sportsman. Dan played him on Thursday nights. They started going at nineteen and were still going the year Nora was born, although for a while there Dan had a cue in one hand and a baby monitor in the other.

Dan could tell Mal one thing he couldn’t tell Sam. He could tell him what it was like to be the older brother the year Sam started at the same school. How teachers who knew Dan’s name suddenly had this other little guy to ask about. How nice it was, some days, to go somewhere the little guy couldn’t follow. Sam was central to the story and uniquely badly placed to hear it. Mal could rip into both of them in one sentence. For a boy who worried that enjoying an afternoon away from his brother made him a bad brother, this was a public service.

Then Mal moved seventy miles away. One Thursday Dan was home with a child who had an earache. Somebody scheduled a late meeting the following week. His cue ended up behind the coats. The two men exchanged birthday messages. Last year Dan received a small animated beer mug performing a dance that, in an actual bar, would’ve gotten the mug eighty-sixed. He sent one back. That tiny beer has maintained a relationship its human sponsors haven’t touched in two years.

Listen to what’s happened to the cost of a phone call. At nineteen, Dan could pick up the phone in the middle of a sentence. “Still think you’d make that shot?” The person answering already had the beginning. Two years go by and Dan feels he should start by explaining the two years. He owes Mal an apology, except that Mal hasn’t called him either, so now he has to word the apology in a way that doesn’t sound like an accusation. He should probably open by asking after Mal’s family. His thumb stops over the screen. Was it Mal’s mom or his aunt who’d been sick? An opportunity has arisen to offer a living woman his sincere condolences on her death. The little beer starts to look like a sound piece of engineering.

In a series of studies published in 2024, Lara Aknin and Gillian Sandstrom looked at exactly this gap. In two of them people identified an old friend they wanted to reconnect with, thought would welcome hearing from them and knew how to contact. They had time, there and then, to write a message. Only about a third reported sending one.[4] An eagerness to reconnect and a thumb willing to press Send appear to be sold separately.

The result describes the person holding the phone. It can’t say what Mal would do on the other end. And people lose touch for better reasons than a full calendar. One friend kept calling from a bar for a ride he couldn’t remember the next day. One spent twenty years making the other feel small. Sometimes the person you miss is dead and you still know the number by heart. Just reach out is cheap advice from people who’ve never met your friends.

None of that happened between Dan and Mal. What happened was less dramatic and harder to explain. The last time they spoke, Mal was telling him about a training course he might take. Dan was putting up shelf brackets and got the drywall anchor into the hole at the exact moment his friend was saying why he wanted a change. Dan made the satisfied little grunt of a man who’s gotten a drywall anchor into a hole. Mal said he’d better let him get back to it. If only somebody had smashed a window. They’d have had a recognizable occasion to come back and put things right.

So here’s what Dan’s afraid of. He’s afraid the friendship died two years ago, on a call he grunted through, and nobody held a funeral. Nobody announced anything. Nobody put the chairs out. Twice a year a cartoon beer visits the grave. If he calls, he finds out.

“I really miss him,” Dan says. He’s surprised how easily it comes out with his head in the school’s lost-and-found closet. The closet holds seventeen left gloves and one retainer. He decides a parent can take some responsibility for that without a personal appeal.

Sam doesn’t say anything for a second. All afternoon he’s been hoping his brother would say what he’s actually feeling. Now Dan has, and Sam would like a minor edit. He’d hoped the person Dan really missed might be him.

Six days ago Sam asked his brother a question because he wanted the answer. Now he has one and it hurts. We like the idea of being someone another person can tell absolutely anything. We tend to picture the absolutely anything as a difficult truth that reflects well on our listening skills. A difficult truth in which we’re the second choice for Thursday night takes longer to admire.

“Would you go now?” Sam asks.

“To Mal’s? It’s seventy miles.”

“To the pool hall. I’ve got an hour.”

Dan shuts the closet. “You hated pool.”

“I’d forgotten. Thanks.”

A reasonably terrible game

The woman behind the counter at the pool hall has a paperback open, face down, over a tray of balls. Dan says he hasn’t been in for years. She says all right. He’d expected the building, at least, to have noticed he was gone. She asks whether they want the table for half an hour or an hour. He has to admit it’s a sensible question.

The felt has been replaced. An ATM stands where the cigarette machine used to be. The little shelf beneath the mirror has survived, exactly wide enough to set your beer on and then back into. Dan finds it with his hip. For about half a second he fully expects to hear Mal say careful of that. It’s the speed of the expectation that gets him. He puts his beer somewhere else.

Aristotle, winding up his discussion of friendship, pays attention to what friends actually do with their hours together. Some drink together, some play dice, some train or hunt, some do philosophy. People want company in the activities that make up a life they like living.[5] I appreciate philosophy’s place on a list that also offers a drink. It gives everybody at the table somewhere to go when the philosopher gets his notebook out.

The activity matters more than you’d think if you take pool for a pretext to get two men into a room to discuss their feelings. Mal once took a shot so confidently that he walked around to collect the ball from a pocket it had no intention of visiting. Close friends get this and no networking lunch can supply it. One of you is magnificently wrong about a small thing in full view of somebody who’ll enjoy it for years. Who else can make Dan laugh by pointing at six inches of wood?

You pick up a small language by being there when things happen. Point at the shelf. Tip an imaginary beer. Nobody else knows what’s funny. A full account of himself is the last thing Dan wants at the end of a bad week. He’s spent the week giving full accounts, with estimates, to people whose cars are making a noise. He’d like somebody to understand careful of that.

A language like that takes years, and it takes Thursdays. Move one man seventy miles, give the other two kids and a service department that opens at seven, and two people can like each other sincerely without ever both being free on the same night. The leaflets about making friends are quiet on the subject of who watches your kids.

Sam has been trying to sink a stripe for some time. He wants one decent shot before the hour is up. Dan starts to correct the angle of his elbow. It’s an automatic gesture, done with the same wrist action he uses to stop a child from putting a finger in an outlet.

“Will you let me miss the damn thing?”

Dan puts his hand over his own mouth. Sam misses. The cue ball travels the length of the table and comes back almost to where it started, having interfered as little as possible with the course of nature.

His brother has the decency to wait for Sam to start laughing. Sam sees the tips of his ears going red with the effort and starts a little sooner than he might have.

“Do the voice,” Dan says.

“What voice?”

“The man who interviews the sprout.”

So Sam leans over the rail and asks the cue ball how it feels to be back home after such an uneventful trip. In the library hush of a half-empty pool hall, a small middle-aged sound escapes from somewhere in Dan’s chest. The woman behind the counter turns a page of her book.

What a person can reasonably know

They get their coats. Sam has chalk on his cuff and a streak of it under his chin. Dan sees it and, instead of rubbing it off for him, points to the place on his own face. He’s done a fair amount of work today on the question of what to do with his hands.

The strange thing is that he still misses Mal. He’s had a good hour with somebody he loves, and part of that hour has made him miss his friend more. If company worked like central heating, this would be a fault in the system. But people know things about you because of where they were standing in 1998. Sam can learn the story of the shelf and the beer. Mal can remember the noise the glass made.

Sam, in turn, has something Mal could never give Dan. He saw their mother through the glass of the hall door. For years he misunderstood what was happening on the other side. That’s how close you can be. In one family, a few feet of linoleum from the same woman, two boys took home different Thanksgivings. Neither one got all of it. Their mom was entitled to part of it herself.

Outside the pool hall, Dan takes out his phone. “What do I say to him?”

“The course,” says Sam. “Did he ever take it?”

Dan had already forgotten telling Sam about the drywall anchor in the school closet. His brother had been listening back there among the gloves.

He sends Mal a voice message. He tells him where he is. A short silence follows while he tries to turn the next part into something elegant. He gives up. “I was drilling a damn hole the last time you tried to tell me about that course. Did you ever do it? I’d like to know.” He nearly finishes by telling Mal to get back to him whenever, if he feels like it, no worries either way. Three successive ways of holding the door open for his friend to leave. He presses Send.

No little burst of music comes out of the phone. Dan feels better for a moment, then a lot worse. He’s sent a message that can be heard without him there to explain it. This is one of the disadvantages of successfully making yourself heard. For two years the friendship has been neither alive nor dead, which was restful. Now somebody can check for a pulse.

On the way back Sam admits he’d been hoping Dan missed spending time with him too. It costs him something to put that too at the end. It lets another person stay in the sentence.

“I do,” Dan says. “You keep wanting to have us all over. I thought maybe you didn’t much enjoy just me.”

“Oh, for Christ’s sake,” says Sam, affectionately, to both of them.

They part at the crosswalk. Sam gets home to find Jo on the couch. She’s put the cushions on the floor and stretched out the whole length of her body. He has a great deal he wants to tell her. He makes her a cup of coffee instead. It gives her a chance to say which kind of evening she’s still having.

Dan’s phone lights up the next morning. Mal has sent forty-seven seconds of audio. There’s wind in the microphone. He took the course. Got all the way through the training and did the job for six months before he found the part of it he hated. Can Dan talk later? Also, is that bastard shelf still up? Dan plays it back. Even after two years Mal retains a keen interest in Dan’s prospects of injuring himself.

By nine that night the two of them are on the phone. The girls are in bed. Dan starts to sit down on the arm of the couch, then moves the pile of clean towels and makes room for himself on the seat. Mal is trying to tell him about those six months. It takes a few tries to explain why he left the barbershop where he first got a chair. Dan pictures Mal with a straight razor and a customer sneezing at a critical moment. He puts a hand over his mouth.

It was the bottles on the shelf behind him. The owner wanted every customer to leave with something extra. One Saturday Mal sold forty bucks’ worth of thickening shampoo to his own dad.

“Was he losing his hair?”

“You met my dad. Where would he have put it?”

Dan laughs. Mal does too, then goes quiet. “I think he just wanted me to get ahead,” he says. “I knew that when I took his money.”

On the other end Dan can hear a teaspoon going around in a cup. Usually this is where he comes in with something useful. Tonight he lets his friend finish stirring.

After a while, Mal goes on.
