Chapter 23You’re Here. Now What?

Your uncle Ben, the one who keeps screws in a chipped blue mixing bowl, has set the table for three. At present it seats one and a half.

One plate is on the fixed part of the table, another on top of the microwave and a third propped against the coffee maker. The hinged leaf hangs at a quiet angle, as if observing a moment of silence for the previous owner. Ben is on his knees underneath it with a flashlight in his mouth. The first thing he says to you today sounds approximately like a request for a bishop.

“Phillips,” he says, taking the flashlight out. “The cross one. In the drawer. Did you bring the lemon?”

You hold it up.

“Put it anywhere it won’t roll.”

You put it on the leaf, which is presently being held up by your other hand. It rolls. Downhill is Ben.

Last time we saw Ben, the two of you were on the phone about that bowl, which you’d been sure was yellow. He had the unfair advantage of being able to walk over and look. When he asked if you had a minute, you sat down. Here’s some of what happened because you stayed on the line.

He told you he wanted to move. The old place had gotten too expensive to heat. He’d given up the car two winters ago, after an exchange of paint with a mailbox that he still describes as mutual, and most of the friends he had left lived two bus rides away. He’d seen an apartment over a store. Was he nuts? You knew squat about apartments over stores, said so, and asked which store. Stationery, it turned out. The loading dock opened early. You went over with him on a Wednesday morning and stood in the back bedroom at seven-fifteen listening to a man on the sidewalk drop a box of envelopes. Most of the weight in a box of envelopes, you learned, is in the swearing.

He took the apartment. The move required a rented van, three change-of-address forms and the discovery that the sandwich maker had not, in fact, been a wise purchase in 1992. Today you’ve shown up after work intending to give the table twenty minutes, drink one cup of coffee and catch the early bus home. Your car is in the shop, where a man is billing you by the hour to agree that it makes the noise. On the way here you took the precaution of not mentioning the twenty minutes to Ben.

“Someone coming to dinner?” you ask.

“Well, you are.”

“I’m the maintenance department.”

“Don’t they feed you in maintenance?”

A delighted part of you immediately starts assembling the account of this evening you’ll give later. The old guy settling in. You, with your screwdriver, bringing a little human warmth to a bare kitchen. If you’d like to know what that sounds like to somebody under the table, Ben asks you to stop putting your weight on his thumb.

The third plate is for the woman upstairs. Her name’s Rosa. She has a big laugh, works nights and owns a small hairy dog that Ben describes as looking like something pulled out of a hotel shower drain. She lent him a can opener on his first night. He found out afterward that she’d gone back up and opened her own beans with a second opener so bad she’d had to punch holes all the way around the can and join them up with language. He’s invited her down for pancakes. His mother’s, the thin kind, rolled up with lemon and sugar. Ben was a grown man before he learned that other families used syrup, and he has never forgiven them. Rosa said she’d see how the afternoon went.

“Do you have her number?” you ask. “We could find out if she’s coming.”

“I know how to find out if she’s coming. If she comes, she knocks.”

His distressing lack of personal growth makes for an agreeable start to dinner.

What you can see from far away

By the winter of 1990 Voyager 1 had finished with the planets and was on its way out. Carl Sagan, a member of the imaging team, had been asking for one more picture for years. Turn the cameras around and photograph home. From out there Earth sits within a few degrees of the Sun, and people worried that pointing the camera that close to it could ruin the camera. On February 14 the spacecraft took the pictures anyway, from roughly six billion kilometers (about 3.7 billion miles) out. Earth came through at 0.12 of a pixel, about an eighth. In the published image it sits in what looks like a shaft of light. The shaft is sunlight scattered inside the camera. Nobody up there trained a follow spot on the lead actor. Thirty-four minutes after the Earth frames were taken, Voyager’s cameras were switched off for good.[1], [2]

Then the pictures waited for time on the big antennas back home. Six billion kilometers at the speed of light, about 300,000 kilometers a second, is a trip of some 20,000 seconds, five and a half hours. The last of the data arrived on May 1, after four passes.[1] Home waited seventy-six days for its portrait.

People use that picture to feel small, and then to feel better about what they said to their manager about her new glasses. The step from small to unimportant is where it gets fishy. Move far enough from anything and it takes up less of your view. Try it with a crying child and a pair of binoculars held the wrong way around, and see how much you learn about whether to pick the kid up. If angular size settled the value of a life, the most important person in most galaxies would be the nearest optometrist.

You’ve leaned on the long view yourself, on days when the only argument you could find for carrying on was that from far enough away your trouble wouldn’t show. It works until about dinnertime. The long view is the right distance for the orbit of a planet. Whether you’re pinning your uncle’s thumb under a piece of wood has to be studied from closer in, and he’s told you twice.

Your instinct with the table is to hold the leaf level and tighten everything. Ben wants to fold it down again. He needs to see where the little wooden support goes when it swings out from underneath. It’s a narrow gate on a hinge, scarcely more complicated than the elbow of the person fixing it. The screws that held one side have worked loose. One is on the floor, one is in the blue bowl, and the last is in a location known only to the previous owner’s dog, who may by now have taken the information to the grave.

“Why’d you buy one that needs fixing?”

“I wanted the one that folds.”

“Plenty of tables fold.”

“So does this. At the moment it’s what it does best.”

The bowl catches the light from the store sign outside. It’s blue, all right. For a memory with so much detail in it, the bowl that lived in your head turns out to be a poor likeness. This one is broader, heavier at the base. It has a little oval scar where somebody left it on a hot burner. You’d imagined that seeing it again would fill in the past. What you learn is that Ben wraps tape around the shank of a spare screwdriver so it doesn’t rattle among the screws. The bowl has had an interesting life during the years when nobody was assigning it literary significance.

You could ask if you might have it one day, for its sentimental value. No phrasing of that question leaves his funeral out of it. You could also let an old man keep using a bowl while everything in his house remains, through the technicality of his being alive, his. He hands you the screwdriver. You put the screw from the floor back in its hole and see why the support wobbles. It turns and turns. The screw has nothing solid to bite into.

An experiment that requires an invitation

Ben’s been practicing the pancakes. Under favorable conditions he can turn one over in the air. He’d like somebody to see this. Ever since he moved he’s had vague plans to have people over. The great advantage of a plan to have people over is that you can keep it going for months without anybody getting crumbs in the butter. The defect shows up around seven in the evening, when you’d like to hear somebody else laugh. At his age the address book loses more names in a year than it gains, and he isn’t the one crossing them out.

He started with the diner across the street. A man can walk in, sit down, have company of a sort and walk out again without asking any one person to risk an evening on him. He knows what the woman behind the counter calls a large coffee, which is a bucket. About once a week he orders a bucket, somebody fills a big cup, and the world contains one small agreement that works.

Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn ran a field experiment on this kind of exchange. Customers at a coffee shop were asked either to have a real moment with the barista, a smile and a few words, or to get through the transaction as efficiently as they could. The ones who talked reported feeling better afterward and felt more of a sense of belonging. The study asked the customers how they felt. It didn’t ask the barista.[3]

Ben doesn’t know her last name. He can go somewhere else tomorrow, and she has to be there by seven. None of that makes the warmth fake. It’d be a bleak account of human beings if two people couldn’t enjoy each other until somebody stopped the payroll.

After the diner closes he wants a different kind of company as well, the kind you can ask for a second opinion on a pancake in a building where nobody’s paid to say it looks great. To get it he has to issue an invitation, accept the possibility of no, and watch the clock while somebody else decides what she feels like doing. One of the inconveniences of wanting another person’s company is their habit of being a person while you want it.

How much did Ben need to know before he asked? Whether the two of them might like to eat together now and then couldn’t be known in advance, because the occasion on which they’d find out hadn’t happened yet. He could have watched her walk the dog for two years without learning a tenth of what she’d tell him over one mediocre pancake.

William James has a passage about this in The Will to Believe. In some situations a willingness to trust first, or to make the first move, helps bring about the very relationship we wanted proof of. If two people each insist on proof of the other’s friendliness before showing any of their own, the proof never gets a chance to exist. James’s wider argument is about choices that are live, consequential and forced on us, including cases where waiting amounts to picking a side. Somebody has to be first to move their groceries off the spare chair.[4]

People use much the same language to explain why an asteroid will miss the planet if we all really commit to the idea. An asteroid has no idea how you feel. A person can notice you’ve set a place, and that can enter her deliberations without deciding them.

Ben has left the third plate against the coffee maker. It’s one of three patterns, bought separately at a thrift store. He didn’t want to spend ten dollars on a set of six because, in his view, the social situation is well in hand long before you reach six people.

The things you thought would be settled by now

By the end of a book like this you might have hoped to be a person who rarely rehearses an old conversation while buying fruit. Somebody with a disciplined relationship with death, an untroubled affection for the truth and the ability to see a photograph of someone else’s kitchen without trying to work out what they earn.

Disappointing news from the apartment above the stationery store. You’ve just spent three minutes admiring the building across the street, which has nicer windows than yours. A resident has opened one, and from the angle of her head you’ve begun to estimate the height of her ceilings. A person can learn a lot about the follies of comparison, agree with all of it, and spend a Tuesday afternoon trying to establish whether a woman they’ve never met has crown molding.

The change, if you got one, comes half a minute later. You notice what you’re doing before you’ve looked up listings in her building. Or you notice that what you envy is a door you could close while your roommate is still up, which is a conversation to have at home about furniture. The mind you use for this still comes attached to the mammal at the window.

You’re badly placed to grade the whole enterprise from inside one head anyway. Somewhere you’re the villain in a dinner story told by somebody from a wedding you don’t remember attending. So you have to ask, which is inconvenient, because the people who know are distracted and occasionally cruel.

Under the sink Ben finds a stick of wood left over from the table’s packing. A sliver of it, tapped into the stripped hole, gives the thread something to grip. Among the screws in the bowl is a newer, longer one that can get purchase in the sound wood beyond the stripped part. You check the thickness so it won’t come up through the tabletop and achieve permanent purchase in a future diner. It’s fine. He holds the gate in position and you drive the screw.

The gate swings out, stops where it should and takes the leaf. You put the lemon down again. It stays. It’d be nice if the great questions submitted to an equally decisive piece of fruit. You can also tell when you’ve done enough. Keep tightening now and all you’ll accomplish is stripping the hole again.

“Put the coffee on,” Ben says.

You look at your phone. Early bus in twenty minutes, the next one forty minutes after that. Nothing terrible is waiting if you take the later one. A load of laundry you’d like to get dry and a call you owe your friend, the one who moved three hundred miles for a job and has a presentation tomorrow she wants to talk through. She used to knock on your door. Now the two of you book each other. You could call her from the later bus and live with damp sleeves. The alternative to one touching scene with an uncle is usually a pile of ordinary good things. They still count.

On other days you should catch the early bus. Being mortal together doesn’t give the last person to reach for your sleeve a claim over everybody else you love. Ben has said he’d like you to stay. He hasn’t said you owe it.

You text your friend. Would the later bus work? It would. She sends a photograph of the jacket she’s planning to wear. You return an opinion on the jacket, which you can already tell she’s decided to disregard. Good. She sends a heart. You put the phone face down.

Batter enough for three

Ben doesn’t use the bowl. A bowl with screws in it strikes him as a ludicrous thing to use on food. You’d pictured flour going in there, as it did when you were small. The man whose arm is still available to put around you reaches into the fridge and comes back with the batter, in a pink plastic measuring cup with half the measurements washed off.

He’s made enough for three. Rosa hasn’t knocked. He asks whether you think it was dumb to invite her. You could tell him she’s bound to turn up, on the strength of one lemon and no knowledge of Rosa. You say, truthfully, that you’re glad you came. He grunts in a way that means he’s pleased and would be grateful if the two of you could cut the sincerity in the kitchen by about half.

“You’re supposed to rest the batter,” he says.

“Does it know that?”

“Mine’s been resting since four-fifteen. It’s had a better afternoon than I have.”

A knock. Rosa is on the landing in her coat. They’ve moved her shift up. She came down to say thanks for asking and to tell him he can keep the can opener, she’s bought a new one. The dog is going to her sister’s. From the bag under her arm comes a small disgruntled sound fully consistent with Ben’s description.

Ben asks if she wants a pancake to take with her. She says no, thank you. Her ride’s waiting. Some other time, she says. She doesn’t give a date. He doesn’t make her find one on the landing.

It would be easy to put an agreement in her mouth. Next Thursday, say, she’ll come down after work and bring her sister. Tonight Rosa is entitled to go to work. Ben is entitled to the small disappointment of a man who bought sugar and washed a third plate.

He stands holding that plate for a moment after she goes. He’s easy to love just then and hard to help. Nothing you know will turn the woman around on the stairs. You could explain to him about the fragile beauty of human existence. He has a good grip on the china and might, in the circumstances, be accurate with it.

“Hungry?” he says.

“Very.”

He sets the plate down on the leaf. It sits level. The evening was supposed to settle some larger matters. What it settled is one end of a table. Yesterday nobody could set a plate there. If someone asks what justified the trip, that’s a whole answer, and one you can put a hot dinner on.

Ben sets the pan on the burner.

While the pan heats

The pan has to be hotter than you’d think, Ben says. While it gets there I’m going to leave the two of you at the stove and walk across town. It’s been a few weeks, and I said at the start that I wouldn’t rescue anybody.

Walt died in February. He was in his chair, with the remote button for the camera in his hand. He hadn’t pressed it. Fran checked. She has two minutes and eleven seconds of finished film. She has the footage for a sequel nobody is going to cut, with Walt playing the alien himself and a take of his own getting-up that he kept on purpose. And she has the twelve seconds she once begged six people and a fifteen-year-old to delete. Two strangers’ copies are still up, one of them retitled He never gave up, and the app has rejected her reports. Now she watches them most nights and lets the app count her. In the take he kept, he’s directing. In the stolen one he’s her father on an ordinary day, before a grocery bagger had ever called him Chair guy. He never told her she could keep it. Most of the Super 8 cans still have no labels, and the last person who knew the names in them is gone.

What the world wanted from her next was a reason. The drugstore where she works kept texting her that his prescription was ready for pickup. To stop the refills, somebody has to open his profile and pick why from a list, and the list offers Therapy completed. Fran sat with the cursor on it twice. In the end Mara did it, on a morning when Fran wasn’t in. Mara is the pharmacist. She has buried a father of her own and knows which screen comes next. She covered Fran’s shifts the week after Walt’s funeral, and neither of them has mentioned that it once went the other way. The texts stopped. Fran noticed on the ninth day and minded more than she’d expected.

The lights are on at the community center. It’s a Tuesday in spring, and the one quarter of evenings bought with a vote of four to three is almost used up. The city’s notice about closing at five is still up. The recreation department has the numbers and hasn’t said what it makes of them. Bev keeps her evening hours on the calendar in pencil. Through the window Leon is emptying his trombone onto a floor she’ll mop at eight. Of the seven who voted, not one would bet much on having been right.

Ellen hasn’t found a job. She’s found work, which pays on a different schedule, about sixty days after the drawing goes out. The electric company prefers thirty. Its shutoff notice was for $120, and the museum’s check paid it and left her a hundred and fifty to eat on. Her car still needs an alternator, which would have cost more than the hundred and fifty even if she hadn’t eaten it. Her sister Tess does payroll and has explained what sixty days looks like from the other side of the desk. The review of Sam’s memo, the one asking that she be put on a retainer, has been moved to next quarter again. She’s still paid by the job. She keeps his copy in the glove box, with the estimate for the alternator. The drafting program that took Ellen’s hours is still drawing for the furniture company. It has never once been late.

Sam and Jo ran the figures again when the winter bills came in. The figures were worse. The breathing room they’d counted on is gone, Jo has picked up a fourth late shift, and they’re staying where they are, because moving costs first month, last month and a deposit, and the cheapest way to be short of money is to hold still.

Dan turned fifty. He and Mal have talked a handful of times and seen each other once. They’ve been trying to set a second date for two months. Both of them mean it and it keeps not happening, which is exactly how it stopped the first time.

June has been back to the bird blind four times, three of them with her father, who brings nothing to do. She still hasn’t seen an avocet. The whiteboard says somebody saw two on a Thursday morning, while she was in school.

You applied for the job in the browser tab. In February they said no in two polite lines, which ended eleven months of nobody turning you down. You were fine by dinner. Not that dinner. The one after.

Your sister came to Thanksgiving. She and your father got through it on the subject of traffic. She calls you some Sundays now, and she starts with hello.

That’s the count, and I don’t have a frame that makes it come out even. I’ve tried several in this book. The big ones are all true. The universe is very old, the brain guesses, the odds don’t know your name, and everybody in this town is going to die. Not one of those facts told Fran what to do about a text message, and not one will tell Ben whether to ask Rosa again.

So here’s the one habit, plainly. Ask the person, because nobody else knows. Then do the small thing that can actually be done. It’ll be smaller than you wanted, and it may not work. Mara’s took four seconds. Ben’s was a third plate, and tonight it didn’t.

Back above the stationery store, the pan is hot. Ben pours. The first pancake goes onto the third plate to keep warm, since that plate has nothing else to do this evening. The second loosens at the edges. He gives the pan a little shake and the pancake slides. The man doesn’t have much to go on. He knows how it felt on the practice runs. He can see you’ve got the plate. He has a loose estimate of the distance and an unreasonable confidence in your catching. Nothing in all those billions of miles of space has undertaken to put dinner where he wants it.

He tosses it. It comes down half on the plate, half over your hand, hot enough to make you snatch the hand back. Ben gets the edge with the spatula and folds the loose half over. A second of professional silence follows while the two of you consider whether you can claim it was meant to come out that shape.

“French,” he decides.

You cut the lemon and he passes the sugar, no discussion. When you squeeze, you remember to put your other hand underneath to catch the seeds. You learned that somewhere. Across the table Ben is grinning, his mouth already full.
