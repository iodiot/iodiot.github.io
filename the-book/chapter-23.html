Chapter 23You're Here. Now What?

Your uncle Ben has laid the table for three. At present it seats one and a half.

There’s a plate on the fixed part, another on top of the washing machine and a third propped up against the kettle. The hinged leaf of the table hangs at a quiet angle, as if observing a minute’s silence for the previous owner. Ben is on his knees underneath it with a torch in his mouth. The first thing he says to you in his new flat sounds approximately like a request for a bishop.

“Phillips,” he says, taking the torch out. “Crosshead. In the drawer. Did you bring the lemon?”

You hold it up.

“Put it anywhere it won’t roll.”

You put it on the leaf, which is presently being held up by your other hand. It rolls. Some species might be prepared to go through an entire afternoon without testing gravity on an elderly relative’s skull. Yours has achieved space flight and appears keen to investigate further.

Last time we saw Ben, the two of you were on the phone discussing a mixing bowl. You were quite certain it had been yellow. He had the unfair advantage of being able to walk over and look at the thing. Blue, he said. Chip by the rim. He kept screws in it. When he asked if you had a minute, you sat down. This is a little of what happened because you stayed on the line.

He told you he wanted to move. The old place had got too expensive to heat and most of his friends lived two bus rides away. He’d seen a flat over a shop. Was he daft? You told him one fact about yourself, which was that you knew absolutely bugger all about flats over shops. Then you asked which shop. A stationer’s, it turned out. The loading bay opened early. You went round with him on a Wednesday morning and stood in the back bedroom at seven fifteen listening to a man on the pavement drop a box of envelopes. Most of the weight in a box of envelopes, you learnt, is in the swearing.

He took the flat. There were other things on that call, but that was the one that required a small van, three changes of address and the discovery that the apparatus for making toasted sandwiches had not, in fact, been a wise purchase in 1992. You’ve been over twice since. Today you’ve turned up after work intending to give the table twenty minutes, have a quick cup of tea and get the early bus home. On the way here you took the precaution of not mentioning any of this to Ben.

“Someone coming to dinner?” you ask.

“Well, you are.”

“I’m the maintenance department.”

“Do they not feed you in maintenance?”

There is a delighted bit of you that immediately starts assembling the account of this evening you’ll give later. The old boy settling in. You, with your screwdriver, bringing a little human warmth to a bare kitchen. If you’d like to know what that sounds like to somebody under the table, Ben asks you to stop putting your weight on his thumb.

The third plate is for the woman in the flat upstairs. Her name’s Rosa. She has a big laugh, keeps hours unlike anybody else in the building and owns a small hairy dog which Ben describes as looking like the material extracted from a hotel plughole. She lent him a tin opener on his first night. He found out afterwards that she’d gone back up and opened her own beans with a different opener so bad she’d had to punch holes all the way round the tin and join them up with language. He’s invited her down for pancakes. She said she’d see how the afternoon went.

“Have you got her number?” you ask. “We could find out if she’s coming.”

“I know how to find out if she’s coming. If she comes, she knocks.”

A certain distressing lack of personal growth on his part makes for a very agreeable beginning to dinner.

What you can see from far away

On 14 February 1990 Voyager 1 turned its cameras towards the planets and, among the photographs it took, sent us a view of Earth. The spacecraft was roughly six billion kilometres from the Sun. At that distance Earth occupied less than a single pixel across. In the published image it sits in what looks like a shaft of light. The shaft comes from sunlight scattered in the camera optics. Nobody up there trained a little follow spot on the lead actor. [1]

Take a moment to consider what went into that speck. People had to design a device, get it off the ground, send it past astonishingly distant places and choose, with the chance of doing a few last things with the cameras, to look back. In a different arrangement of priorities we would have the same planet, the same orbit, the same collection of intelligent mammals trying to decide what to have for dinner and no photograph. The image is a physical record of light and a historical record of somebody thinking a particular look was worth taking. Both descriptions fit inside the same frame.

The image can make one person feel relieved about an embarrassing remark at work. Look how small we are. Hardly anybody out there has a view on what you said to your manager about her new glasses. It can make the next person almost unable to bear the thought of losing even one person from a place that tiny. Same light, same camera, quite different effects on two nervous systems standing in front of it.

There’s something wonderfully dodgy about the familiar inference from small to unimportant. Move far enough from any object and it occupies less of your view. Do this with a crying child while holding binoculars the wrong way round and see how much useful information you get on whether to pick them up. If you could establish the value of a life by angular size, the most important individual in most galaxies would be the nearest optician.

This matters because there are days when the only argument you can find for carrying on is a vague idea that, fed into a sufficiently enormous frame of reference, your difficulty would cease to matter. Sometimes the trick works. On other days you still have to get through an empty evening after you put the photograph down. We are allowed to work at the distance at which the information we need can actually be obtained. To investigate the orbit of a planet, get a long view. To find out whether you are holding your uncle’s thumb under a piece of wood, a somewhat more local investigation has already been commissioned.

Your instinct with the table is to hold the leaf level and tighten everything. Ben wants to fold it down again. He needs to see where the little wooden support ought to go when it swings out from underneath. This is a surprisingly lovely mechanism, a narrow gate on a hinge, scarcely more complicated than the elbow on the person trying to mend it. The screws that used to hold one side have worked loose. One is on the floor, one is in the blue bowl, and the last is in a location known only to the dog of a previous owner who may by now have taken the information to the grave.

“Why’d you buy one that needs fixing?”

“I wanted the one that folds.”

“Plenty of tables fold.”

“So does this. At the moment it’s what it does best.”

The bowl catches the light from the shop sign outside. It’s blue all right. It’s less like the dish that lived in your head than the detail of your memory had any right to be. Broader, heavier at the base. There’s a little oval mark where somebody left it on a hot ring. You had imagined that seeing the object again would fill in the missing parts of the past. Mainly what you learn is that Ben puts tape round the shank of a spare screwdriver so it doesn’t rattle quite so much among the screws. The bowl has had an interesting life during the years when nobody was assigning it literary significance.

You could ask if you might have it one day, for its sentimental value. You could also allow an old man to keep using a dish while every item in his house remains, through the considerable advantage of his being alive, his actual fucking stuff. He hands you the screwdriver. You take the first screw out and see why the support wobbles. The screw has nothing solid to bite into.

An experiment that requires an invitation

Ben’s been practising the pancakes. He can do the batter. He can also, under favourable conditions, turn one over in the air. He would quite like to show someone this. Ever since he moved he has had vague plans for having people round. The great advantage of a plan to have people round is that you can sustain it for months without anyone getting crumbs in the butter. The defect in the arrangement becomes apparent around seven in the evening when you would actually like to hear somebody else laughing.

He started by eating in the café over the road. You can walk in, sit down, have company of a sort and walk out again without ever having had to ask an individual to risk their evening on you. He likes the café. He knows what the woman behind the counter calls a large tea, which is a bucket. About once a week he orders a bucket and somebody fills a big cup and the world contains an entirely satisfactory minor agreement.

In a field experiment, Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn had customers in a coffee shop either have a brief social interaction with the barista or conduct the transaction as efficiently as they could. People asked to interact reported more positive feeling afterwards and a greater sense of belonging. A small change in an ordinary exchange affected the customers’ experience of that exchange. The experiment doesn’t establish that the barista wants to hear about your separation while fifteen people wait for a flat white. The customer’s warm feeling is evidence about the customer. [2]

Suppose Ben gets just such a lift from his café. What does it prove? That he can be pleased by a person who knows his order and has a moment to spare. That’s already a good deal. Does it establish a close friendship? He doesn’t know her surname. The terms of the acquaintance include the fact that he can go somewhere else tomorrow and she has to be there by seven. Does it follow that the warmth is fake? It would be a bleak account of human beings if two people were incapable of enjoying one another until somebody stopped the payroll. The terms are part of what the encounter means. So is the smile.

Alone in the flat after the café has shut, he has found he’d like a different kind of company as well. The kind you can ask for a second opinion about the fate of a pancake, in a building where nobody is being paid to say it looks excellent. To get that kind he’ll have to issue an invitation, accept the possibility of no, put a bit of milk in the batter and watch the clock move while somebody else decides what she feels like doing. One of the inconveniences of wanting another person’s company is their persistent habit of being a person while you want it.

There’s a serious question inside this fairly humble recipe. How much evidence should Ben require that an invitation will succeed before he issues it? If his aim is to establish whether Rosa is free tonight, he could obtain quite useful evidence by going upstairs and asking. If he wants to know whether the pair of them might like to eat here regularly, the evidence is harder to obtain in advance. The occasion in which their preferences could form hasn’t happened yet. He could watch her walk the dog for the next two years without finding out a tenth of what she might tell him over a single indifferent pancake.

William James, in The Will to Believe, describes situations in which a willingness to trust or make the first move helps bring about the very relationship we want evidence for. If each of two people insists on conclusive proof of the other’s friendliness before displaying any of their own, the evidence may never get a chance to exist. James’s wider argument concerns choices that are live possibilities, consequential, and in some way forced on us, including cases where waiting amounts to choosing a side. You needn’t accept every application he makes of it to recognise the ordinary social difficulty. Somebody has to be first to shift their shopping off the spare chair. [3]

People have used surprisingly similar language to explain why an asteroid will miss the planet if we all really commit to the idea. It may be worth identifying the difference before the observatories close for lack of encouragement. An asteroid has no idea how you feel. A person can notice you’ve set a place. This new fact may enter their deliberations. It cannot decide those deliberations for them. An invitation creates the possibility of a shared evening. It doesn’t make the evening owed.

Ben has left the plate against the kettle. You pick it up and look on the back. It’s one of three different patterns, acquired separately from a charity shop. He didn’t want to spend ten pounds on a set of six because, in his view, the social situation can be considered well in hand before you’ve reached six people. There were two of the willow pattern, but one had a chip in roughly the place a person’s lip would go if they drank gravy off it. He didn’t want to foreclose on anybody’s options.

You’d have liked this job to have a clean end. Tighten the screw, hold the leaf, stack the plates. Done. You could go home feeling useful and go on thinking of yourself as exactly the person you were on arrival. You realise now that you’ve spent a considerable part of the last half-hour trying to establish whether Ben has enough social support in the area. This is a subject on which you are keeping yourself marvellously well informed for somebody currently deciding whether to stay for tea.

The things you thought would be settled by now

By the time you got to the end of a book like this, you might reasonably have hoped to become a kind of person who hardly ever rehearses an old conversation while buying fruit. It would be nice to have developed a disciplined relationship with death, a deep, untroubled affection for the truth and the ability to see a photograph of someone else’s kitchen without trying to work out what they earn.

There is some disappointing news from the flat above the stationer’s. You have just spent three minutes admiring a building opposite which appears to have nicer windows than yours. On seeing one of the residents open a window you have begun, from the angle at which her head appears, to estimate the height of her ceilings. You can learn quite a lot about the follies of comparison, deeply agree with all of it, then spend Tuesday afternoon trying to establish whether a woman you’ve never met has cornicing.

The useful change might come half a minute later. You notice what you’re up to in time to avoid spending the next half-hour searching for pictures of her flat. Or you notice you’ve been envious of a particular practical thing, a room where it might be possible to close the door and sleep while the rest of the household is up. Perhaps there’s a conversation to have at home about moving the furniture. Once in a while the unlovely private motive leads you to some perfectly serviceable public information. The mind you get to use for the purpose still comes attached to the mammal looking through the window.

A person who has a difficult temper can apologise after an hour instead of insisting on three days of silence and leaving the others to guess where the dishes go. An anxious person can still feel the rush of alarm when a phone goes unanswered and nevertheless let the individual having a shower finish rinsing their bloody hair. Small differences in what you do with the character you have may amount to a fairly large difference in what it’s like to live in the house with you. You may be quite badly placed to notice. You still feel anxious. Ask the man who got to come out of the shower fully rinsed.

This is one reason to be cautious about grading an entire life from the inside of a single head. You have imperfect access to the consequences. People can owe you things they barely remember getting from you. You can also be the central villain in a dinner story told by a woman whose face you would struggle to identify in good light. At least I meant well may be an entirely true fact with a rather poor view of what happened next. And nothing I do makes any difference is an impressive claim to make on the basis of being able to see about fifteen feet in any direction and hearing mainly from the people with your current number.

It follows that we need other people, which is deeply inconvenient because they offer no guarantee of a fair assessment either. Some are distracted, some are cruel, some know you accurately in the particular cramped place you occupy in their lives. The person at the till can tell whether you spoke to them civilly. They haven’t conducted an audit of your worth. Your partner may love you with splendid generosity and have a somewhat optimistic view of whether you can install a dishwasher. Correcting the extravagances of the story in your own head needn’t entail outsourcing the whole enterprise to whoever happens to be nearest the microphone.

Under the sink Ben finds a little stick of wood. It’s part of the packing from the table. In among the screws there’s a new, longer one that can get purchase in the sound wood beyond the stripped bit. You check the thickness so it won’t come through the tabletop and achieve permanent purchase in a future diner. There’s enough room. He holds the gate in the right position and you put the screw in.

The gate swings out, stops where it ought to and supports the leaf. You put the lemon down again. It stays. It would be lovely if all the great questions submitted to an equally decisive piece of fruit. You can see the instant you have done enough work on this particular one. Go on tightening at this point and the main thing left to accomplish is stripping the hole again.

“Put the kettle on,” Ben says.

You have a look at your phone. Early bus in twenty minutes, next one forty minutes after that. Nothing especially dreadful awaits if you catch the later one. There is a load of washing you would like to get dry, a bit of work you intended to finish and a message from a friend who wants to tell you about an interview tomorrow. You could text her to arrange a call on the bus. You could put the wash on when you get in and accept that a few damp sleeves may remain in your near future. Quite often the alternative to a particular good thing is a set of rather ordinary good things. It would be dishonest to insist they count for nothing because a touching scene with an uncle is currently being staged somewhere else.

There are also days when you ought to catch the early bus. A child is waiting. You have to unlock a shop in the morning at six. Or you genuinely want to be alone, and coming to help with the table was the part of the day you freely had to give. We’ve built ourselves some spectacularly unpleasant moral arrangements out of the idea that whoever asks last and looks loneliest wins the evening. Being mortal together doesn’t grant the last person to reach for your sleeve a prior claim over every other person you love. Ben has been quite clear he’d like you to stay. He hasn’t got you down on a compulsory feeding order.

You text your friend. Yes, on the later bus would be fine. She’ll have got the baby down by then. She replies with a photograph of the jacket she’s planning to wear. You return an opinion about the jacket, which you can already tell she has decided to disregard. Good. She sends a heart. You put your phone face down.

Batter enough for three

The planet has had time to turn a little while you were getting the table up. The light from the stationer’s sign is bright on one side of the bowl. Ben wants to mix the batter in a jug. A bowl with screws in it strikes him as a perfectly ludicrous thing to employ in the preparation of food. You had pictured flour going in there, as it did when you were little. The man whose arm is still available to put round you reaches into the cupboard and comes back with something pink and plastic with half the measurements washed off.

He’s made enough for three. Rosa hasn’t answered. He asks whether you think it was a daft idea to invite her. There are several places you could take the question. You can tell him she’s bound to turn up, a fact entirely unavailable to a person in possession of one lemon and no particular knowledge of Rosa. You can tell him she’d better appreciate what a lovely neighbour he is, which would make her responsible for some feelings she had no part in bringing down the stairs. You can say, truthfully, that you’re glad you came. He grunts in a way that means he’s pleased and would be grateful if the two of you could decrease the sincerity in the kitchen by approximately half.

“You’re supposed to rest the batter,” he says.

“Does it know that?”

“Mine’s been resting since quarter past four. It’s had a better afternoon than I have.”

There’s a knock. Rosa is on the landing in a coat. She has a night shift after all. They’ve brought her start time forward. She wanted to come down and say thanks for asking and to tell him he can have the tin opener, she’s bought a new one. The dog is going to her sister’s. From the bag hanging under her arm there issues a small disgruntled sound fully consistent with Ben’s earlier account of the drains.

Ben asks if she wants a pancake to take with her. He can have one ready in a few minutes. She says no, thank you. She’s got a lift waiting. Some other time, she says. She doesn’t give a date. He doesn’t make her find one on the landing.

It would be easy to put a little agreement into her mouth. Next Thursday, say, she’ll come down after work and bring her sister. A novelist can rescue a man from almost any painful uncertainty by assigning an obliging line to a woman on the stairs. We often try something rather like this at home. We have a picture of the outcome which would make our conduct worthwhile, and the people we love find themselves cast in it long before anyone has offered them the courtesy of reading the script. Tonight Rosa is entitled to go to work. Ben is entitled to feel the small disappointment of a man who bought sugar and washed a third plate.

He stands holding that plate for a moment after she goes. He’s almost comically easy to love in that instant and quite difficult to help. There’s no information you can give him that turns the woman round on the stairs. You could explain about the fragile beauty of human existence. He has a good grip on the china and might, in the circumstances, be quite accurate with it.

“Hungry?” he says.

“Very.”

He puts the plate back on the table. It balances perfectly. You hadn’t thought to be pleased with it until this moment. With all the grander proceedings scheduled for this evening, you have nearly missed an actual piece of the world that the two of you have managed to alter. Yesterday you couldn’t set a plate on this end of the table. Now you can. If someone asks what justified the trip, that’s a whole answer already, and one you can put a hot dinner on.

I would like to leave the two of you here for a minute and ask about something nearer your end of the arrangement. While you’ve been reading, time has been passing wherever you actually are. It might be morning. You may be on a train. There might be someone in the next room who has asked for a cup of tea twice and is entertained by the spectacle of you reading a book about showing up for the people you love. You might be alone in a house where the other cup hasn’t been needed for years. There is a limit to what a person who has never stood in that room can tell you to do when you close the book. That limit is one of the premises on which the whole thing has been written.

Some of us would like a charge considerable enough to justify being here in the first place. The difficulty with that formulation is the sequence of events. You’re already here. The air has been moving in and out for ages while the committee considers whether to approve your lungs. You don’t need to win a cosmic tender before the woman in the next room could use a cup of tea. You might need to apologise, earn a living, have a lie-down or do six months of sustained work on a problem far larger than this kitchen. Nobody becomes unaccountable because the universe has declined to chair the hearing. Other people can still tell you very particular things about the afternoon they spent in your company.

You also don’t need to squeeze a practical takeaway out of every afternoon. There are blue bowls that would be perfectly happy to hold screws for the next fifty years without being made to signify anything. There are people you can love more accurately by letting them be absorbed in something gloriously irrelevant to your development. And sometimes the answer to the question of what you were doing with the one life you’re going to have is that you were standing by a stove trying to remember who last had the spatula. It took about eight seconds. They were available for purchase on no other planet.

Back in the flat, Ben has put one pancake on the spare plate to keep warm. The next one loosens at the edges. He gives the pan a little shake and the pancake slides. Behold the impressive piece of information the man has at his disposal. He knows what it felt like on the practice goes. He can see you’ve got the plate. He has a loose estimate of the distance and a quite unreasonable confidence in your catching. Nothing in all those billions of kilometres of space has undertaken to put breakfast where he wants it.

He tosses it. It comes down half on the plate, half over your hand, warm enough to make you snatch it back. Ben gets the edge with the spatula and folds the loose half over. There’s a second of perfect professional silence while the two of you consider whether you can claim it was meant to come out that shape.

“French,” he decides.

You cut the lemon. He holds out his half of the pancake. This time, when you squeeze, you remember to put your other hand underneath to catch the pips. You learnt that somewhere. Across the table Ben is grinning, his mouth already full.
