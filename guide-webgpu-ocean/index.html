An ocean built from wave spectra.

A deep-water ocean is simulated on the GPU: spectral waves evolve over time, inverse FFTs turn them into a surface, and three scales of detail combine into one moving sea.

01

From a wave spectrum to a moving surface

The ocean starts as a distribution of wave energy. Every frame, GPU compute evolves that field, transforms it into spatial displacement, and assembles textures for rendering.

h(k,t)Advance phase
2D IFFTReturn to space
AssembleDisplace + foam
ShadeReflect the sky

Make a seeded spectrum

Gaussian noise is combined with a JONSWAP or Phillips wave-energy spectrum. When a spectrum control changes, the GPU rebuilds the initial complex field h₀(k).

Advance every wave in time

Deep-water dispersion sets each wave’s angular frequency. The shader rotates the initial complex amplitudes forward and backward in phase while preserving the conjugate symmetry needed for a real surface.

Transform frequency into space

Four complex fields are packed so each inverse FFT produces two real fields. A row pass and a column pass apply an in-place radix-2 Stockham transform.

Build displacement and surface derivatives

The assembly pass applies horizontal choppiness, evaluates the displacement Jacobian, updates foam and writes two filterable GPU textures for the renderer.

Shade the displaced water

The vertex stage samples the displacement textures; the fragment stage reconstructs normals and combines sky reflection, sun glint, foam and backlit-crest scattering.

Frame cost

With three cascades, each steady-state frame records an evolve, horizontal IFFT, vertical IFFT and assemble pass per cascade: 12 compute dispatches in one compute submission. Spectrum initialisation is added only when its shaping parameters change.

02

Three overlapping scales, without duplicate waves

A single patch cannot cover long swells and tiny ripples with enough samples. Three 256 × 256 patches each own a different band of wave numbers.

Why split the field?

More detail where it matters

The large patch carries long swell. Smaller patches contribute progressively shorter waves. Each cascade receives a bounded frequency band, so the same wave is not counted twice; together the bands span roughly 250 m down to 5 cm.

Explore a cascade

250 m · broad motion

The largest patch captures long-period energy and sets the slow, wide movement visible near the horizon.

37 m · rolling structure

The middle patch fills the gap between distant swell and local wave detail without repeating the same frequency band.

7.3 m · crisp surface detail

The smallest patch provides high-frequency detail; the band partition reaches wavelengths of about five centimetres.

03

Evolve wave energy, then run the inverse FFT

The simulation stores frequency-domain fields and uses workgroup memory to transform them into height, displacement and derivatives.

Spectrum

Physical inputs shape the sea

JONSWAP models a fetch-limited wind sea; Phillips is an alternate spectrum. Wind speed, direction, fetch, directional spread and wave scale determine the initial amplitudes. Deep-water dispersion uses ω = √(g|k|).

The initial field is seeded with Gaussian noise. Its paired values for k and −k preserve Hermitian symmetry, so the inverse transform produces a real-valued surface.

h(k,t) = h₀(k) e^(−iωt)
       + conj(h₀(−k)) e^(iωt)
8Fields in four complex grids

Height, horizontal displacement, slopes and Jacobian derivatives are packed pairwise into four complex signals.

2DRows, then columns

Each cascade transforms 256 lines horizontally and 256 vertically for each of its packed grids.

GPUNo per-frame readback

The work is recorded through Three.js TSL compute nodes; the renderer samples storage textures produced on the GPU.

04

Choppy crests make foam; lighting sells the water

The inverse FFT produces more than height. Derivatives describe how the surface stretches, folds and catches light.

Surface assembly

Sharpen waves and detect folds

Horizontal displacement makes crests steeper. The assembly kernel computes a Jacobian from the horizontal derivatives; when it falls below a tunable threshold, foam is added and then exponentially faded.

Two rgba16float storage textures carry displacement + foam and slopes + Jacobian terms. Mipmaps and anisotropic filtering keep the signal stable as the camera looks toward the horizon.

NNormals from slopes

The fragment shader reconstructs the normal of the displaced surface, including the horizontal stretch of a choppy wave.

FSky reflection and sun

Schlick Fresnel blends an analytic sky reflection; GGX adds a sun glint that becomes rougher with distance.

SDepth and atmosphere

Backlit crests get a subsurface glow, foam is lit, and exponential fog blends the surface into the horizon.

05

Explore how wind changes the sea

The live panel exposes the spectrum, wind, foam, lighting and time controls. Presets give three starting conditions.

Sea-state presets

Calm · Breeze · Storm

The Breeze preset starts with a 9 m/s wind and 120 km fetch. Storm raises the wind to 21 m/s, increases fetch to 400 km and brings in heavier cloud and stronger foam. Calm starts at 3.5 m/s.

  • Waves: JONSWAP or Phillips spectrum, direction, fetch, scale, choppiness and spread.
  • Foam: formation threshold and decay speed.
  • Lighting: sun angle, overcast, exposure and fog.
  • Time: slow down, pause with Space or resume the simulation.

Implementation detail

Compute-capable WebGPU is required

This is not a WebGL fallback. The FFT relies on compute shaders and workgroup memory, so the app checks for WebGPU and shows a clear fallback screen when it is unavailable.

A camera-centred polar LOD mesh spans a radius of about 9 km with roughly 190,000 vertices. Its density stays approximately even in screen space, while the wave textures provide detail at each vertex.

Try it

Open the demo with ?preset=Storm, then change wind speed and direction. Spectrum-shaping changes re-bake the initial field; lighting and time controls do not need that step.
